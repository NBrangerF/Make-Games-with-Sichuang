# From Theme to Rule: Making “Prepare Before Use” Work

“Make a game about a lantern festival” gives us a setting, but it does not yet tell players what they can do. We can draw lanterns, stalls, and organizers. What must happen first, though, and who spends time on it? Relationships like these begin to turn a theme into rules that can run.

The original preparation model below retains just one relationship: a lantern must be checked before it can be lit. The checking conditions and four-action deadline are invented for this model.

## Choose a relationship worth representing

Two players, organizers A and B, share the task of preparing the left and right lanterns before opening. They have a common goal and do not score individually.

We want preparation to matter. Checking therefore leaves a visible marker, and that marker determines whether lighting is allowed. Checking is now more than work mentioned in the story: it changes what can happen next.

The deadline gives that work a cost. An action spent checking cannot also be used for something else. We have not yet decided how much one check covers. Start with a version that handles lanterns individually.

## Version one: check each lantern separately

Prepare two lantern cards, both unchecked and unlit. Place two check markers and two light markers beside them. All states are public, and there are no random events. Players may discuss their plans, and either player can work on either lantern.

Players act in the order A, B, A, B: at most four team actions. Each action is exactly one of these:

- **Check:** place a check marker on one unchecked lantern.
- **Light:** place a light marker on one checked but unlit lantern.
- **Wait:** change nothing, using up the action.

Check markers do not expire and are not consumed by lighting. There is no movement, payment, or extra action. An unchecked lantern cannot be lit. As soon as both lanterns are lit, the team succeeds and stops. If the fourth action ends without that result, the team fails. There are no individual rankings or ties.

Here is one complete sequence:

| Action | What the player does | State afterward |
|---|---|---|
| 1, A | Check the left lantern | Left checked; both unlit |
| 2, B | Light the left lantern | Left lit; right unchecked |
| 3, A | Check the right lantern | Left lit; right checked |
| 4, B | Light the right lantern | Both lit; success |

Each lantern needs one check and one lighting action. Finishing both therefore requires at least four actions. This sequence uses the entire budget. That is not because the rules demand this exact script. Someone must perform all four necessary pieces of work.

## Version two: check the whole batch

Change only the scope of checking. If any lantern remains unchecked, one check action now places a check marker on **every unchecked lantern**. Keep the other actions, markers, players, turn order, and four-action limit.

First, A checks both lanterns. Second, B lights the left one. Third, A lights the right one. Both are lit, so the team succeeds immediately; there is no fourth action.

Both versions represent preparation before use. The first accounts for each lantern's preparation separately. The second treats inspection as a single job covering the batch. The number of lanterns and the goal are unchanged, yet the required work falls from four actions to three.

A theme therefore leaves another decision for the designer: what is the target and scope of an action? The verb “check” does not specify whether it covers one lantern or a batch.

## How the relationship reaches the table

The players are organizers. They check and light lanterns under a four-action limit. One concrete condition connects these things: **a light marker can only be placed on a lantern that already has a check marker.** A reader can now carry out the rule and verify that it has been followed.

If the check marker affects neither lighting nor any other result, it imposes no action constraint in this model. Additional sentences about diligent organizers cannot give the marker a function that the rules omit.

Clear conditions do not guarantee interesting decisions, however. Version one has no faults, competing jobs, or changing information. Its four necessary tasks do not provide rich strategy. Version two is faster, but that does not automatically make it more enjoyable. Before adding another job, decide why you want players to hesitate. A setting having many objects is not sufficient reason to include them all.

## Check: must lighting immediately follow checking?

Return to version one. A checks the left lantern; B checks the right. A then lights the right lantern, and B lights the left. Does the team succeed?

**Yes, after the fourth action.** Each lighting action has the corresponding check marker available, and neither marker has expired. The rule specifies an order within each lantern's preparation. It does not require checking and lighting to be consecutive, or demand that the left lantern be completed first.

## Bring this step to your own theme

To represent a relationship, describe a change someone can verify: who acts on which object, what must already be true, what remains afterward, and how many opportunities the action uses. Then follow two legal sequences. Examine which relationship the rules preserve and which freedoms remain.

When showing this prototype to someone, observe whether they understand why an unchecked lantern cannot be lit. If they merely repeat an action sequence without understanding the marker's role, make that condition clearer in the next version. This model represents a preparation dependency; it does not model real inspection procedures, durations, or technical requirements.
