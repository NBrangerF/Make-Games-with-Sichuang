# Trading: can both players benefit when values are public?

The same resource can serve different purposes for different players. A trading opportunity need not depend on someone misunderstanding its price. Each player may simply lack the other half of a useful combination.

## One proposal and one crafting opportunity

This original two-player phase starts with A holding 2 red materials and B holding 2 blue materials. Both have 0 points. All holdings, recipes, and rules are public.

A may make one proposal specifying which currently held materials A gives B and which B gives A. Each side must give at least one material. Only existing red and blue materials may be included, within each player's holdings. B may accept or refuse, with no counteroffer. Acceptance transfers both sides simultaneously. Refusal, or no proposal, transfers nothing. There are no forced trades, future promises, coins, or later proposals.

Each player then has one optional crafting opportunity. A pays 1 red + 1 blue for 4 points; B pays 1 red + 1 blue for 3 points. Paid materials leave private holdings and cannot be reused. End after both crafting opportunities. Leftovers score nothing. Record each player's points separately; this phase has no additional condition that only the higher scorer succeeds.

## One exchange, different gains

Without trading, A lacks blue and B lacks red. Neither can craft, producing 0–0.

A offers 1 red for B's 1 blue. If B accepts, both hold one of each color. A crafts for 4 points and B for 3, using all materials.

| Sequence | A's final points | B's final points |
|---|---:|---:|
| No trade or refusal | 0 | 0 |
| Accept one red for one blue, then both craft | 4 | 3 |

The gains are unequal, but each player's phase points exceed their no-trade result. All numbers are known in advance. This establishes a mutually beneficial exchange opportunity in this position. It does not establish that players will accept or that trading automatically balances a whole game.

## Restrict only the eligible exchange to one color

Reset the inventories. Add only this eligibility condition: all materials given by both sides must be of the same color. The red-for-blue offer is now illegal. A initially holds only red and B only blue, so they have no common color to contribute. No trade is legal and crafting still yields 0–0.

Recipe values and personal needs stay fixed. Trading permission changes. Beyond asking how players negotiate, specify which assets may move, when offers are allowed, who may refuse, and when accepted terms take effect.

## Public value does not guarantee acceptance

The book's Trading discussion emphasizes relative values, difficult valuation, and room for negotiation. Our public recipes supply a boundary case: uncertain valuation is not necessary for a mutually beneficial exchange opportunity to exist.

With whole-game rankings, B might object to A gaining 4 while B gains only 3. With another round of resource use, the immediate 3 points might not justify the trade. Those extra objectives cannot be inferred from these two columns. This example also does not measure waiting, negotiating skill differences, or participants' experiences.

## Check: what if A already has a blue material?

Restore permission to exchange red for blue. Start A with 2 red and 1 blue; keep B at 2 blue and all other rules unchanged. Without trading, A can already craft for 4 points while B scores 0. After the same one-for-one exchange, A holds 1 red and 2 blue, while B holds one of each. Crafting gives A 4 and B 3.

**A's phase points do not increase; B gains 3.** A legal exchange, a strict gain for both players, and actual acceptance are three separate judgments.

## Sources and classification

Geoffrey Engelstein and Isaac Shalev, *Building Blocks of Tabletop Game Design*, second edition, ECO-02 “Trading,” printed pages 310–311, discusses exchanges between players, different valuations, tradable assets, and timing. The boundary analysis of whether value must be difficult to determine precisely, along with all recipes and traces, is this site's inference and original example.
