# Drafting: Whose Options Change When I Take This Card?

Two identical moon cards are on display. Why would one player take a moon while another prefers a sun? The same card need not have the same value to everyone. Taking it also changes what others can choose next. Drafting connects your own needs with a shrinking set of opportunities.

## Three players divide six cards

This original example has three players, A, B, and C, and six face-up picture cards: Sun 1, Sun 2, Moon 1, Moon 2, Star 1, and Star 2. Numbers distinguish copies without changing their value. Each player's scoring table is public:

| Who takes the card | Each sun | Each moon | Each star |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 3 points | 2 points | 0 points |
| B | 0 points | 3 points | 2 points |
| C | 1 point | 4 points | 0 points |

Players take turns in the order A, B, C, A, B, C. Each must take one card from the common pool, at no cost, and keep it face up. Removed cards are not replaced and cannot be taken by anyone else. There is no passing, trading, discarding, or separate purchasing phase. You may take both copies of a picture, scoring each separately.

After the sixth pick, each player has two cards and the pool is empty. The game ends immediately. Add each player's two card values. The highest total wins; players tied for the highest total share victory. There are no hidden cards or secret goals.

## Follow the shrinking opportunities

For this comparison, everyone uses one simple policy: take the remaining card worth the most points to you right now, choosing the lower-numbered copy when pictures match. Players are allowed to choose differently. This policy is not a game rule or a guarantee of the best long-term result.

| Pick | Card taken | Cards remaining: suns / moons / stars |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A takes Sun 1, worth 3. | 1 / 2 / 2 |
| 2 | B takes Moon 1, worth 3. | 1 / 1 / 2 |
| 3 | C takes Moon 2, worth 4. | 1 / 0 / 2 |
| 4 | A takes Sun 2, worth 3. | 0 / 0 / 2 |
| 5 | B takes Star 1, worth 2. | 0 / 0 / 1 |
| 6 | C must take Star 2, worth 0. | 0 / 0 / 0 |

A scores 6, B scores 5, and C scores 4. A wins. All six cards have moved to players' displays; none were copied or lost.

Look at pick 3. A moon is worth 4 to C and 3 to B. Once C removes the last moon, a star becomes B's best remaining option for the next pick. C's gain and B's lost opportunity are two consequences of the same selection. C need not have intended to block B for this to happen.

**Drafting** distributes cards or other game elements through a specified selection process. This example uses an open draft: the pool is visible, and each card taken removes one shared opportunity. Beyond simply acquiring a card, ask which options a player can select from and who will alter them before that player's next pick.

## Reverse only the second round

Restart with the same six cards, changing only the order to A, B, C, C, B, A, commonly called a snake order. Keep the scoring table and selection policy unchanged.

The first three picks are still Sun 1 for A, Moon 1 for B, and Moon 2 for C. Sun 2 and both stars remain. Now C takes Sun 2 fourth for 1 point; B takes Star 1 fifth for 2; A must take Star 2 last for 0.

The totals become A: 3, B: 5, C: 5. B and C share victory. A's first pick has not changed, but the second comes later: C has already removed the sun A previously received. C picked last among the three players initially and now receives consecutive picks.

Reversing the order pairs the selection positions differently; it does not automatically make the game fair. These players have different scoring needs, and we compared only one selection policy. This allocation cannot establish which order is generally better. Designers must examine relative card values and whether important combinations disappear before particular positions get access to them.

## Open pools, passed hands, and denial

A draft can also involve passing hands: players keep a selection from a packet they receive and pass the unchosen cards onward. They may decide simultaneously, but each usually faces only the options in the packet received. A card can disappear before reaching a player. This differs from everyone facing one public pool in both information and access.

A random deal without a choice does not demonstrate this drafting relationship. Deciding which card to use from a hand you already own is primarily a hand-use decision. Drafting allocates opportunities; rules must separately establish when acquired cards can be used and whether using them costs anything. In this example, players simply retain cards for scoring, with no later fee.

Sometimes a player takes a card they barely need because someone else needs it more. This is a behavior drafting can produce, rather than an additional rule players should always follow. It can sacrifice personal points and requires knowledge or a guess about others' needs. Larger pools and more scoring combinations can also increase the comparison work. When only one card remains, there is still a pick, but no choice of picture.

## Self-check: does denying a sun help C?

Return to A, B, C, A, B, C. Keep the first two picks, but let C take Sun 2 third. Afterward, everyone resumes taking the card currently worth the most to themselves. What happens?

Moon 2 and both stars remain. A takes Moon 2 fourth, B takes Star 1 fifth, and C takes Star 2 last. Totals: A gets 3 + 2 = 5; B gets 3 + 2 = 5; C gets 1 + 0 = 1. A and B share victory.

C has reduced A's original total by 1, but C's own total falls from 4 to 1. Removing something another player wants does not necessarily improve your result. In your design, observe which card a player expects to remain and who needs it before their next turn. If one card is consistently unwanted, examine when it helps different players and what taking it means forgoing, beyond its visual appeal.
