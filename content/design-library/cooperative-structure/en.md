# Cooperative structure: who may spend the materials if everyone wins together?

A shared objective determines who wins. Resource permissions determine what an action can use. Write them separately: enough timber across the table does not automatically authorize a combined payment.

## Two actions and two shared tasks

This original two-player diagnostic position gives A 1 timber and 1 cloth, and B 1 timber. Everything is public. The bridge and signal tasks are both incomplete. A takes one action, then B takes one action, and the phase ends immediately. There is no replenishment, trading, gifting, or extra action.

An action completes one unfinished task or does nothing. Repairing the bridge costs 2 timber; raising the signal costs 1 cloth. Under the base rule, a player may spend only their own materials and must pay the full cost at once. Spent materials leave the game and cannot be reused. Each task can be completed once.

After both actions, A and B win together if both tasks are complete; otherwise both lose. There are no individual points. Public discussion is allowed, but the active player makes the final decision on their action.

## Why enough materials across the table are insufficient

The table holds 2 timber and 1 cloth, exactly the combined task costs. However, neither player owns 2 timber, and joint payment is forbidden. Neither can repair the bridge.

A can spend their cloth to complete the signal. B can then only do nothing. The signal is complete but the bridge is not, so both lose. A doing nothing instead cannot give B a second timber.

This starting position deliberately has no winning route. It exposes the difference between sufficient total supply and a legal payment. It is not a balanced cooperative challenge.

## Change only payment permission

Reset the start. Change only this rule: the active player may use both players' holdings for payment. Materials stay publicly placed in front of their owners. Order, tasks, costs, discussion, and final decision authority stay unchanged.

A spends one timber from each player to repair the bridge, removing both. B then spends A's cloth to raise the signal. Both tasks are complete and both players win.

| Rule | Total materials | Is bridge repair legal? | Can both win in two actions? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spend only your own holdings | 2 timber, 1 cloth | No player can pay alone | No |
| Pay from both players' holdings | 2 timber, 1 cloth | Yes, combine the timber | Yes |

Permission causes the difference, without adding materials or reducing costs. A requirement for both owners to consent would need its own rule. This variant directly authorizes the active player to use these materials; it does not rely on an unstated approval procedure.

## Shared victory does not mean deciding every action jointly

Both versions have shared victory and both leave final action authority with the active player. Public information, shared resources, and unanimous approval can be designed separately.

A common objective also does not guarantee participation. One player may calculate and suggest everyone else's actions. To assess support for individual judgment, observe who proposes plans, who decides, and whether others understand the consequences. The cooperative label alone cannot answer that.

A cooperative challenge may come from constraints and tasks without a scripted enemy. This example has neither an enemy nor random events.

## Check: do private holdings always cause failure?

Restore spending only your own materials. Change only the starting distribution to A holding 2 timber and B holding 1 cloth. A repairs the bridge and B raises the signal, winning together in two actions. **Private holdings are not sufficient to cause failure.** Evaluate distribution, task costs, order, and deadline together.

## Sources and classification

Geoffrey Engelstein and Isaac Shalev, *Building Blocks of Tabletop Game Design*, second edition, STR-02 “Cooperative Games,” printed pages 4–7, discusses shared victory, different challenge sources, individual agency over resources and actions, and players making decisions for others. These tasks, inventories, failure diagnosis, and payment variant are original to this site.

[BGG: Cooperative Game](https://boardgamegeek.com/boardgamemechanic/2023/cooperative-game) is a classification identity reference. It does not imply that all cooperative games pool every material, prohibit private information, or require a vote on every move.
