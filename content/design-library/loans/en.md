# Loans: Why Can You Have Money Yet Still Be Unable to Repay?

Receiving four coins from a bank really does add four spendable coins. But if the rules also require returning five later, recording only the first transfer misses something. To design a loan, track available cash, later obligations, and the final score separately, then examine when they connect.

*Building Blocks of Tabletop Game Design*, second edition, Chapter 7, ECO-07 “Loans,” printed pp.322–323, discusses launching projects, recovering from cash shortages, and different treatments such as final penalties, recurring interest, or reduced income. Not every loan in the book requires principal repayment. Below we construct one particular game rule with a definite maturity payment. All coins and projects are fictional, and no commercial game's example is reproduced. [Publication information](https://doi.org/10.1201/9781003179184)

## State the loan terms before the actions

One player takes three rounds, starting with one coin and zero project points; the bank has twenty-nine coins. There are no hands or hidden outcomes, and all costs are public. At setup, choose either to borrow four coins once or not to borrow. This decision uses no round action, and no later borrowing is allowed. Borrowing takes four coins from the bank and places a debt marker requiring five coins at the end of round three.

The five consists of four principal plus a fixed fee of one. The fee does not grow with time; there is no recurring or compound interest. Early repayment, voluntarily paying less, and paying with points are unavailable. Each round, choose just one action:

| Action | Condition and cost | Immediate result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening project | Available only in round one; pay the bank five coins | Gain ten project points |
| Small project | Pay the bank three coins; repeatable in different rounds | Gain five project points |
| Work | No cost | Take three coins from the bank |
| Pass | No condition | Nothing changes |

An unaffordable action is illegal; do not take its reward first and pay later. After round three's action, settle debt automatically. With at least five coins, pay all five. With less, surrender all cash and record a three-point penalty for each coin short, then remove the debt marker. The shortfall has become a penalty and is not collected again in this example. Later money cannot cancel the penalty. No player is eliminated.

Then end the game. Final score equals project points plus remaining coins minus penalties. Higher-scoring plans are better, tied plans have equal results, and negative scores are allowed. Coins move only between player and bank, always totaling thirty. Debt and penalties are not additional coins.

## Borrow the same amount; leave different resources for round three

Both routes borrow four at setup, bringing cash from one to five and creating the same five-coin obligation. They take identical actions for two rounds, then diverge.

| Time | Route A: preserve repayment capacity | Route B: take another project reward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Round one | Pay five for the opening project: zero coins, ten project points | Same: zero coins, ten project points |
| Round two | Work for three: three coins, ten project points | Same: three coins, ten project points |
| Round three action | Work for three: six coins, ten project points | Pay three for a small project: zero coins, fifteen project points |
| Maturity | Repay five, leaving one coin and no penalty | No cash to repay; short five coins, giving a fifteen-point penalty |
| Final score | 10+1=11 | 15−15=0 |

A's cash account is 1+4−5+3+3−5=1, leaving twenty-nine coins in the bank. B ends with zero coins and the bank thirty. Both debt markers are removed, but B's recorded penalty still counts. Removing the marker does not erase the consequences of borrowing.

B's third-round small project is legal: three coins are available at that moment. The problem arrives in the later mandatory settlement. Treating an action's present legality as proof of a better final route omits the obligation that follows. The extra five project points cannot substitute for repayment; those points affect only the final score.

A is not simply converting four borrowed coins into four net points either. Borrowing makes the five-coin project affordable while it is available in round one; two later work actions restore cash and cover the one-coin fee. Another complete route is to avoid borrowing and work three times: 1+3+3+3=10 coins, hence ten points. That explicit alternative gives A's eleven a useful comparison.

## Move only maturity to the end of round two

Restart with the same principal, fee, three-round limit, and actions. Change only the settlement time to immediately after round two's action. Keep A's opening project→work→work sequence:

| Time | Cash and obligation |
| --- | --- |
| After borrowing and the round-one project | Pay all five coins, leaving zero; five remains due |
| After work in round two | Hold three coins; surrender all three at maturity, record six penalty points for the two-coin shortfall, and remove the debt |
| After work in round three | Receive and retain three new coins; they cannot be paid retroactively |
| Final score | Ten project points plus three coins minus six penalty points equals seven |

The same action sequence falls from eleven to seven. Earlier settlement also changes legal choices. B's former round-three small project cannot be repeated unchanged: mandatory collection has removed all cash at round two's end. Round three starts with zero, so paying three requires choosing a different legal route.

This does not establish that seven is the earlier-maturity version's maximum. Players can reconsider the whole sequence. Avoiding the loan and working three times still earns ten. The table establishes the effect on this project route and on its later options; a fixed route's result is not an upper bound on every strategy.

## What else the design needs to establish

Decide why borrowing exists: to keep a temporarily cash-poor player participating, or to enable a project opportunity that is about to disappear. Here the opening project lasts one round, giving early access to cash a specific value. If it remained available forever, or points could repay debt, these calculations would need revisiting.

Then separate ordinary fees from shortfall consequences. This example charges a fixed fee of only one coin but a severe three-point penalty per unpaid coin. Raising one is different from raising the other: one changes the cost of every borrowing route, while the other mainly affects routes unprepared for repayment. Whether the penalty discourages players from trying the mechanism requires playtesting; these tables cannot establish that response.

Borrowing, committing to a project, and receiving a reward are distinct actions. Here borrowing provides cash with an obligation, projects spend cash for points, and work receives cash. A loan can connect to a delayed return, but check whether the return enters the hand before the debt matures. The topic relates to income and production; an increase in cash alone does not establish an absence of later costs.

## Self-checks with answers

1. After receiving four coins, are four or five due? **Five: four principal plus a fixed fee of one, with no additional interest each round.**
2. Why can B legally do the third-round small project yet finish with zero points? **Three coins cover its action cost, but later debt cannot be paid. Fifteen penalty points cancel the fifteen project points.**
3. Can round-three earnings cancel the earlier-maturity version's penalty? **No. The debt was settled and closed under the shortfall rule in round two; its penalty remains.**
4. What happens after borrowing, completing the opening project, and passing twice? **Zero cash, ten project points, and a fifteen-point penalty for being five short give a final score of −5. Negative scores are allowed.**
