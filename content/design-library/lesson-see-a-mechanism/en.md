# See a mechanism: explain what this step changes

“It is a mail-delivery game” tells us the theme, but not how players act. This lesson uses a small Valley Post experiment to turn a story action into a rule that someone can execute and check.

## Resolve two picks

This is an original, independent local phase, not a new rule for the crossing course. Two parcel cards lie face up: an urgent parcel worth 2 points and an ordinary parcel worth 1. Lin and Qiao begin with no cards and 0 points each. All information is public. Lin acts first, then Qiao. Each must take one card still on the table, put it in front of themselves, and immediately gain its points. Taken cards are not replaced or exchanged and have no additional effects. The phase ends after both have picked once. We record these points without inferring the winner of a larger game.

In sequence A, Lin takes the urgent parcel, reaches 2 points, and leaves only the ordinary parcel. Qiao takes it for 1 point. The table is empty and the phase ends: Lin 2, Qiao 1.

Reset to the same starting state for sequence B. Lin takes the ordinary parcel for 1 point, leaving the urgent parcel. Qiao takes that for 2 points. The final totals are Lin 1, Qiao 2.

| Lin's first pick | Qiao's legal options afterwards | Final points, Lin / Qiao |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Urgent parcel | Ordinary parcel only | 2 / 1 |
| Ordinary parcel | Urgent parcel only | 1 / 2 |

Qiao “takes one card” in both sequences, but the available card differs. An action verb alone misses this change.

## Translate the story into a rule relationship

This is an open selection process: players take an option from a shared, visible supply in turn order, removing it from later players' options. It is one implementation of **drafting**. Other drafts might pass a hand of cards, hide some information, or resolve card effects later. None of those steps belongs to this example.

For this lesson, a mechanism is a repeatable relationship established by rules. Describe the acting player, their legal options, how those options change, and the resulting state together. This is a working description for the lesson, not a demand that every source define the term identically.

Cards are components. Mail delivery is the theme. “Lin takes the urgent parcel” is one decision. Removing a selected card from the shared options is the rule connecting the two players' choices here. That relationship would remain if the cards became plain slips marked 2 and 1.

## Change one sentence and trace the result

Keep the original cards and points. Change only the selection rule: cards now remain on the table. Each player chooses a card and records its points without taking it. Each still chooses once, with Lin first.

Lin chooses the urgent parcel for 2 points. Both options remain for Qiao, who also chooses the urgent parcel for 2. Both finish on 2 points and both cards remain on the table. This version still has choices and scoring, but selecting an option no longer depletes the shared supply. Two cards on a table do not make the selection processes equivalent.

The first comparison is that Qiao can now also choose the urgent parcel. Which version feels more tense or suits your players requires observation. A score table cannot report their feelings.

## Identifying a mechanism does not establish a good choice

In the original experiment, the urgent parcel offers 1 more point with no cost, private objective, or later use. To maximize this phase's points, Lin can simply take it. The example shows how the first player changes a later player's options; it does not give Lin a complicated tradeoff.

That creates a concrete design question. Should players compare immediate points, future uses, or opportunities left for others? Describe the difference you want to examine before adding rules. A third parcel might increase the number of options while merely adding another clearly inferior choice.

## Check: do identical cards still affect another player?

Return to taking cards without replacement. Change only the rewards so that both cards are worth 2 points and have no other differences. Lin's pick removes a physical card. Does it reduce the points Qiao can obtain?

No. Qiao can still take the remaining card for 2 points, so both finish on 2. The number of cards decreases without reducing Qiao's available reward in this example. “Removed an option” does not automatically mean “harmed the opponent.” Different uses would require additional rules and another calculation.

## Carry the question forward

You can read the sequences or replay them with two slips of paper. In a familiar game, choose one action and write down the legal options before and after it. If you cannot yet say what the next player may do, fill in the rules before searching for a label.

The next lesson keeps the question of who gets the urgent parcel and compares free selection with bidding. It resets the starting conditions; points from this lesson do not carry over.

## Sources and scope

Kathleen Mercury's [How I Teach Mechanics Now](https://www.kathleenmercury.com/how-i-teach-mechanics-now.html) connects prior game experience, discussion of mechanisms by function, and attempts to modify games. This lesson's sequence-first explanation is our original teaching arrangement. Its postal rules, values, and check are not her classroom materials.

The drafting concept draws on Geoffrey Engelstein and Isaac Shalev, *Building Blocks of Tabletop Game Design*, second edition, chapter 13, CAR-06, “Drafting,” particularly its discussion of removing options from a public selection. Our experiment uses only its explicitly stated rules, not the rules of the book's commercial-game examples.
