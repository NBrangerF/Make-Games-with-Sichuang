# Area Majority: Why Doesn't Being Here Guarantee Points?

Several players have markers in the same garden. Who scores from it? The answer can depend on how many each has, without anyone first driving the others away. This original endgame position has just three placements remaining. Follow them to see how one last marker can change the rewards.

## Two gardens, three final placements

Lin, Yu, and Qiao seek support in two gardens. North is worth 4 points; South is worth 3. Each marker represents 1 influence. Their current positions are below. Everyone's score is 0.

| Garden | Lin | Yu | Qiao |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| North, 4 points | 1 | 1 | 0 |
| South, 3 points | 1 | 0 | 1 |

Each player has one unplaced marker remaining. All markers and quantities are public. There are no secret cards or random events. In the order Lin, Yu, Qiao, each places their last marker in either garden. There is no capacity limit. Placed markers cannot move, and players cannot remove other people's markers. Everyone may share a garden, regardless of who currently leads.

After those three placements, the model ends. Check both gardens at that point. If exactly one player has the most markers in a garden, with at least one marker, that player receives its full value. If players tie for the most markers, everyone receives 0 from that garden. An empty garden also scores nothing. Add the two garden scores: the highest total wins, with tied leaders sharing the win. Scoring happens only once, at the end.

Lin places in North, changing its counts to **2, 1, 0**. Lin leads for now but has not scored. Yu also places in North, making it **2, 2, 0**, a tie for first. Qiao places in South, making that garden **1, 0, 2**.

North awards no points; South awards Qiao 3. The final scores are Lin 0, Yu 0, Qiao 3. Qiao wins. Having led earlier did not give Lin lasting ownership of North. Yu could enter without Lin's permission.

## Compare influence—and specify when it counts

**Area majority/influence** connects benefits within an area to the players' relative influence there. In this example, each marker contributes equally, and only the sole leader receives points. Other designs may reward second place or require a particular margin of superiority. Those arrangements need their own rules.

Having the most also need not mean holding more than half. Consider a separate counting example: three players have 3, 2, and 2 markers. Three is less than half of seven, but its owner is still the sole leader and would qualify under our rule.

“Control North to score 4” is therefore incomplete. How much influence establishes control? What happens on a tie? When is control checked? Each answer may change where players want to invest.

## Change one rule: tied leaders receive full points

Keep the starting counts, each player's last marker, placement order, and ending unchanged. Change only the tie rule: **players tied for the most markers each receive the garden's full value, provided they have at least one marker.** An empty garden still awards nothing.

Replay exactly the same placements: Lin North, Yu North, Qiao South. North still ends at 2, 2, 0; South still ends at 1, 0, 2. The counts are unchanged, but Lin and Yu now receive 4 points each from North. Qiao receives 3 from South. Lin and Yu share the win.

In the first version, Yu's equalizing move prevents Lin from scoring those 4 points without earning Yu any. In the second, the ending above awards both players 4. In positions where further investment is possible, the two rules would also change the reasons to pursue a sole lead. This does not prove one version is better. It shows that tie rules help shape the contest.

## Investing here means giving up something elsewhere

Each player has just one marker left. Placing it in North cannot also strengthen South. Investment therefore involves more than counting total pieces. Players must consider where a marker can change a ranking, what that change is worth, and whether someone can still respond.

This competition can happen without combat, yet players can still lose rewards they expected. Conversely, full rewards for tied leaders may make a tie satisfactory, reducing the reason to pursue first place alone. If someone can no longer improve their own score but can determine who else scores, observe whether that influence fits your intended game.

There is also a reading cost. More regions and scoring moments mean more counts to track. If markers contribute different amounts of influence, those values must be easy to add. Players should not mistakenly assume that having the most pieces always establishes the lead.

Do not confuse this with exclusive occupation. Our leader cannot stop others entering, and majority scoring itself specifies no movement routes. Nor does it require enclosing a boundary. Here, players compare placed markers, not the size of a space surrounded by lines.

## Check your understanding

Return to the first version. Lin and Yu still make the first two placements in North. If Qiao also places the last marker in North, what does everyone score?

The answer is **0, 0, 0**, and all three share the win. North is 2, 2, 1, with a tie for the lead. South remains 1, 0, 1, also tied. Qiao's placement in North is legal, but one marker does not make Qiao the leader—and it does not drive anyone else's markers away.

## Bring it back to your design

First observe whether players understand what a placement is seeking: a lead, a tie, or points that will be awarded later. Record where their expected score differs from the eventual result. Then investigate whether quantities are hard to read, tie rules are misunderstood, or the scoring moment is unclear. That distinction helps you decide whether the next version needs a clearer display, a better explanation, or a different reward structure.
