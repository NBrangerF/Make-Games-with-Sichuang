# Connect mechanisms: what happens after you acquire something?

“Drafting plus set scoring” is not yet an executable rule. When can a newly acquired item be used? Where do spent items go? How many sets may be submitted at once? This lesson specifies those connections through a Valley Post materials experiment.

## Set up this experiment

This is a new original local phase. Lin and Qiao have 0 points each. Lin already holds one red address card and Qiao one blue postage card. All cards are face up. The shared supply contains one more red and one more blue. A separate delivered area starts empty; cards placed there do not return this phase. Colors are categories defined for the experiment, not actual postal requirements.

Lin acts first, then Qiao. Each must take one card still in the shared supply and add it to their own cards. They may then submit one red and one blue to the delivered area for 3 points, or decline to submit. Each action permits at most one submission. Players cannot use someone else's cards. There are no trades, replacement cards, or other effects.

The phase ends only after Qiao's action, including the submission opportunity. Remaining cards score nothing; earned points remain. We record this phase separately from the previous postal lessons and the crossing course.

## Acquire first, then check for a submission

Lin takes the shared blue, now holds one red and one blue, and may submit them. Lin does so, scores 3, and has no cards left. Only the red remains in the supply.

Qiao takes that red, combining it with Qiao's blue. Qiao submits both for 3 points. The phase ends with both players on 3, no cards in their holdings or the supply, and two reds and two blues in the delivered area.

The supply became empty when Qiao took the card, but play must not end at that instant. Qiao still has a submission opportunity within that action. Treating an empty supply as an immediate end would incorrectly leave Lin on 3 and Qiao on 0.

## Make a different legal choice and follow both players

Reset to the initial state. This time Lin takes the red, holds two reds without a blue, and cannot submit. Lin remains on 0. The only remaining shared card is blue. Qiao takes it, holds two blues without a red, and also cannot submit.

Both finish on 0, each retaining two cards of one color. The supply and delivered area are empty. Drafting determines who acquires which materials; the submission rule determines how those materials become points. Their connection produces the two different outcomes.

This does not establish that acquiring more is always better. Each player gains exactly one new card in either sequence. The difference is whether it forms a usable set with their existing card.

## Move only the check, and the result changes

Keep the original cards and order. Change only the submission opportunity: it now happens before taking a card. A player first decides whether to submit one red and one blue, then takes a shared card. There is no check after acquisition. The one-submission limit and end after the final complete action remain.

Lin initially has only a red and cannot submit, then takes the blue. Qiao initially has only a blue and cannot submit, then takes the red. Both end holding a red and a blue, but each scores 0 because their submission opportunity has passed.

| Same picks: Lin takes blue, Qiao takes red | Submit after taking | Submit before taking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Points, Lin / Qiao | 3 / 3 | 0 / 0 |
| Lin's remaining cards | None | One red, one blue |
| Qiao's remaining cards | None | One red, one blue |
| Delivered area | Two reds, two blues | Empty |

The mechanisms may retain the same names while their connection changes. “Can the new card be used in this action's submission?” gives another designer something more checkable than “the combination feels awkward.”

## Check: having two sets may not permit two submissions

Return to submission after taking a card. Change only Lin's starting holdings to two reds and one blue. Qiao still has one blue; the supply still contains one red and one blue. Lin takes blue, then Qiao takes red. Both submit as much as permitted. What are their scores, and what does Lin retain?

After taking blue, Lin has two reds and two blues. The action permits only one submission, so Lin scores 3 and retains one red and one blue. Qiao submits the newly completed set for 3. The delivered area contains two reds and two blues; the supply is empty. Lin's remaining set does not automatically score when the phase ends. The end-scoring rule gives it no points.

## Write the connections into your prototype

Specify a complete action's order: acquire materials, check conditions, pay or submit, gain effects, and check for the end. Your game may use another order. What matters is making yours explicit. If an effect may trigger again, specify a limit or stopping condition. Players should not have to finish the design by guessing that they can repeat it.

Use paper pieces to replay one successful sequence and one that fails the conditions. A complete game still needs its broader flow and goals; local experiments do not replace whole-game testing.

## Sources and scope

Conceptual research draws on Geoffrey Engelstein and Isaac Shalev, *Building Blocks of Tabletop Game Design*, second edition, CAR-06, “Drafting,” and SET-01, “Set Valuation”: acquiring elements and defining a set's reward are distinct rules to specify. The submission order, once-per-action limit, and all values here are original experimental rules.

Kathleen Mercury's [Prototyping](https://www.kathleenmercury.com/prototyping.html) encourages early prototypes with enough material to test their main ideas. This lesson uses a few paper pieces for that purpose. It does not adopt her classroom timetable or claim that these calculations establish learning effectiveness.
