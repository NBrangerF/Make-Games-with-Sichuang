# Worker placement: occupy a space, change the available actions

You want players to compete for limited opportunities. What actually changes when someone places a piece on a space? Follow two placements before naming the relationship.

## Follow one complete local phase

This is an original local phase, not a complete game or an addition to the crossing course's rules. A and B each have one worker and no planks. A shared supply holds six planks. There are two spaces: the sawmill gives two planks and the forest gives one. A places first, then B. Each must place their worker on a space with remaining capacity and immediately take the indicated planks from the supply. Each space holds one worker during this phase. The phase ends after both have placed once. The supply covers every legal sequence; later retrieval and final victory are outside this example.

A occupies the sawmill, takes two planks, and leaves four in the supply. B cannot use that space, so B takes one plank at the forest. The supply now holds three. A has two planks and B has one.

If A instead chooses the forest, A takes one and B can take two at the sawmill. A's placement determines both A's gain and which space remains for B.

## Name the mechanism

**Worker placement** makes action selection visible through spaces. Players assign a limited supply of workers to actions under the spaces' rules. Where those workers remain then affects later opportunities. The useful relationship is between spaces, available workers, capacity, and effects, rather than the figures' appearance alone.

Our example uses alternating placement, immediate effects, and exclusive spaces. Other designs can admit more workers, charge later arrivals, or resolve rewards later. Knowing that a player placed a worker does not specify all the rules. Ask who may enter, when the effect happens, and when the worker returns.

## Change capacity and calculate again

Keep players, order, workers, rewards, and supply unchanged. Remove only the limit on workers per space. A again takes two planks at the sawmill. B can now also use the sawmill and take two. Both have two, leaving two in the supply.

| At the end of the phase | Exclusive spaces | Unlimited capacity |
|---|---|---|
| A's planks | 2 | 2 |
| B's planks | 1 | 2 |
| Shared supply | 3 | 2 |
| Can B also use the sawmill? | No | Yes |

The first change is to **legal options**. The different rewards follow from that restriction in the specified sequence. This does not establish that the exclusive version feels more tense or is more enjoyable. No players have been observed here.

## Blocking does not establish a tradeoff

In the original phase, A has no other goals or costs. If A only wants more planks, two beats one. The example shows an opportunity being removed, but it does not establish a difficult decision for the first player.

To examine a tradeoff, state the additional conditions. Consider a separate local phase: A has one worker, one plank, and zero points, and may take one action. A warehouse grants two planks. A landing instead exchanges A's existing plank for three points. Choosing the warehouse leaves A with three planks and zero points. Choosing the landing leaves A with zero planks and three points. The phase ends there; no later round is specified.

These are different outcomes, but we still cannot say which is preferable. We have not defined later uses for planks or whether scoring is imminent. A designer needs those uses and goals to create understandable tradeoffs. Giving actions different names is insufficient. This separate phase has no opponent, so it cannot establish an effect of blocking either.

## What does this ask of the design?

Spaces can display opportunities, while making turn order a condition that needs attention. If one space always offers more and later players repeatedly lack a useful alternative, examine rewards, turn order, and capacity together. Worker count alone may not explain the problem.

The number of workers also limits how many things a player can do in a round. Adding a worker calls for checking round length, congestion at important spaces, and the resources needed to pay rewards. If players actively retrieve workers, state whether retrieval itself costs an action. Returning a piece is not necessarily free housekeeping.

If later arrivals may pay to share a space, specify the recipient of the fee, any remaining capacity limit, what happens when someone cannot pay, and whether rewards still occur immediately. This changes the admission rule. Its consequences need not be a simple reduction in blocking.

## Two common confusions

**Worker-shaped pieces do not guarantee competition for spaces.** If a piece only tracks a personal quantity and its location does not affect available actions, its appearance alone does not justify applying this example.

**An action opportunity is different from spatial control.** A occupies the sawmill to select an action. This does not imply ownership of nearby land or prevent B from moving past it. Movement restrictions and area scoring require their own rules.

## Check your understanding: add a second slot

Return to the original phase with one worker each and six shared planks. Change only the sawmill's capacity to two workers; the forest still holds one. After A uses the sawmill, can B take two planks there?

**Yes.** A occupies only one slot. B can take the other, so both gain two planks and the supply retains two. Introducing a third player would require additional conditions about their worker, order, and supply. The answer does not silently extend to an unspecified third player.

## Bring the question to your design

Choose a space that often fills up. List the next player's legal actions before and after it is occupied. If nothing changes, check whether occupation has a functional role. If an important action disappears, observe whether the later player understands the alternatives and their uses. These are questions for a future test, not findings from one already conducted.
