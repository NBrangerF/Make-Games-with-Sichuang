# Trade now or deliver later? Follow a promise through settlement

“Your rope for my wood” names the goods, but not when they move. Materials that both players can hand over now might be used before a later delivery. To compare that change, first fix who accepts, what actions remain legal, and what happens when the goods are missing.

## One offer, two transfers

This is an original two-player local phase. A has one rope token, B has one wood token, and both start at zero points. Inventories, uses, and all rules are public. There are no random events or other sources of materials.

A may make one specific offer, “My one rope for your one wood,” or make no offer. B may accept or reject; there is no counteroffer. Acceptance is legal only if both players currently hold the required goods. Once B accepts, A immediately transfers the rope to B. In the base version, B immediately transfers the wood to A as well. No action can interrupt these two transfers.

B then has one processing opportunity: if B still has wood, B may move one wood to the used-material area for three points, or wait. Next, resolve any delivery scheduled to fall due here. Finally, A has one crafting opportunity: move one wood to the used-material area for five points, move one rope that A still owns there for one point, or pass. A chooses only one option and cannot choose an action without its required material.

The phase then ends. If B holds the rope, B scores four additional points. All other leftover materials score nothing. Record each player's total separately; there is no additional win condition or later round. Materials in the used-material area cannot be recovered, transferred, or scored again.

One delivery rule applies in both versions: **if the promised material is still held when delivery falls due, it must be transferred. If it is missing, record non-delivery, with no point penalty, refund, or postponement.** Accepting an offer does not reserve the goods. Until transfer occurs, a player may use materials they hold under the ordinary rules. The base version transfers immediately, leaving no intervening chance to use the wood, so its missing-goods rule does not come into play.

## Resolve the immediate exchange first

A offers and B accepts. A transfers the rope to B, and B transfers the wood to A. B has no wood left and must wait. A crafts with the wood for five points; B holds the rope at the end for four. The result is **A 5, B 4**.

If A makes no offer or B rejects it, neither material moves between players. B can process the wood for three points, and A can use the rope for one: **A 1, B 3**. Comparing these two paths, with both players taking the available scoring actions, the immediate exchange increases each player's own score. That does not predict acceptance by real players.

## Change only the time of B's delivery

Reset the starting position. Move B's wood delivery from immediately after acceptance to after B's processing opportunity and before A's crafting opportunity. A still hands over the rope immediately upon acceptance. Acceptance requirements, uses of materials, missing-goods rules, and scoring remain unchanged.

After acceptance, B now temporarily holds both rope and wood and has two legal choices. First follow one path for each, with A crafting whenever possible.

**Wait, then deliver:** B does not process the wood. B still has it when delivery falls due and must transfer it to A. A crafts for five points, and B scores four for the rope: again, 5/4.

**Process first:** B uses the wood for three points. No wood remains at delivery time, so the rules record non-delivery; the rope is not returned to A. A has neither wood nor rope and must pass, scoring zero. B adds four points for the rope, reaching seven: 0/7.

| Path from the fixed starting position | A holds before crafting | B holds at the end | Final A/B points |
|---|---|---|---:|
| No trade; each takes the scoring action | Rope | No materials | 1/3 |
| Immediate trade; A crafts | Wood | Rope | 5/4 |
| Later delivery; B waits; A crafts | Wood | Rope | 5/4 |
| Later delivery; B processes first | No materials | Rope | 0/7 |

Every path contains only the original rope and wood: each is either in a player's inventory or in the used-material area. Recording “non-delivery” does not bring processed wood back for a second use.

## Later delivery does not automatically make a promise optional

If B waits and still holds the wood, “Keep it and refuse to deliver” is not a legal option. Non-delivery here results from legally using the wood first, together with an explicit rule allowing a shortfall without a refund. **Delivery time, permission to use goods, enforcement, and the consequences of a shortfall are separate design decisions.** This comparison changes time and deliberately fixes the others.

To make delivery reliable, another comparison could reserve goods, forbid their earlier use, or specify compensation for a shortfall. Those would be further experiments, not quiet additions to this version. A point penalty might change the appeal of an action, but it does not create wood that has already been used.

“Immediate trades are always safe; promises are always risky” would also exceed the example. Its immediate exchange works because inventories are public, acceptance checks them, transfers happen at once, and no action can interrupt. Hidden goods, permitted misrepresentation, or interrupted settlement would each need their own rules.

## What the score difference tells us

Processing first gives B three more points than waiting, while A loses the five points available from receiving the wood. These are consequences of the stated local rules, not a judgment of anyone's honesty. The phase includes no repeated dealings, reputation rewards, or overall ranking, so it cannot settle choices under those additional conditions.

Nor can a designer simply write “All promises must be fulfilled,” leave it legal to use the promised goods beforehand, and omit what happens when they are gone. A test can follow the record: What was held at acceptance? When did each material leave its owner? Which intervening action remained legal? What exactly changed after non-delivery? Locate the missing decision before deciding whether promises need stronger enforcement.

## Check: B waits, but A does not craft

In the later-delivery version, B waits and hands over the wood, but A passes. What are the scores? Could B instead keep the wood and simply say, “I won't deliver”?

**Answer: A 0, B 4.** A keeps the wood, but leftover wood is worth nothing. B still scores four for the rope. B cannot refuse while holding the wood: having the goods when delivery falls due requires transfer. Both 0/4 here and 0/7 after processing give A zero points, but through different events. Final scores alone do not explain what happened.

## Sources and scope

Geoffrey Engelstein and Isaac Shalev, *Building Blocks of Tabletop Game Design*, second edition, ECO-02 “Trading” (printed pages 310–311) and ECO-18 “Negotiation” (348–349), distinguish exchanges of resources from agreements about actions and discuss scope, timing, and future inability to perform. The rules, inventories, scores, and comparison here are original to this site. The authors' descriptions of common practices are not treated as definitions that every game must follow.

ECO-15 “Contracts” (341–342) mainly discusses objectives that reward meeting requirements; sharing the word “contract” does not make those objectives equivalent to enforcing agreements between players. [BGG: Trading](https://boardgamegeek.com/boardgamemechanic/2008/trading) serves only as a classification identity here, not a delivery guarantee for this example.
