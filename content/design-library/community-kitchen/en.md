# Community kitchen: two bowls cooked, but one need remains unmet

A community-kitchen setting can make shared labor, limited workspaces, and different needs visible. This episode puts them into six actions: preparing ingredients, cooking a bowl, and distributing it each change a different state. Red and blue are fictional need labels, not dietary or nutritional judgments.

## Two people and three stages

A prepares; B cooks; both may distribute. The red need and the blue need each require one matching bowl and leave once completed. Start with 2 red ingredients and 1 blue ingredient. The preparation station holds one item, and the finished area at most two bowls; both start empty.

Play three rounds, A then B taking one action each, for six total. A may consume one ingredient to prepare its type on an empty station. B may cook that prepared item into a matching bowl, clearing the station; the finished area must have space. Either may distribute a finished bowl to an incomplete matching need, removing the bowl, or wait. Each option uses one action opportunity, without another cost.

Ingredients are spent at preparation. There is no reversal, type conversion, or automatic distribution. An already completed or differently labeled need cannot receive a bowl. Reselect an unavailable action without spending the opportunity. Inventories and needs are public and deterministic. After round three, completing both needs wins jointly; otherwise both fail. There is no individual production contest.

## Repeat red: output is not coverage

Round one has A prepare red and B cook red. Round two repeats both actions. The finished area now contains two red bowls; the station is empty, and the blue ingredient remains.

In round three, A distributes red, completing the red need, while B waits. The other red bowl cannot complete red twice or count as blue. **One need completed, failure**, despite two bowls having been cooked.

## Organize production around both needs

Reset and follow this complete sequence:

| Round | A's action | B's action | End-of-round state |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Prepare red | Cook red | Red finished; both needs incomplete |
| 2 | Prepare blue | Cook blue | Red and blue finished; both needs incomplete |
| 3 | Distribute red | Distribute blue | Both complete; finished area empty |

**Two needs completed, success.** Each person still takes three actions, with one red ingredient left. The difference comes from selecting another type in round two and scheduling distribution, without additional labor opportunities.

## Whose work changes which state?

A's preparation gives B something to cook. B creates something distributable. Distribution completes the need. High output from one stage does not establish that the whole process meets its goal. The single preparation space also prevents A from loading every ingredient into the process at once.

The roles cannot freely exchange all tasks. A design about flexible labor could let players learn or exchange work, then examine which constraint changes. Faster turns alone do not create a new skill.

## Check and model boundary

Red and blue have both been cooked by round two, but both players wait in round three. How many needs are complete?

**Zero.** Finished bowls exist, but distribution never happened. Handing them out automatically at the end would add a resolution step and change the deadline's effect.

Actual community services also involve participants' wishes, ingredient conditions, and less visible work. This model selects division of labor, stages, and matching needs; it supplies no recipe, allergy judgment, or measured service outcome. Observe whether readers track preparation, finished goods, and completed needs separately before expanding the situation.
