# Multi-Use Cards: Can a Display Used for Funding Still Be Built?

A card offering both money and a structure does not necessarily deliver both. If you must choose one use, turning the card into funding also gives up the opportunity to build it. Multi-use cards put that tradeoff on a single object. Choosing its use now and deciding whether it can return for another use later are separate rules.

In *Building Blocks of Tabletop Game Design*, second edition, Chapter 13, CAR-08 “Multi-Use Cards,” pp.552–555, Engelstein and Shalev discuss exclusive uses, single-use and recycled cards, and using a card itself as payment. The three-turn exhibition below is our original construction. It does not reproduce a book example or imply that every multi-use card must leave play permanently. [Publication information](https://doi.org/10.1201/9781003179184)

## Three cards, each with a choice of destination

This is a solo local game lasting three turns. Begin with one coin; the bank holds nine. Your entire hand consists of the three cards below. There is no deck, replenishment, or hidden information. The installed and discard areas are empty, and your score is zero. Coins carry between turns.

| Card in hand | Use for funding | Use for construction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lighting frame L | Take three coins from the bank and discard this card | Pay the bank two coins, install this card, and gain six points |
| Display T | Take two coins from the bank and discard this card | Pay the bank three coins, install this card, and gain seven points |
| Sign S | Take one coin from the bank and discard this card | Pay the bank one coin, install this card, and gain two points |

Each turn take exactly one action: fund or build with a card still in your hand, or pass. You cannot build without enough coins or borrow in advance. Funding pays immediately but uses the turn's action, so you cannot then build another card that turn. Installed and discarded cards never return and generate no further income. Passing leaves your hand unchanged.

After turn three, total your points and aim for the highest score. Leftover coins and cards score nothing. Plans with equal scores count as equal results. The bank always has enough for payments in this example; the ten coins move only between you and the bank. Printed points are game rewards, not coins.

## Similar funding leaves different structures behind

Compare two complete legal routes that both spend every coin.

| Turn | Route A: sacrifice the lighting frame | Coins / cumulative points afterward | Route B: sacrifice the display | Coins / cumulative points afterward |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Fund with L and discard it | 4 / 0 | Fund with T and discard it | 3 / 0 |
| 2 | Pay three to build T | 1 / 7 | Pay two to build L | 1 / 6 |
| 3 | Pay one to build S | 0 / 9 | Pay one to build S | 0 / 8 |

A ends with T and S installed and L discarded. B ends with L and S installed and T discarded. Both hands are empty, the bank holds all ten coins, and each of the three cards occupies exactly one location.

A is not automatically better because it raised more money. Its three coins support the later construction costs while preserving the seven-point display. B also raises the money its route needs but sends the display away. The coins received and the card surrendered belong in the same account.

L and T together promise thirteen points, but that printed sum is not an achievable result here. They cost five coins to build; sacrificing only S would provide just two coins including your starting money. Nine is the original version's maximum. Building two cards requires funding. The affordable pairs are T+S for nine and L+S for eight; a single structure is worth at most seven. Route A is not the only order that scores nine, but the ceiling remains the same.

## Change only the permission to use both options

Restart with the same cards, costs, rewards, starting funds, and three-turn limit. Replace only the either/or restriction: you may now take one action that first receives this card's funding coins and then pays its construction cost. Combined use is legal only if your current coins plus this card's funding amount cover its construction cost. Otherwise, take no coins and move no card; choose another legal action. With this new option, the card goes directly to the installed area instead of the discard area. No card is copied, and the original individual actions remain available.

Use the new option on L, then T, then S:

| Turn | Income and payment | Coins afterward | Cumulative points |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | L: 1+3−2 | 2 | 6 |
| 2 | T: 2+2−3 | 1 | 13 |
| 3 | S: 1+1−1 | 1 | 15 |

All three cards finish installed, with empty hand and discard areas. You have one coin and the bank nine. Six coins were received and six paid, leaving the original one coin, while the score rises from route A's nine to fifteen.

There is no fourth card. Instead, the same card can provide funding and still become a structure, removing the original sacrifice. This is not a recommendation to apply the variant to the original rules. It is a controlled way to examine whether a difficult decision comes from insufficient resources or from mutually exclusive uses. The demonstrated order can pay for all three cards, and fifteen is the sum of their printed rewards. What disappears is the cost of sacrificing a structure; payment thresholds and the three-action limit still apply.

## When the tradeoff helps

If you want players to have something useful to do without receiving every benefit together, a card unsuitable for construction can become funding. An alternative that is too weak may exist only on paper. Even with combined use, current cash and action order matter. If you first build S using the ordinary action, you have zero coins and cannot use both options of T next turn: 0+2 cannot pay three. Test particular hands, current funds, and remaining turns together.

On page 553 the authors discuss matching the strength of options on the same card and the hesitation that permanent sacrifice may produce. Those are their design considerations. Our nine/eight/fifteen-point comparison establishes only relationships among these three cards. It does not measure reader anxiety or demonstrate which version players prefer.

Multi-use cards relate to hand management but are not identical to it. Managing a hand can matter even when each card has one use; here the focus is competition between uses within one card. Nor is this deck building: the example has no deck and creates no new distribution through reshuffling. Several automatically executed icons do not automatically represent several uses that a player chooses between.

For a paper prototype, sending funding cards to a discard area and structures to an installed area makes the selected use visible. If later changes of use are allowed, specify their timing, cost, and treatment of earlier rewards. A discarded card should not quietly become a structure again in the calculation.

## Self-checks with answers

1. Can you start by building L? **No. You have one coin and need two. Funding and building are separate actions in the original version.**
2. Can route A retrieve L from the discard area and build it on turn three? **No. There is no retrieval action, and L has already been used.**
3. If every turn is spent funding, do the final seven coins score seven points? **No. Coins do not score; all three cards are discarded and the score is zero.**
4. Does the variant reach fifteen points by creating another card? **No. There are still three cards. The change removes exclusivity between uses within the same action.**
