# Deck Building: How Does One More Card Change the Next Hand?

You gain a card but cannot immediately do more. It goes to a discard pile and may return only after a shuffle. When it does appear, it occupies a hand position that another card could have taken. Understanding deck building means connecting acquisition, delay, drawing, and use.

## Change the pool you will use again

Deck building lets players change their own pool of cards during play, then repeatedly use the changing deck. The important feature is the ability to shape future draws and actions, rather than simply accumulating cards. Choosing an entire deck before play is a different design activity. Repeatedly shuffling an unchanged deck does not itself constitute building one.

Where gained cards go, when reshuffling happens, and how many cards are drawn all belong to this process. The same card entering your hand or your discard pile creates different delays.

## A transport phase lasting exactly three rounds

This is an original, single-player local example, separate from the crossing game. It records crates gained over three rounds, without defining a whole game’s winner.

Prepare one Heavy Haul card, which produces three crates when played, and three Short Haul cards, each producing one. Shuffle these four cards thoroughly into your personal deck, face down. Its composition is known; its order is hidden. Start with an empty hand, an empty discard pile, and no crates. Twelve crate counters form the supply. One additional Short Haul card is face up outside the deck.

Before drawing anything, make one modification choice: gain that candidate card for free, placing it in your discard pile, or pass and leave it outside. There are no further acquisitions, removals, or costs in this example.

Then play three rounds. Each round, draw two cards, play every card in your hand once, and collect its crates. Discard all those cards. If the deck cannot supply a draw, take its remaining cards first, then shuffle the discard pile to complete the draw. Cards cannot be kept between rounds; there is no hand limit or action-point cost for playing them. End after resolving and cleaning up round 3, retaining your total crates.

## What changes when you take the free card?

Suppose you gain the candidate, and this shuffle happens to order the original cards Heavy Haul, Short Haul, Short Haul, Short Haul. Round 1 draws the first two and produces four crates. The candidate remains in the discard pile and has not been used. Round 2 draws the remaining pair and produces two more: six in total.

Round 3 requires a shuffle. The discard pile now contains five cards: one Heavy Haul and four Short Hauls. If the next hand contains two Short Hauls, it produces two crates, finishing with eight. Passing on the candidate would still produce six crates over the first two rounds, but only the original four cards would be shuffled for round 3. The acquisition changes the next cycle.

Assume a random shuffle giving every two-card combination the same chance. Drawing two from four gives the unique Heavy Haul a 2/4, or 1/2, chance of appearing. Drawing two from five gives it a 2/5 chance. A hand containing Heavy Haul produces four crates; a hand without it produces two. The average round-3 yield is therefore three crates without the added card, and 2.8 with it.

An average describes repeated draws under those conditions, rather than promising fractional crates in one hand. The free Short Haul still has a function. Here, however, it enlarges the deck while being weaker than Heavy Haul and offering no other benefit. Changing its effect, the objective, or combination rules would require a new calculation.

## Change only the destination

Keep the decision to gain the same candidate, but change its destination from the discard pile to the hand. Everything else remains unchanged. It is now in hand before the first draw, and each round still draws two and plays every card held.

Using the same original deck order, round 1 plays three cards: the candidate Short Haul, Heavy Haul, and an original Short Haul. That produces five crates. Round 2 produces two, bringing the total to seven. Round 3 still draws two from the same five-card pool. If that draw again contains two Short Hauls, the final total is nine, one more than under the original rule.

Changing the destination allowed one earlier use. It did not increase the printed reward or improve the chance of drawing Heavy Haul in round 3. A separate limit of two cards played per round would invalidate the first-round calculation; that would be another rule to specify.

## What does this mechanism offer, and cost?

Deck building can let earlier acquisitions shape later actions, allowing players to develop how they play. Benefits may arrive late, however, and frequent shuffling takes handling time. Near the end, even a strong card may never return. A hand full of action cards may also exceed the number the player can use.

Deck building overlaps with engine building without being identical. The deck concerns what enters a cycle and how it is drawn; an engine can instead use components that stay on the table. A bag can preserve similar pool composition, but replacement, replenishment, and how drawn items are used still need checking. Changing the physical component does not guarantee identical operation.

## Check your understanding—with answers

**Can the candidate in the discard pile appear in round 2?** No. Two original cards remain in the deck, so that draw does not trigger a shuffle.

**Does gaining it into the hand improve the round-3 chance of Heavy Haul?** No: two cards are still drawn from five, giving 2/5. Its first opportunity for use changed.

In your own design, record how many actions pass between gaining a card and actually using it, and what choice it displaces then. Ask whether the player expected that delay. Rules can establish when a card may return; satisfaction needs separate observation.
