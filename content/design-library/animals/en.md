# Animal Themes: Who Acts, and Who Is Being Observed?

A picture of a fox and an otter does not mean the players are those animals. Players could be caretakers, observers, or storytellers. A different role changes which decisions belong to the player: where the animal goes, or where we look for it.

This site's animal theme entry includes those different perspectives without treating them as one mechanism. An original observation model will show the difference between seeing an animal and controlling it.

## Two animals, two rounds of records

Players A and B cooperate to record one fox and one otter. The map contains only North and South, with one animal in each area each round. To make the sequence traceable, the positions below are fixed and public. This is an invented event schedule, not a claim about real animal movements.

| Round | North | South |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Fox | Otter |
| 2 | Otter | Fox |

The record sheet has four boxes: Fox Round 1, Fox Round 2, Otter Round 1, and Otter Round 2. All start empty. The goal is to fill all four by the end of round two: both animals must be recorded in both periods.

In each round, A acts before B. Each has one opportunity to take a photograph. An unused opportunity is lost, not carried into the next round. To photograph, choose North or South and mark the box for that animal in that round. Photographing an already marked box adds no further box. Players may discuss their plans and choose the same area. There is no blocking, secret information, payment, or random draw.

The model does not charge travel time: players simply choose an area for each photograph. They cannot move animals. After both first-round opportunities, the animals switch to their second-round positions. After both second-round opportunities, check the sheet. Four filled boxes mean shared success; otherwise the team fails. There are no individual scores or ties.

## Sequence one: four photographs following the fox

In round one, both players photograph North. Both pictures show the fox, but they fill only Fox Round 1.

In round two, the fox is at South. Both players photograph South, filling Fox Round 2. The episode ends with four photographs but only **two filled boxes**. Both otter boxes remain empty, so the team fails.

No photography rule was broken. Repeated images simply did not complete the required coverage. In this model, more photographs of the same animal in one period do not create more distinct records.

## Sequence two: cover both areas each round

Keep the starting state, position schedule, and all rules unchanged. In round one, A photographs North and B photographs South, filling Fox Round 1 and Otter Round 1.

In round two, A again chooses North and B South. The animals have switched, so these photographs fill Otter Round 2 and Fox Round 2. Four photographs fill **all four boxes**, and the team succeeds.

| Sequence | Round 1 areas: A / B | Round 2 areas: A / B | Final records |
|---|---|---|---|
| Both follow the fox | North / North | South / South | Both fox boxes: 2 total |
| Cover both areas each round | North / South | North / South | Two boxes per animal: 4 total |

The second sequence uses no larger lens or additional opportunity. It distributes the same four actions across four different requirements.

## What role do animals play in the rules?

Here, players are observers. Animals are subjects whose positions change with the period. Players choose where to point the camera; they cannot order animals to move in front of it. One rule connects role, action, and pressure: **limited photographs must cover different animals in different rounds.**

If players were the animals, choosing an animal's destination might become a player action. If players were caretakers, the design would need to specify which actions affect which needs. Neither relationship follows automatically from an illustration. Choosing a perspective helps establish what players control and which circumstances they must respond to.

The recording goal is also a design choice. This model values coverage of two subjects in two periods, making duplicates unhelpful. If the goal instead rewarded every photograph, both sequences would produce four and the distinction would disappear. The first goal is not the only correct goal for an animal game.

## What does this model retain and omit?

Fox and otter are distinguished by labels, without different physical abilities, food requirements, or species behavior. The model represents observation and coverage, not an ecosystem. Two fictional people could replace the animals while preserving the counting relationship. Animal subject matter offers an understandable observation setting; it does not own this mechanism.

Public positions also leave little challenge in finding unknown animals. If discovery, identification, or approach should be central, define clues, information gathering, or distance separately. Merely hiding the position table would not establish a realistic observation model. Real identification errors, disturbance, and environmental changes are not represented here.

## Check: which record is missing?

In round one, A photographs North and B South. In round two, both photograph South. How many boxes are filled? Does the team succeed?

**Three boxes are filled, so the team fails.** Round one records both animals. In round two, South contains only the fox, and both photographs correspond to Fox Round 2. Otter Round 2 is missing. There are still four photographs; their total cannot substitute for the absent record.

## Bring it back to your animal game

First observe whether a reader describing an action distinguishes changing an animal's behavior from changing where they direct attention. Then examine why the reader thinks a record matters. If role and goal remain unclear, additional species names and pictures will not establish the relationship. When you choose to represent a particular animal difference, research that difference using reliable sources and identify which conditions the game retains.
