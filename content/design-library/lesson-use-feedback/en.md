# Use feedback: “add a book” is not yet a revision decision

Feedback can describe how someone experiences a sequence. A suggestion can supply a candidate change. Separate them so that “someone proposed this” does not become “this rule is already suitable.”

## Discuss the same version

This is this site's original *Book Cart* v1.0, separate from the main course's river-crossing example. A and B share one cart, with A acting first and turns alternating. The cart starts empty at Depot 0. The two-way route is Depot 0—Door 1—Reading Corner 2. Two books start at the depot; the cart holds at most two. All information is public.

There are at most six actions in total. Each turn chooses one legal action: load one book at the depot; move to an adjacent location; unload one book at the reading corner; or wait. Each uses one action. Movement does not automatically load or unload. Books can only be loaded at the depot and unloaded at the corner; delivered books cannot be collected again. An unavailable action must be reselected without spending an action. Discussion is allowed; the active player decides.

After each action, both players immediately win if both books have been unloaded at the corner. Otherwise, both lose after action six. There are no individual points, random events, or extra actions.

[Download the English print-and-play: rules, board, and complete variant comparison](/print-and-play/book-cart/en.pdf). The worked example below is complete without printing.

The base success trace is load, load, move, move, unload, unload, ending on action six. A takes odd-numbered actions and B even-numbered ones, three each. All feedback below is invented for teaching. No real playtest was conducted or recorded.

## One reaction can point to different problems

Suppose a demonstrator says, “Actions five and six are both unloading; it feels like repeatedly moving things.” They suggest adding a third book so there is more to do.

Preserve the identified moment, then separate the parts: the described reaction concerns repeated unloading; the suggestion increases delivery quantity. Required actions and feasibility before the deadline remain unanswered. “More to do” might mean more judgment rather than more physical transfers.

## Follow two candidates to their consequences

Candidate one changes only the number of books from two to three. Capacity stays two, the deadline six, and each loading and unloading action handles one book. Three books need at least three loads and three unloads. Capacity two requires at least two outward trips: two moves out, two back, and two out again, totaling six moves. **The minimum is twelve actions, making completion within six impossible.** Adding content does not automatically preserve the previous pressure.

Candidate two retains two books but unloads all cargo in one action. Load, load, move to 1, move to 2, unload all: immediate success on action five. This removes one repeated unload, but also removes B's former action six.

| Version | Minimum actions | Can succeed within original deadline? | A / B actions along the fastest route |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Base: two books, unload individually | 6 | Yes | 3 / 3 |
| Three books, otherwise unchanged | 12 | No | At most 3 / 3 before expiry; fastest route cannot finish |
| Two books, unload all at once | 5 | Yes | 3 / 2 |

“One less repeated action” and “more decisions for everyone” are different results. Candidate two may help examine repetitive handling, but does not establish that it meets the speaker's underlying need.

## Turn a revision into an examinable choice

If the next question concerns repeated unloading, provisionally choose candidate two while keeping other rules fixed. Provide the same opening and complete materials. Record actions after reaching the corner, who makes the final decision, and how participants describe that segment. When comparing versions, record their order and familiarity, rather than attributing all improvement on a second attempt to the variant.

If the actual problem is too little meaningful judgment, state that separately. Consider candidates creating different uses or route tradeoffs and recheck the complete rules. Finishing faster cannot substitute for that unresolved objective.

A designer can decline a specific suggestion while retaining the experiential clue behind it. Explain the choice through the intended problem and rule consequences rather than denying the person's reaction.

## Check: can the three-book deadline also become twelve?

Yes, that version can be made and run, but it changes both quantity and deadline. **Its differences cannot all be attributed to “one extra book.”** Record both changes and the question the new version addresses.

If unloading all succeeds on action five, can we write “everyone prefers the revision”? **No.** Five actions is a calculable result. Preference requires actual feedback, which is absent here.

## Sources and next steps

Kathleen Mercury's [Providing Feedback on Prototypes: the WINQ](https://www.kathleenmercury.com/providing-feedback-on-prototypes-the-winq.html) separates strengths, improvements, ideas, and questions, followed by designer reflection. Our feedback scenario, candidate comparison, and follow-up checks are original, without copying her form or classroom grading requirements.

The six methods can form a short investigation: observe events, clarify rules, choose a risk, examine participation, make suitable materials, and use evidence to decide what comes next. Each can be read independently and leaves its unanswered questions explicit.
