# An expedition: when does a teammate's return information matter?

A farther station may offer a more valuable record, while the observer may not know the return conditions. This original episode gives two teammates different information and checks whether a report can inform the decision to continue. It isolates one branching decision, not an actual outdoor route.

## Map, supplies, and who knows what

A and B share 4 supply tokens. They first travel together from Camp C to Station I, spending 1 and automatically obtaining a value-1 record. This mandatory opening leaves 3 supplies. From I, returning directly to C costs 1, while advancing to II costs 1. From II, only a predefined direct return line to C is available; returning to I is not allowed.

The direct return cost is a fixed 1, 2, or 3. Only B has seen its card: it is 3. Only A has seen II's record-value card: 3. Arrival at II automatically replaces the value-1 record with this value-3 record; values do not add. Both know who holds which information but cannot inspect the other's card.

At I, B may accurately report the return cost once for free or remain silent. A then chooses to return or advance once. Travel requires supplies already available, without borrowing. If the team reaches II but cannot afford its return line, the episode ends there without submission. Returning to C automatically submits the carried record. At least 1 submitted means shared success; otherwise failure. There is no replenishment, extra transport, randomness, or individual scoring.

Both card answers are disclosed here for analysis, rather than presenting readers with a blind test.

## No report: the better record stays in the field

B does not report. A advances for the value-3 record, spending 1 of the 3 supplies at I. At II the team has 2 supplies and the upgraded record. Returning costs 3, which cannot be paid. Nothing is submitted: **0, failure**.

An unaffordable return does not subtract supplies into a negative value. The team remains at II with 2. Nor can it improvise II—I—C: that return option is absent from these rules.

## Report first: bring back less, but actually submit it

B reports a return cost of 3. A can now compare the combined cost: advancing and returning require at least 1 + 3 = 4 supplies, against only 3 available. A returns directly from I for 1, retaining 2 and submitting the value-1 record: **success**.

| Sequence | Choice at I | Final supplies | Final location | Submitted value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Advance without report | Go to II | 2 | II | 0 |
| Return after report | Go to C | 2 | C | 1 |

Both finish with 2 supplies but different locations and completion states. Remaining resources alone do not establish the result. Reporting adds no supplies; it makes a constraint available to A's decision.

## How does a partial perspective enter the rules?

A knows the content's value; B knows the return cost; A retains final route authority. This preserves different knowledge among teammates while exposing another design question: why does only A decide? Shared decision-making would need its own discussion or confirmation procedure.

The specified sequence does not establish that every silent team advances or that a report necessarily changes human judgment. A concrete observation would check whether A adds both travel costs and recognizes why B's information matters.

## Check and model boundary

Change only II's return cost to 2. What happens if the team advances?

**Success becomes possible.** From 3 supplies at I, advancing leaves 2, exactly enough to return. The team reaches C with 0 supplies and submits value 3. Return cost causes the difference; farther is not automatically more dangerous.

Supplies, the one-way direct return, and automatic records are constructed for this model, not real travel or safety conditions.
