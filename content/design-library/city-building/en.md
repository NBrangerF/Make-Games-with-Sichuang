# City building: the same buildings, but who can use the services?

City building might give one player control over a whole plan or let residents express separate needs. Establish who decides about space and who experiences the result. This original five-space model gives one planner control of services without modeling residents' entire lives.

## Complete rules for a five-space street

Draw a line of five spaces numbered 1–5. One player has two houses H, one park P, and one market M. The street starts empty. Take four actions, each placing one unused building in any empty space. Buildings cannot subsequently move. There are no purchases, road construction, hidden information, or random draws. Both houses follow identical rules.

After placement four, end with one space empty. Score each house: 1 point for an immediately neighboring park on either side, plus 1 for an immediately neighboring market, at most 1 per service type. Other houses and empty spaces provide no service. Do not skip spaces or connect the line's ends. Parks and markets score nothing themselves. A service is not consumed: one building can serve houses on both sides.

Add the two house scores. Higher is better; equal totals have equal value. All buildings and rules are public. There is only one player, with no competition for turns.

## Place buildings, then examine their relationships

Each sequence starts empty and places the first four spaces below from left to right, using exactly four actions.

| Sequence | Spaces 1–5 | House results | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Park, two houses, market | P—H—H—M—empty | House 2: park 1; house 3: market 1 | 2 |
| Share the park on both sides | H—P—H—M—empty | House 1: park 1; house 3: park 1 + market 1 | 3 |

Each arrangement contains the same two houses and two facilities. Placement changes the result: the second park serves both houses, giving **3 rather than 2** points.

## Does 3 points mean all residents are more satisfied?

This model counts only adjacency between houses and two facility types. The first arrangement gives each house 1; the second gives them 1 and 2. A higher total says nothing about rent, quiet, work, or residents' feelings about fairness, because none is represented.

Even “service” is a selected interpretation. An immediate neighbor counts as usable, without queues or capacity limits. A design about congestion would need usage times, capacities, and rules for unmet demand. A larger market icon would not supply those rules.

## Connecting the city to the spatial mechanism

The role is a planner holding construction authority. Actions are irreversible placements; limited sites provide pressure. The crucial rule makes location determine which houses receive services. Mechanically, this is adjacency scoring. The city setting gives the relationship the meaning of housing and facilities.

The player is not an individual resident and has no action to move home, negotiate, or oppose construction. Adding these perspectives would require corresponding decision rights. Human-shaped pieces alone do not give residents a role in planning.

## Simplifications and the next observation

The street is one-dimensional. Services never fill up, walking has no obstacles, and buildings occupy identical areas. Points are not real urban evaluation criteria. The model checks whether a reader distinguishes “how much was built” from “who can use it”; it does not judge actual plans.

Ask readers to identify the neighboring pair responsible for each point, then name needs the model leaves out. That gives further complexity a purpose beyond adding more building types.

## Check: can a service skip an empty space?

Consider a house at 3, a park at 1, and a market at 5, with 2 and 4 empty. What does this house score?

**0.** Both services are two spaces away, not immediate neighbors. There is no rule allowing a two-step walk through empty spaces to count. A two-space service range would be a variant requiring new comparisons.
