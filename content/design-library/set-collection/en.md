# Set Collection: Why Can the Same Motif Suddenly Be Worth More?

Your display contains two Moons and one Wave, with one final piece to take. Choose another Moon or your first Leaf? The pieces alone cannot answer. You need to know how they will be valued together with what you already have.

Set collection concerns relationships that change the use or value of objects: matching types, completing a range of types, or forming a specified combination. “Score a set” is incomplete. Define which objects belong together, when scoring happens, and whether the objects remain afterward.

## One final choice, scored by matching type

This original solo display phase is independent of the crossing game. You have two Moon pieces and one Wave, not yet scored. The public supply contains exactly one Moon and one Leaf. Take exactly one for free and add it to the display. Leave the other in the supply, where it does not score. Then score once and end the phase. There is no later acquisition.

All pieces of each type form one group. Do not split a group or count a piece twice within it. Score each group using this table, then add the group scores. Scoring does not remove pieces. At most three of any type can occur here, so the table covers every possible count.

| Pieces of one type | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Group score | 0 | 1 | 3 | 6 |

Taking the Moon gives three Moons, one Wave, and no Leaves: `6 + 1 + 0 = 7`. Taking the Leaf gives two Moons, one Wave, and one Leaf: `3 + 1 + 1 = 5`. Both choices add one piece, but their contributions differ. A third Moon increases its group's value from three to six, adding three points. A first Leaf adds only one.

Find the added piece's contribution by subtracting the old value from the new value. The first Moon being worth one point does not make a third Moon worth just one additional point.

## Add only a three-type bonus

Reset to the original two Moons and one Wave, with the same supply. Keep the matching-type table and add one rule: if at least one Moon, Wave, and Leaf are present, add three points. Award this bonus only once. Pieces satisfying it still count toward their matching-type groups and remain in the display.

Resolve both choices again.

| Final choice | Display | Matching-type score | Three-type bonus | Total |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Moon | 3 Moons, 1 Wave, 0 Leaves | 7 | 0 | 7 |
| Leaf | 2 Moons, 1 Wave, 1 Leaf | 5 | 3 | 8 |

The first Leaf now adds its own point and completes the missing type for a three-point bonus: four points in total. The Moon still adds three. The candidates, existing pieces, and acquisition costs have not changed. A scoring relationship changed the final choice's comparison.

This does not establish which version is more enjoyable. If final points are the only objective and there are no other costs, Moon is better in the original phase and Leaf is better in the new one. Neither version leaves an unresolved tradeoff here. Future supply, opponents, or other uses would require additional stated conditions.

## State whether one piece can count in two places

The Moon group scoring three points while one of those Moons also satisfies the three-type condition is not automatically an error. This version expressly permits both layers of value. Problems arise when the rule leaves players to decide for themselves whether that overlap is allowed.

Now compare a separate scoring rule. To obtain the three-type bonus, first submit one Moon, one Wave, and one Leaf, removing them from the display for three points. Only the remaining pieces then receive matching-type scores. You may submit once or not at all. Keep the acquisition conditions unchanged.

After taking the Leaf, the display is still two Moons, one Wave, and one Leaf. Submitting leaves one Moon, for `3 + 1 = 4` points. Declining to submit scores the intact display for five. In this situation, declining is better. Taking the Moon still prevents submission because there is no Leaf, and scores seven.

The shared-piece version gave the Leaf branch eight points; requiring submission reduces that branch's best result to five. Receiving three points for submission is not a net gain of three: removing those three pieces drops the matching-type score from five to one, losing four points first. Calling a step a bonus does not automatically make it worthwhile.

## Scoring time can change the cost of waiting

Every score above is calculated once, after the final acquisition. Comparing values before and after a choice explains its contribution; it does not pay players once now and then pay the same display again at the end.

If a full game allows earlier voluntary submission, specify whether it uses an action, whether someone else can take the available reward, and whether pieces can be retained until the end. Different deadlines can change opportunities even with the same scoring table. This phase does not define those conditions. Seven beating five here is not a reason to always specialize in matching types.

The scoring table also creates handling work. Making a group's current value and the effect of one more piece visible can help explain the choice. Whether actual players understand and calculate it comfortably still requires observation.

## Check your understanding—with answers

**Under the shared-piece version, a different display contains one Moon, one Wave, and one Leaf. Add a second Moon. What is its final value?** Eight: three for the Moon group, one each for the other groups, and the three-type bonus once. The original display would be worth six under that same rule, so the new Moon increases its value by two. It does not trigger another bonus to make eleven.

**After taking the Leaf to reach two Moons, one Wave, and one Leaf, can the submission version score all the pieces for five and then submit three for another three points?** No. Its order is explicit: decide whether to submit, then score what remains. Submitting gives four; declining gives five. Eight belongs to the different rule that permits shared pieces.
