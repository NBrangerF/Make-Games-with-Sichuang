# Repair café: the same symptom, so can the previous method be copied?

An attempt may change an object, reveal information, and consume material that cannot be recovered. This develops the repair-café setting through two fictional demonstration boxes, comparing separate checks with copying the previous method. It contains no real devices, electrical work, or disassembly instructions.

## Two boxes and four opportunities

One volunteer faces boxes A and B, both displaying the same “stuck” symbol. Each has a fixed R or S fault. They may match or differ; there is no guarantee of one of each. The actual answers, disclosed for analysis but initially unknown to the operator, are A=R and B=S.

Start with 2 materials and four actions. Inspect an unrepaired box to reveal R or S without spending material; repeated inspection is allowed. Alternatively spend 1 material attempting method R or S on an unrepaired box, or wait. Each option takes one action.

A matching method repairs the box. A mismatch leaves it unrepaired, without refunding material. Failure publicly rules out the attempted method; with only two faults, the other can therefore be inferred. Trying without inspection is allowed, but requires material. Reselect an invalid action without spending the opportunity. Repaired boxes cannot be inspected or repaired again. There is no material recovery or random effect.

After action four, both repaired means success; otherwise failure. Leftovers do not score. Matching outward symbols are not a rule that the faults match.

## Copy the first box's method

Inspect A on action one, learning R. Use R on A on action two, repairing it with 1 material left. Because B looks the same, try R there on action three. It fails and consumes the last material. Wait on action four.

**Only A is repaired: failure.** That failed attempt already supports inferring S for B, but the information came at a cost. One action remains without any repair material.

## Obtain evidence for each choice

Reset and inspect each box before selecting its method:

| Action | Choice | Materials left | Repaired |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Inspect A: R | 2 | None |
| 2 | Use R on A | 1 | A |
| 3 | Inspect B: S | 1 | A |
| 4 | Use S on B | 0 | A, B |

The same four actions and two materials **repair both successfully**. Inspecting on three does not itself fix B; it directs the final material toward the matching method.

## Feedback informs, but also changes what remains possible

The volunteer chooses inspection and attempts under limited time and nonrefundable materials. Inspection changes knowledge. A successful attempt changes repair state. A failed attempt also informs but spends material. Calling all three merely gaining experience would hide these differences.

With deterministic answers, separate inspection works here. This does not establish a universal inspection schedule for real repairs or claim identical symptoms never share causes. The conclusion depends on independent possible faults, strictly matching methods, and limited materials in these rules.

## Check and model boundary

Replace the first sequence's final wait with an inspection of B. Does that succeed?

**No.** Inspection confirms S without restoring material or simultaneously repairing anything. Action three's failure already ruled out R, and another check creates no repair opportunity. More information is not more usable resources.

Binary faults, certain effects, and uniform materials deliberately narrow the diagnostic problem. They do not imply all real objects are repairable or should be repaired. Observe whether readers separately update inference, remaining materials, and remaining actions rather than only noting the correct method.
