# Push Your Luck: Should You Risk What You Already Have?

You have gained two rewards. Continuing might add another, or lose everything currently in hand. Stopping is not doing nothing: it turns a temporary gain into a result you can keep. The decision concerns how much to secure now and what to risk for more.

## Something to keep, and a decision to continue

Push Your Luck, also called Press Your Luck, lets players keep current gains or pursue more while accepting a failure consequence. Dice, cards, or a bag can generate the risk. What matters is an opportunity to stop and an understanding of which gains or opportunities continuing puts at risk.

Rolling to move does not automatically create this mechanism. If you must follow the result and cannot decide whether to try again, randomness is resolving the action. A reroll can implement the mechanism, but check whether earlier results can be kept and what an unsuccessful attempt costs. Seeing “roll again” is not enough to establish the same tradeoff.

## A complete local drawing phase

This original single-player phase is separate from the crossing game and does not define a whole game’s winner. Prepare six pieces of identical size and feel: four marked Gain and two marked Failure. Mix them thoroughly in an opaque bag. The player knows the composition but cannot look inside. Drawn pieces remain openly beside the bag and are not replaced.

Prepare six reward counters. Two start in a Temporary area, four in the supply, and none in a Secured area. This is the phase’s starting state; no earlier acquisition needs to be played out.

At each decision, choose exactly one option:

- **Stop:** move all temporary rewards to Secured and immediately end the phase.
- **Continue:** draw one piece. Gain transfers one reward from the supply to Temporary, followed by another stop-or-continue decision. Failure returns all temporary rewards to the supply and immediately ends the phase with zero secured.

There are no fees, extra actions, or redraws. An empty bag would automatically resolve as stopping if the phase had not already ended; with this composition, any Failure piece ends it earlier. Record only the number secured, without adding a win threshold.

## One success changes two numbers

At the start, stopping secures two. Alternatively, continue. Suppose the draw is Gain: temporary rewards rise from two to three, and the bag now contains three Gains and two Failures. Stopping now secures three. Continuing and drawing Failure returns all three rewards to the supply, securing zero.

That success changed both the reward and the composition. Failure initially had a 2/6 chance; after removing one Gain, it has a 2/5 chance. Meanwhile, the temporary rewards at stake increased from two to three. This is no longer the same decision.

None of this labels a player timid or greedy. Knowing the distribution of outcomes does not tell us what that person values in this situation.

## Calculate one more attempt

Limit the comparison to a specific plan: **draw once more, then stop immediately if successful.** Assume thorough mixing, with each remaining piece equally likely to be drawn.

At the start, each of four Gain pieces leaves you with three secured rewards, and each of two Failures leaves zero. The average is `4/6 × 3 + 2/6 × 0 = 2`, equal to stopping immediately. Equal averages do not mean equal outcomes: stopping guarantees two; continuing produces either three or zero.

After one success, the same plan averages `3/5 × 4 + 2/5 × 0 = 2.4`, below the three available by stopping. This compares the average secured amount for one extra draw followed by stopping. It does not establish the best strategy for every objective or subsequent plan, and it is not a measurement of human stopping behavior.

Replacing each drawn piece would prevent the failure chance increasing in this way. Concealing the initial composition would deny the player the same basis for calculation. Either would be an explicit rule change.

## Change only the loss on failure

Keep the same bag, information, drawing procedure, and stopping rule. Replace only the failure consequence: return one temporary reward to the supply, secure the rest, and end the phase. If fewer than one remain, reduce the amount to zero.

Follow the same Gain-then-Failure sequence. Temporary rewards still rise from two to three, and the second draw still ends the phase. Now two rewards are secured instead of zero. Failure did not become less likely; its loss changed.

From three temporary rewards, with three Gains and two Failures remaining, one more draw followed by stopping now averages `3/5 × 4 + 2/5 × 2 = 3.2`. This exceeds stopping at three. Changing the loss reverses this particular comparison. It does not show that the new version creates more tension, suits a particular audience, or necessarily persuades players to continue.

## Using it within a larger game

This decision can give a short phase a clear pause: what have I gained, and what would another attempt put at risk? If success never requires reconsideration, or stopping preserves nothing, check where the meaningful tradeoff actually lies.

Costs are concrete too. A large failure loss can erase a long accumulation; repeated attempts can leave others waiting. Partial protection, shorter phases, or relevant decisions for other players are possible comparisons, but each may change the original pressure. Changing them together prevents attributing the difference to one rule alone.

## Check your understanding—with answers

**After drawing a Gain, is the next failure chance still 2/6?** No. The piece was not replaced, so it is now 2/5.

**Does losing only one reward make failure less frequent?** No. That same state still has a 2/5 failure chance. The loss and the final secured amount changed.

In your own design, record what players could see when they stopped, how much was temporary, and what another attempt put at risk. Then ask why they stopped there. An early stop does not by itself establish that the rules felt frightening; the player might already have what they needed from the phase.
