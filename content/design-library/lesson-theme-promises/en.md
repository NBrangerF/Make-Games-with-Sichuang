# A Theme's Promise: Does Reaching Camp Mean You Are Done?

A tent may suggest rest, gathering, or keeping things safe. Those associations can make a setting understandable, but they cannot decide what the tent actually does in a game. A reader's prior knowledge may match only part of the rules.

This original sample-recording model separates three things: what the story invites someone to expect, what the rules specify, and which action a player chooses as a result.

## Establish which rules actually take effect

One player is a researcher standing outside camp, carrying two sample markers. Camp is one adjacent space away. The recorded-samples area is empty. All information is public. There are no other characters, random events, or sources of samples. Two actions remain.

Each action is one legal choice:

- **Return:** while outside camp, move into camp. Carried samples remain in the “In hand” area.
- **Record:** while at camp, move all carried samples to “Recorded,” scoring 1 point per sample.
- **Wait:** leave position and samples unchanged, using an action.

Each choice costs one action, with no additional payment. Stop after the second action. Unrecorded samples go back into the box and score nothing. There is no opponent or tie rule: this episode compares the final score from recorded samples. Each sample can be recorded only once. Placing it beside the tent illustration has no extra effect.

## Two interpretations, two sequences

Suppose the prominent instruction says “Bring the samples home,” while a large tent fills most of the display. Someone might interpret reaching camp as completing the task. The following sequence is constructed from that possible interpretation; it is not a playtest record.

First, the player returns. The researcher is at the tent, but both samples remain in hand. Second, the player waits. The episode ends, both unrecorded samples return to the box, and the score is **0**. Waiting was legal. The problem was an expected effect that the Return action does not provide.

Now consider a different display. Divide camp into “Samples brought back” and “Recorded,” and place this explanation beside Return: “Arriving at camp scores nothing. Record samples to score 1 point each.” Keep all the rules above.

Construct a second sequence. First, return: both samples are at camp but remain unrecorded. Second, record: move both into Recorded and finish with **2 points**.

The starting position, action budget, and scoring rules match. The second actions differ: Wait and Record. This comparison demonstrates a possible consequence of misreading a rule. It does not show that everyone will misunderstand the first display, or that the second has passed a reader test.

## Names help, but effects still need rules

“Return” helps us remember where the researcher moves. “Record” helps distinguish carried samples from completed records. The theme is useful here because words, locations, and actions can reinforce one another.

The problem is treating those reminders as additional rules. “Camp” does not automatically make samples permanent, and “home” does not automatically award points. Similarly, in another game, a hospital does not automatically grant free recovery, nor does a warehouse automatically permit unlimited storage. Each effect needs its own conditions and consequences.

There is no need to rename everything A, B, and C. Retain the helpful setting while making easily confused states visible. Let readers see the difference between being somewhere and having completed a task.

## Does the presentation need changing, or the rule?

If you want players to spend time organizing samples after returning, improve the names, spaces, and examples so that recording is easier to notice. This adds no action and changes no score.

If you actually want arrival to secure the results, you can change Return so that it automatically records samples. That changes the action cost: what previously required returning and recording now takes one action to score both samples. Check it as a separate version. Do not merely change a sentence while some players continue using the old effect.

Understanding and agreement are different, too. Someone may understand recording perfectly and still dislike spending an extra action on it. That is an evaluation of the design, not necessarily a reading problem.

## Check: does renaming the tent change the score?

The researcher has returned, still holds two samples, and has one action left. Rename the tent “Safe Camp,” leaving every rule unchanged. What does the player score by waiting? What about recording?

**Waiting still scores 0; recording scores 2.** The new heading moves no samples and does not alter their treatment at the end. If it suggests that waiting preserves them, revise the relationship between the heading and the effect. Do not silently invent another rule for it.

## Bring it back to your design

Show a reader the position after an action. Ask what becomes possible next and which results are already secured, then follow the rules through resolution together. Record the specific state where the expectation differs from the rule. That helps distinguish misleading words, overlooked information, and a rule that does not match what the reader wants.

This model does not simulate a real research organization's sample-handling procedures. It uses one traceable relationship to show how a theme can guide understanding while still requiring expectations to be checked against the rules.
