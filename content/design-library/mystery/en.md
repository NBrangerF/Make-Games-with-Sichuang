# Mystery: do two answers eliminate possibilities twice?

A mystery can let readers await a reveal or let players change their judgment through evidence. This original, crime-free episode about a misplaced festival key selects the latter. It compares answer count with information gained and presents the answer openly for analysis.

## Four drawers and two questions

One investigator seeks a single key, fixed in one of four drawers. It never moves. Their features are public:

| Drawer | Position | Material |
|---|---|---|
| A | Upper | Wood |
| B | Upper | Metal |
| C | Lower | Wood |
| D | Lower | Metal |

Initially all four are candidates. Ask exactly two questions, choosing each from: “Is it upper?” “Is it lower?” “Is it in wood?” “Is it in metal?” Repetition is allowed and still spends a question. There is no opening drawers, asking a name directly, or moving the key. No payment, dice, or other players are involved.

Answer yes or no truthfully according to the key's actual location. Answers are reliable. Retain every candidate compatible with the answers, without adding guesses. After two questions, a single remaining candidate identifies the location and succeeds. Multiple candidates mean it is undetermined and fail. Guessing a correct name does not replace elimination evidence; there are no ties or individual points.

For both worked sequences, the actual location is **C**. The feature table determines all answers, without exceptions introduced during play.

## Ask upper, then lower

“Is it upper?” receives no, eliminating A and B and leaving C and D. “Is it lower?” then receives yes, still leaving C and D.

Two answers eliminate the same group of locations. **Two candidates remain, undetermined.** The second response confirms consistency but, with reliable answers and complete mutually exclusive features, adds no distinction between C and D.

## Ask something that separates the remaining candidates

Reset. Ask upper again: no, leaving C and D. Then ask wood: yes. C is wood and D metal, so only C remains. Success.

| Sequence | After question one | After question two | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upper? Lower? | C, D | C, D | Undetermined |
| Upper? Wood? | C, D | C | C identified |

The number of questions is identical. A useful second question does not merely sound different; it produces different answers among the possibilities that still remain.

## How do mystery and deduction relate?

The investigator selects queries under a two-question limit. Reliable responses constrain the candidate set, letting the player demonstrate a reasoned path from four places to one. Other themes can use elimination, while mysteries can use branching narratives or character conversations. The theme and mechanism are not synonyms.

There is no culprit, liar, or concealing player here. Unreliable testimony would require rules about trust, contradictions, and success; each response could no longer be treated as a definite elimination. Additional story text alone does not necessarily deepen the evidence relationships.

## Simplifications and the next observation

There are only two binary feature divisions, with fixed, complete, correctly read answers. This checks redundancy between questions, not real investigation methods or judgments about people's honesty. Materials are labels, requiring no physical tests.

Ask readers to list remaining drawers after each question and explain why each remains. If they can repeat answers without explaining candidate changes, examine the connection between clues and rules. No human puzzle-solving data are claimed here.

## Check: wood yes, then metal no

Keep C as the actual location. Ask wood and receive yes, then metal and receive no. How many candidates remain?

**Two: A and C, still undetermined.** Both questions separate materials, neither separates upper from lower. Knowing C from this article does not make that question sequence uniquely support C.
