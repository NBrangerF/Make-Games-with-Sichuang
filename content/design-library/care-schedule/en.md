# A care schedule: when time shrinks, who agrees to take over?

An initially accepted plan may stop fitting after availability changes. Mutual support also does not let a system automatically move work to someone else. This original three-task episode separates sufficient total time, feasible allocation, and consent to a handoff.

## Tasks, time, and a fixed change

A and B arrange support tasks R1, R2, and R3, each costing its performer 1 time. Only A can do R1; only B can do R2; either can do R3. Initially each has 2 time. An accepted plan assigns R1 and R3 to A, and R2 to B. Eligibility and units are game conditions, not real abilities or care burdens.

Before execution, a fixed change reduces A's available time to 1; B retains 2. All information is public and deterministic. Keep the original plan or propose one task transfer. It changes assignment only if the recipient agrees. Refusal incurs no penalty and does not force acceptance. Transfer only to someone eligible for the task.

Finally check that all three tasks have accepted performers and nobody's workload exceeds current time. If all conditions pass, execute and complete the three tasks successfully. If any assignment is unaccepted or overloaded, record an invalid plan rather than claiming completion. There is no extra time, input of real experiences, or personal ranking.

The single-revision limit isolates a comparison; it does not prescribe stopping real negotiation after one refusal.

## Keep the plan: enough total time, still an invalid allocation

After the change, A retains R1 and R3, requiring 2 time with only 1 available. B's R2 requires 1 out of 2 available. Total time is 3 and the tasks require 3, yet their distribution makes **the plan invalid**.

B's spare unit does not automatically perform R3 for A. The rules require an explicit accepted transfer, not a completion claim based solely on totals.

## Propose explicitly and obtain acceptance

A proposes moving R3 to B, who agrees. A now performs R1 for 1 time; B performs R2 and R3 for 2. Both fit current availability and all tasks have consenting performers. The plan executes, completing all three: **success**.

| Plan | A's tasks / required time | B's tasks / required time | Available A / B | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Keep original | R1, R3 / 2 | R2 / 1 | 1 / 2 | A overloaded |
| B accepts R3 | R1 / 1 | R2, R3 / 2 | 1 / 2 | All conditions pass |

No time was added. An agreed responsibility changed; B's unused time was not treated as a resource anyone else could command.

## Available, eligible, and willing are different conditions

Each person has a time limit, eligibility, and a decision about taking over. Coordination checks allocation and consent alongside capacity. Unlike the fixed-support consultation article, members here can accept or decline a transfer.

Consent is represented by a clear outcome, without modeling discussion or reasons for refusal. The rules do not rank those reasons. Fairness or sustained sharing would need longer-term records, goals, and participants' perspectives; one balanced allocation cannot establish them.

## Check and model boundary

B refuses R3. May the system execute the second row anyway because B appears to have one spare time unit?

**No.** No transfer occurred. The original plan still requires 2 from A with only 1 available, so success cannot be recorded. Refusal is not an additional cost or permission to force an assignment. Another solution needs further negotiation or changed conditions outside this episode, not an invented acceptance.

Tasks and quantities are fictional, without assessing real households, health, or support networks. Observe whether readers mistake a proposal for acceptance and whether changed availability triggers a fresh check of the earlier plan.
