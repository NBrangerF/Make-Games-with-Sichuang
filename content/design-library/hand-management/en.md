# Hand Management: What Will Be Left After You Play This Card?

If two cards can complete the current job, does it matter which one you play? A specialist tool may be enough today, while a flexible tool can handle a different job later. Playing a card changes both your score and the choices you will have next round.

## Three rounds and three tool cards

This original solo example examines playing, keeping, and replenishing cards. All three jobs are visible from the start. Round 1 needs a blue tool and awards 2 points; round 2 needs a blue tool and awards 4; round 3 needs a red tool and awards 3. Each job is available only in its own round. You cannot complete it early or save it for later.

Your starting hand contains blue tool B1, which handles only blue jobs, and flexible tool W, which handles either red or blue. One more blue tool, B2, sits face up in a reserve. There are no other cards and no hidden information.

Each round, either play one suitable card from your hand to complete the job and score, or pass for no points. Played cards go to a discard area and never return during this game. W completes only the current job when played. There is no extra money cost. Cards cannot be discarded except by playing them; exchanging cards or playing directly from the reserve is not allowed.

The hand limit is two. Check for replenishment exactly once, at the end of round 1: if you hold fewer than two cards, take B2. Otherwise it stays in the reserve, with no later opportunity to take it. After round 3, add your points; unused cards score nothing. Aim for the highest total. Plans with equal totals have equal results.

## The same 2 points leave different options

First, use the specialist card:

| Time | Choice and points | Hand afterward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Round 1 | Play B1 for the blue job: 2 points. Take B2 afterward. | W, B2 |
| Round 2 | Play B2 for the blue job: 4 points. | W |
| Round 3 | Play W for the red job: 3 points. | Empty |

The total is 9, with all three cards discarded. Compare another legal sequence: play W in round 1 for 2 points, leaving B1 and B2 after replenishment; play B1 in round 2 for 4; then pass in round 3 because B2 cannot handle red. The total is 6. W and B1 are discarded, while B2 remains in hand.

The immediate reward for either first play is identical. The score difference appears in round 3: spending W also spends the only tool that can handle red. Keeping a card does not necessarily preserve an equally useful option. Holding B2 at the end still gives you no legal play for that job.

Here, **hand management** means arranging the use, retention, and replenishment of available cards to connect present actions with later ones. It concerns when a card becomes available, where it goes after use, and what the cards you keep can do.

## Move only the replenishment check

Restart with one change: move the single replenishment check from the end of round 1 to the end of round 2. Keep all cards, jobs, points, and the hand limit unchanged.

If you still play B1 for 2 points in round 1, you start round 2 holding only W. Completing that blue job for 4 points requires spending W. You then receive B2, which cannot handle the final red job. Total: 6. Passing in round 2 to keep W for red instead gives 2 + 0 + 3 = 5.

A better sequence is to pass in round 1, play B1 for 4 points in round 2, take B2, and play W for 3 in round 3. You finish with 7 points and B2 in hand. Forgoing the smaller immediate reward preserves the combination needed for the later jobs.

Seven is the maximum in this version. Scoring the red job requires keeping W. Before B2 arrives, B1 can complete only one of the two blue jobs, so choose the 4-point job. Using W for blue instead allows at most 6 points from the two blue jobs.

Both versions contain exactly three cards. Their availability changes the result. B2 is visibly on the table, but it cannot help before it enters your hand.

## Distinguish the related ideas

There is no shuffle or random draw here, yet there is hand management. Nor does play change the composition of a cycling deck, so this is not a demonstration of deck building. W's two uses make the cost of keeping it clearer. Cards with a single use can also create retention decisions when their uses and useful moments differ.

Acquiring more cards is different from using them well. Two blue cards are both unsuitable for red. A hand limit says how many cards you may keep, not which ones to keep. A design must also specify how many cards arrive, when the limit is checked, and what happens if it is exceeded.

This example reveals the future, allowing us to calculate a maximum. With hidden future jobs, players must choose using the information available at the time; hindsight alone cannot establish that an earlier decision was poor. Retention may also matter less if cards can always be exchanged freely for each other or immediately recovered after use.

## Self-check: does playing nothing let you save more?

In the original version, suppose you pass in round 1, then play B1 in round 2 and W in round 3. Where is B2 at the end, and what is your total?

B2 remains in the reserve. B1 and W fill your two-card hand when the single replenishment check occurs, so B2 cannot enter. The later jobs award 4 and 3 points, totaling 7. Both starting cards end in the discard area. Keeping every card can also mean missing a replenishment opportunity.

For your own design, examine one decision: what future use did the player surrender? Which job expires before the next card arrives? If someone reports having cards but being unable to act, inspect card functions, arrival times, and action conditions before deciding whether to change card quantities or the rules for moving them.
