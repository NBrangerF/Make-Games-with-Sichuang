# Things the Rulebook Never Wrote Still Happen

The river example showed how rules tell players what to do and when to stop. Even a complete set of rules, however, would not write every word Lin and Yu might say. One might give Rabbit a line of dialogue. The other might prefer to work quietly toward getting both animals across.

This optional reading moves to another table. An independent example will help us see what players can add while following the same rules.

## How Does a Cube Become “Cargo”?

Twelve wooden cubes lie on a table. Four players agree who starts, then take turns clockwise. Each takes one or two cubes and places them in front of themselves; if only one remains, they take one. Cubes cannot be traded or returned, and everyone's cubes stay visible. The game ends when the shared supply is empty. Whoever has the most cubes wins, with shared victory in a tie.

Imagine someone arranging their cubes in a line and announcing, “My dock is open.” Another points to the last cube in the shared supply and calls it “the final shipment.” Quantities and permitted moves stay the same, but the players now have another way to describe them. Taking the last cube might become “loading the final crate.”

Someone else has to take up that idea for it to become a shared joke. A player might instead remain quiet, calculating how many cubes to take next. Both ways of participating can fit the same rules. Making up a story is not a test of whether somebody is playing seriously.

## A Personal Description Does Not Automatically Become a Rule

The final shipment is still one cube. Naming it has not made it count as three. If somebody takes it and then says, “This is worth three points, so I win,” they change the conditions the others used for their earlier decisions.

The idea could become a variation for the next game. First, everyone agrees: the final cube taken is worth three points, every other cube is worth one, and taking rules stay unchanged. Compare total points at the end, with shared victory in a tie. The new score could make the last cube matter differently, so people need to know about it before choosing.

We can now see the difference between the two changes. A nickname adds another way of talking about a cube. A new value gives some choices different consequences. People can suggest ideas freely while explaining any part that changes the shared game. Others may choose not to adopt it.

## Playfulness Can Continue During Packing Up

After the game, everyone starts putting the cubes away. Imagine someone dropping each cube into the box with a little speech: “Work's done—back tomorrow.” Packing continues, but it has acquired a touch of performance. There are no new points and no need to choose another winner.

This attitude of adding play to what we are doing can be called playfulness. Packing originally meant returning materials to their places; giving a cube a line adds imagination to that same action. The tidying can still finish, without a new set of winning conditions being needed first.

Suppose everyone is ready to leave, however, and somebody keeps emptying the packed cubes out again, insisting the performance must continue. Other people's plans are now affected. “I'd like to keep playing” can begin a conversation; it does not decide how long everyone else must stay. An enjoyable idea can mean something different depending on what people need to do now.

## Objects can suggest an idea first

Cubes stand, form rows, and can be picked up, so they may suggest cargo, queues, or small buildings. Not every possible use is a permitted game action. Arranging personal cubes as a dock preserves their number. Stacking the shared supply into a tower that hides its quantity would obstruct choices based on public information.

A designer can leave room for personal arrangements that keep quantities visible while making the shared supply easy to identify. That provides conditions for expression without requiring everybody to enjoy the same material or invent the same story. What the material invites and which results count need attention together.

## Leave Some Things for Players to Fill In

Once rules are written, players still develop their own descriptions, habits, and ways of being together. Putting every spontaneous joke into the rulebook, then requiring the next group to perform it, may not recreate the pleasure. A joke may matter partly because those people invented it themselves.

A designer can provide a clear starting point: how objects are used, which results count, and what requires a shared decision. Then they can notice how players make the session their own. Inventing a name, discovering an approach to taking cubes, and making a small performance out of packing up can all be creative uses of what is already there.

Back at the river, Rabbit may receive a new nickname while a forward step still costs one plank. If the nickname is supposed to grant an extra step, that needs a separately explained rule. Leaving space for expression and leaving rules unclear are different choices.
