# How many ways can we add one small decision?

We want The 24-Space Race to retain anticipation around rolling while giving players a little more to decide. Adding a second die is an obvious answer. Before implementing it, however, compare several ways of placing the decision.

Write each of the three ideas on its own sheet. They are separate drafts to try. Choose a sheet before starting and play by the rules on it.

## Choose, reroll, or finish sooner

The first candidate is C. Roll two dice, see both results, choose one movement, and ignore the other die. The decision follows the random event, with the alternatives visible together. Its costs include another die, an instruction about selection, and time spent comparing.

The second is R, a separate experiment built from B. Each player starts with two reroll tokens. After rolling one die but before moving, they may spend a token to reroll once and must accept the replacement, even if worse. Declining preserves the original result. At most one reroll is allowed per turn; spent tokens leave the game and never refresh. This asks players to compare a known outcome with paying for another risk, while adding a resource and a timing rule.

A third candidate changes only the finish in B: reaching or passing 24 wins. It adds no strategic choice after rolling, but may address concern about repeated turns without movement near the finish. It belongs to an ending experiment and does not fulfil the goal of adding a decision.

## Compare candidates in the same position

Imagine a pawn at 6. In C, results of 2 and 6 reveal both destinations: 2 reaches 8 and climbs to 17; 6 stops at 12. In R, an initial 6 gives one known outcome. The replacement purchased with a token has not happened yet. Both candidates create a decision about movement, but the available information differs.

Rerolling does not guarantee improvement. From 6, a replacement 2 reaches 17, while a replacement 1 stops at 7. Giving up a known result to try again is part of the cost. Showing only fortunate rerolls would conceal the proposal’s risk.

The finish candidate needs another position. From 23, B leaves a pawn in place after a roll of 2, while the reach-or-pass experiment ends the game. The ladder position near 6 cannot reveal this candidate’s main change.

## Constraints let us omit unrelated work

Set attack cards, player roles, and the shop aside for now. We already have three ways to tackle one small change. Each extra system would need time to explain who can use it, when, and what happens afterwards.

We will make choice version C first. Two dice put the results side by side, and fit the materials in our brief. Keep base version B for comparison and bring reroll version R back when we discuss resources.

Do not casually put R’s tokens into C. That creates unanswered questions: reroll one die or both? Select before rerolling, or select afterwards? Until those questions are resolved, the combination is not a complete version.

## Preserve the candidates you set aside

Keep a short record for each proposal: its starting version, its one change, the intended problem, the added cost, and a fixed position that exposes the difference. When R returns, this record explains that it concerns limited retries rather than being a default addition to C.

Keep the ideas you set aside. Label the finish change “reduce turns stuck near the end” and the reroll “spend a limited chance to try again.” When either problem comes up, you will know which sheet to return to.
