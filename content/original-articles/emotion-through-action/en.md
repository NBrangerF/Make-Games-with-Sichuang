# After You Offer Something, What Can Your Friend Choose?

Hedgehog staying to collect showed us a step where helping can happen. Another question follows: when you offer an opportunity to a friend, does that friend have room to respond? This time we use an independent lamp-repair example, without the river’s shared-plank rules.

## Which lamp can the final part repair?

Imagine three people repairing lamps together, one lamp each. One is already lit. Yours and the lamp belonging to the friend opposite you are still dark, each needing one part. It is the final turn, you are the active player, and you hold the only remaining part. No more parts or turns will become available.

You can repair your lamp or offer the part to your friend. If your friend accepts, their lamp is repaired immediately within your action, without another action being spent. If they decline, you keep the part and may repair your own lamp.

Each working lamp earns the team one point. There are no individual scores or bonuses for helping. Either repair leaves two of the three lamps lit and scores two points. Equal scores may still lead to different conversations.

## Two points do not give both people the same thought

Imagine offering the part and hearing, “What about your lamp?” You might want your friend to finish the job they have been responsible for. Your friend might think that, since you obtained the part, you should finish yours. Both see the same point available, without necessarily interpreting the opportunity alike.

Perhaps you do not care which lamp is lit and offer the part casually, while your friend sees a thoughtful gesture. Or everyone may be ready to pack up and give it little attention. A transfer can carry these different meanings. The score sheet does not settle them.

An offer can acquire a helping meaning here because your choice changes what your friend can finish. The lamp will not light itself; you could keep the part, and your friend can accept it. Displaying a message about helping after both lamps light automatically would not provide the same relationship between choices.

## Declining still leaves a next step

In this fragment, your friend can say, “No, you use it,” without costing the team a working lamp. You can still repair your own and preserve the two-point outcome. Refusal has a clear path onward.

Without that rule, questions might arise. Does making the offer use the whole action? Is a declined part wasted? If the recipient fears that saying no will cost everyone a point, the conditions of the decision have changed. Saying that refusal is allowed is not enough; the rules must explain where the part goes and how the action can finish.

That cannot guarantee freedom from pressure. Imagine adding, “But I offered it to you—why won’t you take it?” Even with refusal permitted, your friend might not want to disappoint you. The game can preserve a choice while the group’s actual relationships still shape the moment.

## Disagreeing over a move need not judge a person

Suppose your friend declines by saying, “My repairer wants someone else to have the light.” This might be character dialogue, or the person might simply want you to keep the opportunity. Continuing the action requires knowing whether the offer is accepted, without first judging whether your friend is grateful enough.

If somebody really is criticized for refusing, calling it character dialogue cannot establish that the person is untroubled. What a character does, what a player chooses, and how friends treat one another can overlap in one sentence. Clear consequences for accepting and declining let the action continue without demanding proof of goodwill.

## Thanks is not a condition for completing the action

To make the offer clear, you can place the part where both people can see it and say which lamp you are offering to repair. A nod or a spoken response can let the action continue. Completing it need not require a hug, eye contact, or public thanks.

If your friend accepts and repairs the lamp without thanking you, we do not know whether they are concentrating on the rules, treating it as ordinary cooperation, or thinking something else. A spoken thank-you would not prove that the arrangement felt comfortable either.

The concern here is how to describe a complete handover. You retain the choice to use the part yourself. Your friend can decide whether to accept, and refusal leaves a clear use for the part. We can count the working lamps from the rules. What the offer means between two people needs room for their own understanding and response.
