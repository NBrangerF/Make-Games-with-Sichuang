# How Do Goals and Endings Hold a Game Together?

The previous chapter followed where planks come from and go while keeping the goal of crossing together within four rounds. Now consider the ending rule. It may look like a final instruction, yet it has been shaping the value of preparation all along.

In the successful basic sequence, Rabbit arrives in round three and Hedgehog in round four. Rabbit’s arrival does not stop the game; Hedgehog’s arrival does. The desired result is “both animals on the far bank.” That result is checked after each action, and the last deadline is the end of round four. Together these rules give the game a complete ending.

## The Stopping Point Must Fit the Intended Result

Suppose a designer wants a quicker ending and changes only the stopping rule to “end when the first animal arrives,” while still requiring both to arrive to win. What happens? The animals act in sequence. As soon as one arrives, the game stops before the other arrives. There is no action that brings them across simultaneously, so this change makes the shared goal impossible.

Changing the goal as well to “win together when at least one animal arrives” makes the rules work, but changes the problem. Lin can move Rabbit for three consecutive rounds, spending three of the starting four planks. Yu can wait throughout and they still succeed. Preparing enough material to get both animals across is no longer necessary.

That could be a different direction for a game. If we still want people to discuss how to bring their friend across too, however, we need to notice what the change removed. A faster ending is one consequence; the reasons for taking actions change as well.

## Which Result Does a Score Reward?

Return to two animals and a four-round deadline for another independent comparison. At the end of round four, award the team one point per animal on the far bank, with the goal of maximizing the team’s score. If both arrive early, they have reached the maximum of two points and may finish immediately. There are no personal scores or individual first place.

One animal across and one short now earns one point, while neither across earns zero. The basic game treats both outcomes as failure; the new scoring gives them different values. When getting both across is no longer possible, getting one across may still improve the result.

What earns a point matters more than the word “score.” Awarding one point per unspent plank would give keeping planks a benefit that could pull against spending them to cross. How many points, when to count them, and whether arrival is still required would all need explicit rules and comparison. “Add points” does not finish designing a goal.

## Replace Four Rounds with Time That Actions Spend

Put scoring aside and return to the shared goal of getting both animals across. Change only how time is measured. Remove the four-round limit and place nine time markers on the table instead. Lin and Yu still alternate turns. Moving costs one time marker, waiting also costs one, and collecting costs two. All plank rules remain unchanged. An action that costs more time than remains cannot be chosen.

For each action, first pay its time and plank costs, then complete the move or collection. Next, check whether both animals have arrived: if so, they win together. Otherwise, if no time remains, they lose. If time remains, the next player acts. There is no way to add time. An animal already across must still wait and spend one time marker. Every action uses at least one, so waiting forever cannot prevent the ending.

Does the original successful plan still work? In round one, Rabbit moves and Hedgehog collects, spending three time markers and leaving six. Both move in round two, leaving four. Both move in round three, leaving two, with Rabbit now across. In round four, Rabbit waits, leaving one. Hedgehog moves and spends the last marker. Both are across, so the success check ends the game in a win. The original sequence also uses the last plank.

If Hedgehog collects again in round two, only three time markers remain at that round’s end. Both animals move in round three, leaving one, with Hedgehog only on the first stone. Rabbit’s next wait uses the last time marker and ends the game in failure before Hedgehog gets another turn. Collecting again has used Yu’s turn and spent one more unit of shared time than waiting or moving would have.

With four fixed rounds, each player is guaranteed four turns unless they succeed earlier. Under the new arrangement, different actions advance the clock at different rates, and the game may end midway through a round. “Markers remaining” therefore cannot be read as “turns remaining for each player.” Nor does this clock guarantee a particular number of real minutes: discussions can still take different amounts of time.

## What Is Still Worth Deciding Near the End?

Return once more to the basic game. At the start, the players arrange who stays on the bank. Midway through, animals that have left cannot add supplies. Near the end, if enough planks and moves remain, both may simply follow their plan. Different stages naturally leave different decisions available.

Rabbit waiting across the river for Hedgehog’s last step can show the completion of their shared success. Requiring two extra rounds after both arrive, without a new goal or action, would leave them merely counting rounds. Checking for the ending immediately after the second arrival connects the completing action to the close of the game.

We can now see how sources, action costs, and stopping points change a plan together. The next chapter puts two rules into the same scene and follows how they help each other—and how they might remove a cost that originally mattered.
