# Can We Affect Each Other Without Attacking?

Two people run separate warehouses, and neither has an attack card. But one berth remains at the harbour. If you use it, the other person cannot. “Building your own operation” sounds independent; one shared space is enough to bring the plans into conflict.

## The last available berth

Consider an independent harbor example, unrelated to the animal rules. Lin has eight points and Rui has six. Lin acts first, then Rui. Each has exactly one action left, after which the higher score wins.

Either player can organize their own warehouse for two points; each warehouse remains available. There is also one shared berth. Once one player uses it, the other cannot. Their orders are public: using the berth gives Lin two points or Rui five. There are no other actions, hidden scores, or random changes.

If Lin chooses the warehouse, Lin goes from eight to ten. Rui can then take the berth and rise from six to eleven. If Lin chooses the berth instead, Lin still reaches ten, but Rui has only the warehouse left and finishes at eight.

Both actions add two points to Lin’s score. The difference is the opportunity left to Rui. Nobody removes an opponent’s piece or takes away points already earned. Yet a single space can change who wins.

## Leaving your pieces alone can still change your plan

An effect that works through a shared opportunity can be called indirect interaction. The term describes how the effect happens; it does not establish that the effect is small. Rui still holds the five-point order but has nowhere to complete it. That lost opportunity matters.

Taking the berth is not automatically poor design either. If both players want to anticipate each other’s plans, this may be exactly what they came for. If the introduction promises quiet development and suggests that personal plans will remain undisturbed, the actual play may surprise them.

An introduction can therefore add something more precise than “There are no attacks”: “You cannot destroy what somebody has already built, but you can take an action space they need.” The listener can imagine a move and its consequence without learning a whole vocabulary first.

## A genre name needs another sentence

You may have heard the name Eurogame. Encountering it for the first time does not require memorizing a long list of features. It can work as a conversational shortcut, giving people familiar with some games a point of reference. It does not give every game under the name identical rules.

Calling our example a Eurogame about business would not tell us the berth’s capacity, whether the orders are public, or who acts first. Those conditions give Lin’s two-point move its particular effect. Even if two games share that description, a berth open to everybody and a berth with only one remaining space would create different opportunities for blocking.

Two-player tells us a player count. Business suggests a subject. Taking shared spaces describes a specific relationship. These descriptions can work together, but cannot replace one another. A genre becomes more helpful when it leads to the next question.

## How does this group want to play?

The same final position can belong to different gatherings. Suppose the players agree before starting that this is a practice game and reminders about overlooked opportunities are welcome before a move. Lin can explain both outcomes to Rui, after which each still makes their own decision. The scores need not change, and nobody has to lose deliberately.

If they agree on a serious contest without strategic advice, Lin can keep that judgment private. Being new does not mean Rui necessarily wants coaching. Being quiet does not establish that Rui has stopped watching the berth.

Offering a hint in a practice game differs from imposing an answer. Suppose Lin asks whether Rui would like to hear the two final scores. Rui might prefer to calculate first. Permitting communication opens a route without requiring everyone to accept all available advice.

Conversely, taking the berth legally cannot establish that this group will welcome the move. If their usual gathering centers on casually showing their warehouses, adding competition for spaces may change their expectations of one another. A designer needs to explain the change and learn whether they want it. A genre name cannot answer on their behalf.

A clearer introduction might therefore be: “You compete for shared spaces. For this first practice game, we can point out opportunities to each other.” It describes both something the game permits and how this group will approach it. The genre name remains useful without making every promise on the players’ behalf.
