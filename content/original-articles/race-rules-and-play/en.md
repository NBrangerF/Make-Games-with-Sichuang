# Almost at the finish—then back down a snake

You are a few spaces from the finish. Then a roll lands your pawn on a snake and sends it sliding back. If you have played Snakes and Ladders, you probably remember the feeling. You might be eager to catch up or ready to pack away. The same snake can get a table talking and make one person lose interest.

That snake is our starting point. We will draw a small board, change its rules, compare other racing games, and put together a draft a friend can try. The turns and conversations in this course are teaching examples we have written, rather than accounts of actual playtests. The prototype still needs people to play it.

## Agree on the version first

Snakes and Ladders is a family of games with differing details. Hasbro’s Sesame Street Chutes and Ladders, product 32930, uses a spinner and makes a pawn stay put when its result would overshoot. Orchard Toys’ Jungle Snakes and Ladders, reference 629, uses a die and does not require an exact result to finish. Recognising the name does not settle the ending rule.

Our original teaching prototype is called The 24-Space Race. Its base version is B. Two players each have one pawn, starting at 0 outside numbered spaces 1–24. Randomly choose the first player before starting, then alternate turns. On a turn, roll one ordinary six-sided die and advance that many numbered spaces. Probability discussions explicitly assume a fair die.

There are four special connections: ladders from 3 to 11 and 8 to 17; snakes from 14 to 4 and 22 to 12. A connection triggers only when the movement ends at its entrance, never while passing it. Resolve the movement and connection, then end the turn. None of the exits starts another connection.

## Slow down one turn

Imagine a pawn at 6 and a roll of 2. First locate the landing space, 8. Then follow its ladder to 17. Finally pass the turn. “Move two spaces” describes only the first part; the pawn finishes the turn at 17.

From the same starting position, a roll of 6 passes space 8 but lands at 12. No ladder activates. A pawn at 12 rolling 2 lands at 14 and slides to 4. Trigger timing determines which of these movements is legal.

Pawns may share spaces; they neither block nor capture each other. Rolling 6 grants no extra turn, and no opening 6 is required. Reaching exactly 24 wins immediately. An overshoot leaves the pawn at its turn-start position, triggers no space effect, and ends the turn. From 23, a roll of 2 therefore leaves the pawn at 23. There is no bounce and no second roll.

## Rules determine outcomes; play gives them meaning

In the base game, once the die lands, the pawn’s destination is settled. You can hope for a 2, dread a 6, or cheer for someone else, but those wishes cannot change the number. Keeping wishes and choices separate will help us see what a new rule adds.

Waiting for a result can be part of the pleasure. You roll, someone counts along, and everyone watches the pawn slide. Look at who follows the action, who speaks, and who looks away. A laugh tells you something about that moment, but it cannot speak for everyone.

Putting a snake at 22 means that landing there sends a pawn back to 12. You can decide that while drawing the board. To find out whether the setback feels exciting or tiresome, you need to hear from the people playing.

## Write down this one turn

Record the position before the action, what happened, and the resulting state. “At 6; rolled 2; landed at 8; climbed to 17; changed player” is enough to check whether two people are executing the same rule.

Then choose a question. Can players distinguish passing 8 from landing there? After sliding backwards, do they anticipate the next roll or want to stop? The first concerns rule clarity; the second concerns experience. They call for different evidence.

You now have a base game you can put on the table. Try a few turns, including landing on a snake and overshooting the finish. When we change a rule later, these turns will give us something concrete to compare.
