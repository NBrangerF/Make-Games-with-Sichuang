# What Can You Decide Before You Have Rules?

“Let’s make a game about animals crossing a river.” That sounds like a direction, yet two designers may picture very different games. One imagines animals racing to arrive first. The other imagines them working things out together. The river, planks, and cute characters could look identical while giving players different things to care about.

Clearing up that difference helps more than immediately deciding how large to draw a plank. In a race, getting in someone’s way might be useful. In a shared crossing, staying behind to prepare materials might help the whole team. Without a clear purpose, each added rule could pull the game in a different direction.

## Describe the situation you want players to face

We have already seen the complete base crossing game. Now imagine working backward to a time when its rules were undecided. A provisional direction might be: two people at a small table play a paper game that fits into a short break. They discuss when each should move and when someone should prepare for their shared goal. Each person should have decisions of their own, and the task is complete only when both animals arrive.

Those few sentences can serve as a short design brief. They say who the game is for, the conditions it must fit, and what we hope will happen. They also leave things open. We do not yet need to choose four rounds, four starting planks, or collecting two planks at a time. Those are rule proposals to compare later.

“We hope they will discuss their plans” is not an achieved result. One person might decide everything while the other follows. So we can keep a question for observation: does each person have opportunities to propose and choose their own actions? Writing down a goal gives later drafts something to check.

## What is fixed, and what can change?

Suppose we only have paper and pens, and our table is small. Those are practical conditions. They make a long, three-dimensional river awkward, but do not decide whether players should cooperate or compete. “Both animals must arrive” is the direction we have currently chosen. Shared planks are one possible way to support it; other ways of helping each other could be explored too.

Keeping conditions separate from direction helps us respond to ideas. An electronic bridge that lights up may exceed our available materials. Giving victory only to the first arrival changes whose crossing matters to whom. These proposals affect different parts of the brief, so simply calling either one “good” or “bad” misses the decision involved.

Some limits are temporary ways to get a small piece working. We can start with one route to see how players divide preparation and movement. Once that relationship is clear, we can decide whether a second route helps. Leaving something out for now does not ban it forever.

## Use the brief to choose without getting trapped

Suppose we have two ideas: give each animal three hats to wear, or let an animal on the starting bank collect planks for both players. Hats might be enjoyable. But for our current direction, collecting more directly creates the relationship we want: one person’s preparation changes both animals’ journeys. That is a reason to try collecting first, without declaring hats worthless.

The brief can change too. If we later discover that we would rather make a relaxed character-dressing game, we can acknowledge the new direction and reconsider the rules. Trouble comes when the direction changes but we keep judging ideas against an old goal. Asking for tense planning while removing every reason to plan can make successive changes cancel each other out.

“Make a fun game” now has a direction two people can discuss. Use the brief to choose an approach and write an action you can put on the table. After testing, return with specific findings and revise these few sentences.
