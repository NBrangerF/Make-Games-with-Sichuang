# Why Pause to Think on My Turn?

We have separated an action into deciding, paying, and updating the situation. Now return to the first round of the basic game: Rabbit moved onto a stepping stone while Hedgehog stayed on the bank to collect planks. Yu did not immediately follow her friend. Her action helped provide enough material for both animals to cross.

Return to the moment before she decides. Rabbit is on the first stone, Hedgehog is on the starting bank, and three planks remain. It is Yu's turn. She can move, collect, or wait. To understand why she might pause, let's follow two of those choices separately.

## What Would Following Rabbit Leave Behind?

If Yu spends a plank to move Hedgehog, it joins Rabbit on the first stone. Two planks remain. Things seem to be going well: both animals have set out, and neither is behind.

Each animal, however, still needs two steps to reach the far bank. Together, they need four planks, but only two remain. Worse, collecting is allowed only on the starting bank. Both animals have left it, and neither can go backward. There is now no way to obtain the missing two planks.

They can still make a few more moves, but these rules no longer allow both to arrive. The problem begins when they lose the opportunity to collect at the end of the first round. We do not need to wait until the supply is empty to see it.

Suppose Yu collects instead. Hedgehog stays put, but the supply rises from three planks to five. Rabbit needs two more steps and Hedgehog needs three: exactly five planks altogether. Hedgehog has three rounds left, so moving once in each will also get it across in time. Staying behind for this turn keeps the later route possible.

## Getting Two Planks Means Giving Up a Step

Collecting helps, but it uses Yu's opportunity to move this turn. Each turn allows one action. She cannot take the planks and then add a forward step. We are comparing “gain two and stay here” with “move forward and spend one,” rather than simply “gain two” with “gain nothing.”

The other opportunity you give up when choosing something is often called an **opportunity cost**. Collecting has no printed price, yet Yu still spends a turn on it. Whether that is worthwhile depends on what the turn makes possible.

Nor can collecting always be treated as the right answer. Suppose Lin collects at the start instead. The supply rises from four to six while Rabbit stays on the bank. Yu can then move Hedgehog, leaving five planks. Both animals still have enough planks and enough remaining turns to finish crossing. This time Lin prepared the supply, so Yu can set out.

“Moving is bad; collecting is good” therefore misses the important difference. We need to look at who remains on the bank, how many planks are available, and who can still obtain any that are missing. An action can have the same name while helping with a different problem at a different moment.

## Seeing a Choice Includes Understanding What Follows

Imagine Yu saying, “I thought we could collect while on the stones, too.” She can see both available actions, but she is planning with a different rule in mind. Drawing bigger buttons would not explain why leaving the bank changes her future opportunities.

A specific reminder would help more than “Think carefully”: “Once you leave, you can't collect again.” It points out a consequence to consider while leaving the decision with Yu. She can ask questions, explain her plan, or accept advice.

If Lin simply takes a plank for her and moves Hedgehog himself, Yu remains at the table but misses the decision. Moving a piece with your hand and deciding its action are not necessarily the same thing. This ability to change consequences through your own decisions can be called **agency**. In the comparison we just followed, it has a concrete meaning: my decision can make what happens next different.

That does not require solving everything alone. Yu can ask, “How many more planks do we need?” and still decide after hearing the answer. Helping her understand the consequences and choosing the answer for her are different kinds of help.

To help Yu understand this choice, a designer can put “collect only on the starting bank” beside the collect action and keep the shared planks visible. She can then find the conditions she needs. A bright arrow pointing to the correct action might get her to follow it without understanding why. Helping her judge requires connecting the opportunity lost on departure with the costs still ahead.

## What Remains Once We Understand?

This crossing example is small. The first discovery—that moving faster now can leave no way through later—can take some thought. Once both players understand how to prepare, they may decide quickly when they play again. We do not need to make the numbers harder to read just to preserve difficulty.

An example that explains one relationship does not necessarily keep producing new questions. To develop a game people want to revisit, a designer would need to consider what situations could differ next time. The next chapter follows the crossing decision through one explicit change: what if the number of planks needed for this move becomes known only when the weather is revealed?
