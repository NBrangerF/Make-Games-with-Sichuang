# Did We Pay Already? Let the Table Remember Something

After choosing a ferry delivery, you still have three things to do: pay one energy, move the parcel across, and add two points. A friend interrupts. Looking back, you wonder whether you already paid. This article follows that one action and asks how the table can help you keep your place.

In a computer game, those updates can follow a single click. At a table, someone has to move the token, move the parcel, and record the points. The player gains another job: making sure all three happen, and happen only once.

## After the Decision, Finish the Result

Choosing the ferry may involve deciding whether it is worth spending energy. After that choice, moving the token and adding two points are *resolution*: carrying out a result according to the rules already established.

After choosing the ferry, the one-token fee, destination, and two-point gain are fixed. Players still have to carry them out without making a fresh strategic choice for every step. A designer therefore needs to consider both whether taking the ferry is an interesting choice and who will finish applying its consequences.

That does not mean resolution should disappear. Moving the parcel across may feel like completing a delivery, and adding points by hand may make success visible. The question is whether a step helps people experience or recognize a result, or mainly makes them worry about missing something.

## What a Photograph Cannot Tell You

Imagine someone picking up the energy token just as another person asks a question. Returning to the action, they cannot remember whether the fee was paid or the points added. The parcel’s location might be visible, while “already paid” has no fixed representation.

One option is to place the fee on the ferry card while the action is being handled. Only after delivering the parcel and adding the points does the player move it to the spent-token pile. The token takes on another job: reminding everyone that this action has not yet been finished.

It still cannot answer every question. If an interruption occurs during scoring, the fee sitting on the ferry does not establish whether points were already added. A reminder can preserve one fact without making every intermediate moment unambiguous. If this keeps causing trouble, the steps or their recording may need adjustment, rather than another complaint about memory.

## Reminders Also Need Updating

An extra “paid” card may seem safer. But if nobody flips it, it may say “unpaid” while the ferry holds the fee. Now the table offers two conflicting answers. Each new reminder needs someone to update it at an understood moment.

Sometimes the existing movement can preserve the information needed. The fee can travel in one direction, with usable and spent tokens clearly separated. If people repeatedly need to check whether a token is spendable, that arrangement records the answer directly. A marker that merely repeats an equally clear nearby record while requiring another update can add another opportunity for omission.

Forgetting a payment also differs from keeping a clue secret. A different game might include private orders; revealing those could change its deduction. A ferry fee that should be publicly confirmed is another matter. Recording it does not expose a secret the design needs.

## A record should answer the current question

The fee left on the ferry answers whether this action is still being handled. It does not identify which of delivery and scoring has finished. If the trouble repeatedly occurs between those steps, another representation could place a delivery token worth two points for each completed delivery, then count those tokens at the end instead of also moving a total-score marker. This replaces the original scoring step.

A token’s presence would record that score without requiring people to recall an earlier total. Each delivery must still receive exactly one token, distinguishable from tokens for other deliveries. The approach costs additional pieces and a final count. More recording is not automatically better; it matters whether the record preserves the fact previously being lost.

## What Can You Find by Walking through It Yourself?

A designer can follow the written steps for one delivery. Where does the fee come from? Can the parcel sit securely? How does the player recognize that scoring finishes the action? A prototype can be plain without leaving missing steps for friends to invent.

Yet knowing the intended rules makes it easy to supply details absent from the page. Other people still need to read and handle it. If a friend readily chooses the ferry but stops after paying to locate the score track, resolution may need attention. If carrying out the action is easy but the reason to choose the ferry is unclear, the choice itself needs another look.

Leave a trace on the table and ask someone to complete the ferry action. If they can find the next step after an interruption, the reminder has done a specific job. Then check whether it takes too much space or is easy to forget to update before deciding to keep it.
