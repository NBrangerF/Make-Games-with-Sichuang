# Are You Still Playing When You Do Not Move a Piece?

The chapter on turns showed how somebody else’s action can change your next move. Now narrow the question. When someone leaves their pieces alone, are they waiting with nothing to do, or watching for something they care about?

## A friend’s track can open my next move

Consider an independent train example, without the river rules. The board has a starting station, a middle station, and a final station. The first stretch of track is complete; the second is missing. Rui, Lin, and Yu act in that order. Each action either completes one missing stretch or moves the player’s train forward along one completed stretch. Trains can share stations, and neither action costs other items.

Lin’s train is at the middle station, the other two are at the start, and Rui is about to act. Rui could move to the middle station or complete the track leading to the final station.

Lin has something specific to watch despite having no action. If Rui builds, Lin can reach the final station next turn. If Rui moves instead, the gap remains and Lin cannot travel straight there. The wait carries a question: will that track be ready when my turn arrives?

Having no action and having no involvement are different things. Lin can anticipate Rui’s choice or reconsider his own next move. Those concerns remain connected to the changing railway while his train stays still. Unmoving hands alone cannot tell us whether he is waiting for an important result.

## Finding a job for the hands is not enough

Suppose every train move requires the other players to shift a tracking token from left to right. If that record neither affects later actions nor helps them understand the position, shifting it does not explain why they would care more about the railway.

A useful reminder could do something different. A noticeable marker beside newly completed track could help a player returning from a brief distraction see what changed. What matters is the information the reminder provides, not the extra movement involved in placing it.

Some games also let other players perform a related action when one person acts. This is called following. It gives another opportunity, along with another question to answer: should I follow, what will I pay, and what will I do? Reconsidering these questions on every turn can make frequent participation tiring too.

## Where does reading a card take your attention?

In another kind of game, where players select actions from their hands, drawing a new card at the start of a turn may mean beginning to read only when everyone is waiting. Drawing at the end lets players see a new card they might use next and prepare while waiting.

That may help with preparation, but we also need to consider what others are doing meanwhile. If a shared map keeps changing, reading a card with lots of text may mean missing a relevant route change. The reading has not vanished; some of it has moved into other people’s turns.

Having something to consider ahead of time can help. So can making changes easy to recognize on returning. One player may want to follow everything, while another needs a brief pause. The game can make newly connected track easy to spot without requiring everyone to watch every second.

## Relevant things can still compete

Suppose the separate hand-card game also has a railway theme. Even a story that fits this theme requires attention to read. Suppose it describes a town awaiting a train just as a crucial route opens on the shared map. Wanting to finish the story and wanting to follow the position both make sense. Putting both important events at the same moment raises a question about how somebody can attend to them.

One approach shortens what must be read during an action and leaves longer story passages until after a round. Another makes new track conspicuous so the reader can return to the board afterward. The first changes reading time; the second supports returning to the position. If the design particularly aims at following a partner’s actions, check whether private reading makes that connection too easy to lose. No arrangement creates attention from nothing.

## Find out what the player is waiting for

Imagine Lin has been quiet. Asking, “When that track was completed, where did you think you could go next?” could reveal whether Lin understood the change. Following the consequence differs from missing the new track altogether. Lin might also understand it while still deciding what to do.

“I cannot tell what changed when I look back” suggests one question about reading the board. “I chose ages ago, but everyone still has a lot to finish” suggests another about the length of the remaining procedures. Both experiences may be described as waiting, but they need different explanations.

Busy hands tell us little on their own. It is more useful to follow the step a player cares about: which change they await, whether they understand it, and how they can continue when their turn arrives. Waiting can contain anticipation, and players also need a way to return and carry on.
