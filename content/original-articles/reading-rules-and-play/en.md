# How Does a Game Happen, One Move at a Time?

Lin places a Rabbit piece on a sheet of paper. Yu puts Hedgehog beside it. A river lies ahead. Lin reaches to put Rabbit straight on the far bank, but Yu stops him: “Wait—can it jump all the way across?”

This is a useful place to begin understanding games. A hand can put a piece anywhere. What the piece may do in this game depends on the rules everyone agrees to use. We will follow an invented example from its beginning to its end. You can follow it in the text; you do not need any pieces.

## Know Where You Are Going

There is one route across the river: the starting bank, the first stepping stone, the second stepping stone, and the far bank. Both animals start on the starting bank. They can move only to the next position ahead, without skipping a position or going backward. They may share any position, so neither blocks the other.

Lin controls Rabbit and Yu controls Hedgehog. Their goal is to get both animals across. Getting just one across is not enough. Four planks lie in the middle of the table for them to share. Each step forward uses one plank to make a temporary bridge. Used planks are put aside and cannot be used again during this game. Another sixteen paper pieces sit outside the playing area as spare planks that have not yet been collected. They are not part of the available shared supply.

The objects now connect to one another. Rabbit needs a plank to move. When Rabbit uses one, Hedgehog has one fewer available. Knowing the names of the animals and the planks would not explain how this game works. We need to follow how a change to one affects the others.

## On Your Turn, Do One Thing

Lin acts first, then Yu. Each person's opportunity to act is a **turn**. When both have taken a turn, one **round** is complete. The game lasts at most four rounds.

On a turn, you choose one thing to do. You may spend one shared plank to move your animal one position forward. If it is still on the starting bank, you may instead keep it there and collect two new planks from those spare pieces and add them to the shared supply. You may also wait, without moving or collecting anything. You cannot move without enough planks, and you cannot collect after leaving the starting bank.

If Yu collects, she cannot move immediately afterward; she must wait for her next turn. An animal that reaches the far bank stays there. Its player can only wait on later turns. The players may discuss their plans, but each decides their own animal's action.

These rules give an action a clear beginning and end. We know who can act, what they may choose, what changes afterward, and when the next person gets a turn.

## Follow Both Animals Through Four Rounds

In the first round, Lin spends one plank and moves Rabbit onto the first stone. Three planks remain. Yu keeps Hedgehog on the starting bank and collects two, bringing the shared supply to five. Rabbit is ahead and Hedgehog has not left, but they now have more planks available.

In the second round, both move. Rabbit reaches the second stone; Hedgehog reaches the first. They each spend a plank, leaving three.

In the third round, Rabbit spends a plank to reach the far bank. Hedgehog also moves forward, reaching the second stone. One plank remains. Rabbit is safe, but their shared goal is still unfinished: Hedgehog must cross too.

In the fourth round, Lin waits with Rabbit on the far bank. Yu then spends the last plank to bring Hedgehog across. Both animals have arrived, so the game ends immediately with a shared win. If either animal had still been short of the far bank at the end of the fourth round, they would have lost.

The rules tell us both when to stop and what counts as success. Rabbit arriving alone does not mean they have won. Using the last plank does not necessarily mean failure, either: here, it took the last animal to the far bank.

## What Comes Next Depends on What Is Here Now

Return to the end of the first round. Rabbit is on the first stone. Hedgehog is on the starting bank. Five planks remain, and one round has passed. Together, the information that matters to what can happen next forms the current situation, often called the game's **state**.

It includes more than a score. Hedgehog's position means it can still collect. Rabbit's position means it no longer can. Five planks can pay for five more forward moves. With one round used, each animal has at most three turns left.

As the game proceeds, what is possible next changes too. Rules connect these changes, and players decide what to do in the new situation. The designer has written a shared agreement for acting, rather than a script that everyone must perform word for word.

We have followed one way to succeed. Why did Yu stay behind in the first round? Wouldn't moving both animals forward have been faster? Keep that question. First, we will learn how to observe: which actions did we actually see, and what explanations have we added to them? Later we will return to this choice and examine how it changes the road ahead.

This little game was invented as a starting point for explaining design. Working through it shows that its rules allow the sequence we followed. Whether friends enjoy it or want to play again remains something to investigate. In the chapters ahead, we will use it to explore ideas of our own and learn how to check a new draft.
