# How Can We Tell Whether an Option Is Too Strong?

The previous chapter separated a shared goal from each person’s decisions. In the basic game, Rabbit arrives before Hedgehog, yet both players win together. Arrival order alone does not show that Lin has beaten Yu. Their results are not scored separately.

When someone says “that is too strong,” first identify what they mean. Is one action always a better deal? Does one animal’s ability help more? Does acting first provide different opportunities? Or is the cooperative task too easy overall? These comparisons concern different things; “who finished first?” cannot answer all of them.

## Do Both Actions Have a Reason to Stay?

Add just one action to the basic game: a “reinforced move” spends two planks to move forward one step. The ordinary one-plank move remains available. Both take one turn and reach the same place. Reinforcement provides no additional protection, reward, or later effect. All other rules stay the same.

If Lin opens with an ordinary move, the supply falls from four to three and Rabbit reaches the first stone. A reinforced move leaves two planks, with Rabbit on that same stone. Both use the same opportunity; one leaves the team one fewer plank.

Under these rules, keeping more planks causes no penalty, blocks no space, and increases no cost. Wherever two planks can be paid, one can be paid too, leaving the other for later. For the goal of crossing together, reinforcement offers no benefit to compensate for its extra plank. Sometimes a plank might go unused and both plans could succeed, but that does not make reinforcement preferable.

If the purpose is to explain paying costs, this can be an explicit comparison example. If we want it to remain a trade-off with ordinary movement, it still needs a useful difference. The earlier island route, for instance, saved a step: its extra plank bought an action opportunity. Restoring that kind of difference requires specifying what is saved and spent, then following the whole plan again.

## A Greater Ability Is Different from More Personal Wins

Put reinforcement aside and consider a separate animal ability: Rabbit alone collects three new planks instead of two. Hedgehog still collects two. Everything else stays basic, including the shared supply.

If Rabbit performs the only collect action, the game provides four starting planks plus three new ones: seven altogether. If Hedgehog does it, the total is four plus two: six. In either plan, the collecting animal stays on the bank in round one while the other moves during that round, and both then follow the original route as soon as possible, waiting after arrival. Both plans finish in round four. The first spends six planks and leaves one; the second spends six and leaves none. Rabbit provides more material, but earns no individual victory or personal reward for the leftover plank.

Whether this ability makes decisions repetitive is another question. If Lin always collects simply because he gains one more plank, Yu may have less reason to decide who prepares. If the extra plank never helps on the actual journey, the numerical advantage may also matter less than it appears. Follow what an ability provides, whether it is useful, and which decisions it gives to which person.

Turn order should be examined separately too. In the basic game, Lin acts first and Yu can see what he actually spent before deciding. Exchanging their order changes which completed results inform a decision; it does not automatically give the three-plank ability to the other animal. Changing abilities and seats together makes it harder to tell which difference caused the result.

## What Does a Third Animal Need?

Remove the animal ability and return to collecting two planks. Change the game to three-player cooperation. The third player controls Squirrel, which also starts on the bank and follows the original three-step route. Each round, Lin acts, then Yu, then the third player, once each. There are still at most four rounds. All three animals arriving gives an immediate shared win; otherwise the end of round four is a loss. The initial shared supply remains four planks. Animals may share places without blocking, and enough spare pieces are provided for three players.

Three animals need nine moves and nine planks. Starting with four leaves a shortage of five. At two per collection, at least three collect actions are needed. Nine moves plus three collect actions use exactly the twelve turns provided by three players over four rounds.

Each animal must make its own three moves and has only four turns, so each must collect once. Giving two collections to the same animal would leave it too few moves. Collection is possible only on the starting bank, so all three must collect in round one. The supply rises from four to ten. Three moves in each of rounds two, three, and four leave seven, four, and one plank respectively, and all arrive. This version is possible, but tightly restricts the sequence: there is no spare wait, and no animal can leave in round one.

In the two-player game, one animal could collect while the other moved first. Adding a third decision-maker does not automatically add workable plans. Four starting planks and two per collection are not quantities that belong to an individual player. The journey they support has grown from six moves to nine.

Changing only the three-player starting supply from four to five opens another arrangement. In round one, Rabbit moves and both others collect, leaving eight planks. All three move in round two, leaving five. All three move in round three, leaving two, with Rabbit across. In round four, Rabbit waits and the other two move, spending the last two planks and winning together. One extra starting plank reduces necessary collections from three to two and makes room for a wait.

## A Calculated Advantage Must Answer a Specific Question

These comparisons identify different effects. Reinforcement costs extra material without compensation. Rabbit’s ability increases team supplies. Turn order changes what is visible before deciding. Three-player starting supplies change which action sequences are possible. They cannot all be addressed by making the numbers look similar.

Adding a plank to the three-player version makes one plan possible again; it does not establish that three people will prefer playing that way. Equal ability numbers also do not ensure that everyone feels equally involved. Later chapters on observation and revision will pursue those questions. Here we first identify what is being compared and the clear consequences of its rules.

Return to the two-player base game and make the fairness question specific: who has actions left, who can add planks, and who must wait? Map those opportunities, then hear how both people describe this attempt at cooperating.
