# Where does a reroll token come from, and where does it go?

Set aside two-dice version C. Begin with the separate R experiment, built on single-die version B. Each player starts with two reroll tokens. After seeing the die but before moving, they may spend one to reroll once and must accept the new result. Spent tokens never return. Imagine a pawn on 4, one token remaining, and a roll of 1: accept space 5, or spend the last token on another uncertain result.

## After spending the last token

The token buys one replacement roll. From 4, a new 2 reaches 6; a 4 reaches the ladder at 8 and climbs to 17; another 1 still reaches only 5. You have already paid, but must wait for the die to know what that payment brings.

Setup supplies the resource. Rerolling before movement consumes it. With no tokens, ordinary movement remains available; only the reroll option disappears. Exhaustion changes the available choices rather than stopping the game.

Sketch the player's supply, the decision where a token is used, and the discard area. Specify who can spend, when, and whether anything returns. R gives players separate supplies; it contains no borrowing or sharing rule.

## Add one supply space

On a separate rule sheet, write “Supply version E.” Keep reroll version R and add one rule: actually moving to finish on ordinary space 6 grants one token, up to a holding limit of two. Staying still earns nothing. This version still uses one die.

Return to space 4 with one token and an initial roll of 1. Suppose the player spends the token and rerolls a 2. Their supply falls to zero, the pawn moves to 6, and one token enters their supply. The same results in R would leave no token. In E, that destination provides movement and replenishment; it does not make every reroll free.

Arriving with two tokens leaves the supply at two. A holding limit prevents unlimited accumulation, but does not cap total receipts across the game. A later snake could return the pawn to 4, allowing another arrival at 6. Repeated access is possible, but players cannot choose to move backward or remain on 6 to collect repeatedly.

## Will a refill change when you spend?

Renewable tokens may encourage using them sooner. A player approaching 6 might be willing to risk a reroll to obtain another token. We do not yet know whether players recognize that relationship or how often replenishment occurs.

During a playtest, follow one player’s supply: tokens received, spent, replenished, and refused because the player already held two. Ask why they rerolled at that position. This can show whether the supply space affects decisions as well as the number of paper tokens on the table.

The ending matters. On space 23, a token could provide another chance to finish exactly. After the game ends, unused tokens score nothing. Having the largest reserve is not a separate achievement in these rules.

## Compare two matching traces

To compare R and E, use identical starting positions, supplies, initial rolls, and reroll results. Record each resource change. This controlled construction checks the logic of replenishment, not its frequency in natural play.

In later complete sessions, separate newly gained tokens, expenditures, and gains blocked by the cap. Rare replenishment may concern access; forgotten replenishment may concern presentation. Unspent tokens need to be understood through player goals and explanations.

Draw a resource route for each draft. The reroll version runs from two starting tokens towards an empty supply; the supply version offers a way back through space 6. Watch for a refill that changes a later decision. E stays in this set of experiments; the eventual handoff still uses choice version C without tokens.
