# How Can Help Preserve Her Decision?

Imagine Yu pausing on her turn without moving Hedgehog. Overlapping paper pieces might hide the number of available planks. She might know she wants to collect but struggle to pick up thin paper. Or she might understand every action and still be deciding whether to move. The visible pause is similar; the useful help differs.

When choosing a play setting, we asked who would join and under what conditions. With actions and materials in place, we can now examine one concrete moment: what does she need to perceive and handle, and what does she want to decide herself?

## Find the step that is difficult

If Yu says, “I cannot count them,” spreading the central planks apart or making their number visible from her seat may help. If she says, “I do not know which pile I can spend,” explain the available, used, and reserve areas. Repeating the number alone would not answer that question.

If she says, “I want to collect, but the paper is too thin to pick up,” she has already decided. Thicker pieces could help, or somebody could move them according to her choice. If she says, “I am still thinking,” choosing collect for her would address a handling problem she never reported while ending her consideration.

Help can therefore begin with a concrete offer: “Would you like something explained, or help moving some pieces?” Every pause need not prompt an interruption. When intervening, however, checking the need is more reliable than inferring it from speed alone.

## Give the same information more than one recognizable form

In the basic game, plank counts and animal positions are public to both people. If similar colors alone distinguish the animals, add Rabbit and Hedgehog shapes or names. If the words on pieces are too small, enlarge the essential labels. Color, shape, and wording can complement one another so a person need not depend on just one cue.

Additional cues must agree. Showing Rabbit on the first stone while a nearby description says the second creates a conflict. A long reference sheet that pushes the board away may make reading easier while making the animal harder to reach.

Appealing materials and usable materials also deserve separate attention. Someone might enjoy the weight of a wooden piece. A large animal figure might hide its stone. Looking more elaborate does not establish that participation becomes easier. We need to identify the particular object and action involved.

## The person moving a piece need not make the decision

Suppose Hedgehog is still at the start and Yu says, “I choose collect. Please take two reserve pieces and put them in the center for me.” Lin does so, Hedgehog stays still, and Yu’s turn ends. It remains her chosen collect action. Which fingers moved the paper does not change its result.

If Lin conveniently chooses both animals’ actions every round, he changes who decides. Yu might still place pieces herself while having little choice left. Handling assistance and strategic advice can therefore be separate: offering to place something does not give permission to decide what to place.

Advice can preserve agency too. Lin can explain why both animals moving first would leave them short of planks, then let Yu consider it. The cooperation chapter developed that reason. Here we need to ensure that she can express her choice afterward and that any assistance follows it.

## Feedback need not require the same performance from everyone

After a move, seeing the animal on its new stone and one fewer available plank provides useful feedback. Requiring the player to stand up and imitate a jump before that move counts would add a bodily performance as a condition of participation.

One person might enjoy performing while another would rather not do it in front of the group. If the design still aims at discussing a shared crossing, imitation can remain optional, with speaking an action or indicating a destination also allowing a choice. A game specifically about performing movements would need its conditions explained afresh. Reluctance to perform cannot simply be treated as failure to understand cooperation.

Being able to reach a piece does not guarantee feeling comfortable either. Whether help was requested, or whether others publicly judge her handling, can affect the encounter. We can provide ways to express a need and listen to participants’ actual accounts without announcing in advance that someone now feels at ease.

## The kind of change determines what it changes

Thicker pieces, a closer shared supply, and spoken choices can support the existing actions. Giving Hedgehog an extra move changes its opportunities and creates a rule variation that needs fresh calculation and explanation. Both kinds of change are open to discussion; they address different problems.

Ask which step a change makes easier and which decisions it preserves. The next chapter uses such a concrete question to choose a prototype’s level of detail. Checking whether positions are visible and checking whether people want to discuss a plan do not require exactly the same details.
