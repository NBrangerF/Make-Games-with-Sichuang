# Waiting for the die—or waiting for the game to end?

Imagine this passage in version B of The 24-Space Race. Lin is at 20 and rolls 2. Yu says, “Not that snake.” Lin moves to 22, follows the snake back to 12, and leaves a hand beside the board. It would be easy to report that the snake created suspense. It would be equally easy to report that moving backwards caused frustration.

Before naming the feeling, look a little longer. Why did Lin leave a hand there? What did Yu mean by that remark? The movement alone cannot answer either question.

## Keep what you actually saw

Start with a note such as: “Lin at 20; rolled 2; moved to 22, then back to 12. Yu said, ‘Not that snake.’” When you return to the note, you can replay the turn on the board.

“He was unhappy” is already your guess. A pause might mean disappointment, checking whether the move had finished, or waiting for the other person to take the die. “Paused” alone is not enough either; note where it happened and where he was looking: “After moving to 12, kept looking at 22 instead of passing the die.”

Leave gaps where you missed something. Mark a remark as unheard if you could not catch it. Guessing a starting position later could change your account of the entire move; a gap reminds you what to watch next time.

## Separate the position from the feelings

After this turn, Lin is at 12 and Yu has the next turn. B offers no action to refuse the snake, pay for protection, or reroll. This helps detect advice based on a mistaken reading. Someone suggesting that Lin should have taken another route must first show that another route exists.

Watch how both people follow the race. Do they count how far the other pawn is from the finish? Say which number they want before rolling? Follow the other person’s movement? These small actions show what holds their attention when the game offers few choices.

Keep the two people separate, too. Yu’s shout does not establish Lin’s feelings or the satisfaction of the whole table. If the winner asks to play again, the other player still needs room to express a different view.

## Ask something that can separate explanations

At an appropriate moment, ask, “What were you thinking after returning to 12?” This invites an account. “Was that snake too annoying?” hands him an answer in advance, and he may simply nod to be polite.

“I thought I still had two more spaces to move” suggests a resolution problem. “I was checking whether I could catch up” suggests continuing attention to the race. The two answers lead to different changes. When you write one down, keep it next to what he just did.

A follow-up task can help check an explanation. If the snake rule seems unclear, set up one move that passes its head and another that lands there. Ask the player to move the pawn. Save the question about playing another game until they have had a game to judge.

## Follow one question through a short passage

A first observation could follow one round, from one player receiving the die until both have finished a turn. Use three columns: what people did and said; how the position changed; your guesses, more than one if you like. With guesses in their own column, you can change your mind later without rewriting what you saw.

To investigate waiting, watch the person without the die as well as the active player. To investigate setbacks, retain the position before the slide and what happens on the following turn. Removing context can make different experiences appear identical.

A useful observation sheet lets you picture the turn again. Keep that detail before deciding to move the snake, rewrite an instruction, or change how you invite someone to try the game.
