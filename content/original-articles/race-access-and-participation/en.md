# Can you make the choice you understand?

Imagine Yu looking at the dice. She wants the 2, but cannot reach her pawn on the far side of the board. Lin reaches over to help: “Six is bigger; take the six.” The pawn has moved, but Yu’s decision has not been followed.

Several steps sit between reading a position and moving a pawn. In this scene, Yu needs help reaching the piece. She has already decided which die to use.

## Locate the task that is difficult

We are using choice version C: two players alternate turns, rolling two dice and choosing one for a single move. Only landing triggers a snake or ladder. From 6, Yu’s 2 reaches 8 and climbs to 17; the 6 stops at 12. She understands the difference. Letting her point to the destination while someone moves the piece would address the difficulty she has.

If the difficulty is seeing a ladder, moving the pawn for someone does not solve it. If the player adds the dice, the action reminder may need work. A player who understands the rule but does not want to explain their reasoning has not necessarily failed to understand. Describe the specific task before proposing a solution.

## Give important information more than one cue

Pawns distinguished only by red and green may be difficult to tell apart under some visual, lighting, or printing conditions. Distinct shapes, with a matching shape beside each player, offer another cue. Ladder rungs, a different curved path for snakes, directional arrows, and clear labels can distinguish transfers without relying entirely on color.

Put the materials on the table and check whether larger numbers crowd the route or extra symbols are easy to recognise. Ask someone to find the connection from 8 to 17. That checks the task more directly than asking which board looks prettier. A grayscale print is also useful for finding information carried by colour alone; feedback from people using the materials still matters.

## Change the operation, or change the game?

If grasping dice is difficult, a companion can roll while the player keeps the choice of result. Another proposal uses six numbered cards: draw one, replace it, mix, and repeat. This matches the probability model of two fair six-sided dice only under explicit assumptions: equally likely numbers, replacement every time, and independent draws. Forgetting replacement changes the mechanism.

Letting someone simply name a number from 1 to 6 may be easier to operate, but replaces a random result with a free choice. That can be an interesting new game. It should receive its own version and a fresh examination of decisions and difficulty, rather than being described as an unchanged way to operate C.

## Ask how the player wants to do it

Snail’s Race 20629 uses all six snails, with players predicting the first and last finishers rather than controlling one each. It offers another way to participate: watching and predicting together. If you borrow that approach, ask whether the people at your table want to do that task.

An invitation can be direct: “If the piece is out of reach, point to where you want it and I’ll move it. We can try another way too.” Explain that pausing or stopping is always possible. In your notes, separate a companion moving the pawn from a companion choosing the number. That distinction lets you check whether the help preserved the player’s decision.
