# Does being closer to the finish always mean a better move?

Return the pawn to space 6 and set out dice showing 2 and 6. In choice version C, the smaller number reaches 17 by ladder; the larger stops at 12. Before choosing, draw both moves. Where does the die take you, and where does the board send you next?

## Find where the pawn actually stops

Counting from 6 to 8 is shorter than counting from 6 to 12, but addition gives only the initial landing space. We must then check its effect. The ladder activates when a pawn lands on 8, not when it passes through. A move of six does not collect that ladder on the way.

Draw two small maps of the action. The first shows the landing spaces offered by the dice: 8 and 12. The second shows their resolved destinations: 17 and 12. Recording this intermediate step explains where the opportunity arises more clearly than labeling one route “fast.”

The two maps show which move travels farther this turn. Comparing the chances of winning from 17 and 12 would mean looking beyond those destinations. Their numbers alone cannot settle that question.

## Visual neighbors need not be connected

Redraw the same 24 spaces as a circle instead of a winding track. If the numbering, four transfers, and ending rule stay the same, the legal moves stay the same. Conversely, drawing spaces 6 and 17 beside each other does not create permission to jump between them.

The important spatial structure is a set of connections. Ordinary movement follows increasing numbers; special transfers connect 3 to 11, 8 to 17, 14 to 4, and 22 to 12. Those connections have directions. A ladder's symmetrical drawing does not allow a return trip from 17 to 8.

When redrawing, keep the numbered route continuous and mark each transfer’s direction. Ask someone new to the sketch to find the way from 8 to 17. If they start looking backwards, check the drawing rather than relying on familiarity with Snakes and Ladders.

## A branch needs an actual decision

Before adding a fork, specify what decision it creates. Does the player choose a route before seeing the dice, or afterward? How are its spaces counted? Can a pawn change routes midway? Does a transfer still apply? A second line on paper cannot answer these questions.

A useful local prototype lists each route's entry conditions, stopping spaces, and subsequent exits. Place the same dice beside it and compare the consequences. If both routes lead to identical positions and future opportunities, the choice primarily expresses a preference. That may be worthwhile, but it is different from navigating a new spatial tradeoff.

Draw the branch on a separate draft and keep the choice version’s numbered route for comparison. With both sheets side by side, the added entry conditions and destinations are easier to see.

## Does arriving first let you hold the space?

Two pawns may share any space in C. Arriving at 17 does not block the ladder for your opponent, charge them a fee, or send either pawn back. Players compete to reach exactly 24 first, rather than to hold space 17.

Adding a capture rule would change this relationship. A ladder destination could become both an acceleration opportunity and an attack target. That deserves its own comparison, with an explicit rule, rather than being assumed because another racing game permits captures.

Circle one important destination on your own map. Write how a pawn reaches it, how it leaves, and whether another pawn can share it. Together, those answers show whether the space is a shortcut, a shared stopping place, or somewhere worth contesting.
