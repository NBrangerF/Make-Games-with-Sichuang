# What Have You Actually Seen After Watching a Game?

In the first round, Lin moves Rabbit forward one step. The shared supply drops from four planks to three. Yu then keeps Hedgehog on the starting bank and collects two new planks, bringing the supply to five. One animal has moved ahead; the other has stayed still. If we watch positions alone, Lin seems to be making progress while Yu holds things up.

But collecting changes the situation too. The animals need six moves between them, and their starting four planks cannot pay for all six. If both move in the first round, the two remaining planks cannot cover the four moves still needed. Both animals have also left the only place where they can collect. Watching positions and planks together reveals how staying still can help them cross.

A designer needs to notice the whole change. Otherwise, “the animal did not move” can become “that turn did nothing,” followed by a new action the game never needed.

## Describe what happened before judging it

“They worked well together” already includes a judgment. “After Lin moved, three planks remained; Yu collected, bringing the supply to five” lets someone else reconstruct the situation. We can then add what people said, what they did first, and what happened next. Order matters: did Yu collect after a reminder from Lin, or did she suggest staying behind? Both paths can produce five planks while raising different questions.

These are two imagined possibilities, not reports of a real playtest. Suppose Lin says, “Stay there and collect first,” and Yu does. We can say that he proposed a plan and she followed it. That sentence alone cannot tell us whether she had ideas of her own or enjoyed the reminder. She may already have had the same plan, or she may still be learning the actions.

A little arrow showing the supply changing from four to three to five is more useful than quickly calling someone “cooperative” or “passive.” The arrow preserves a change we can check. The label can hide what we still do not know.

## Your feelings are clues with limits

If you play this round and feel impatient while Yu decides, you can keep that observation too. Add the moment: Rabbit was on the first stepping stone, three planks remained, and you were worried about having enough next round. Now the feeling is connected to a particular situation.

Your impatience cannot tell us how Yu felt. She might be excited because she has just understood the plan. Or she might be unsure whether collecting ends her turn. The same pause could come from comparing choices or from a question about the rules. Seeing her pause and knowing why she paused are separate steps.

You could ask, “What were you considering when it was your turn?” and compare her answer with her actions. Asking “Were the rules too difficult?” puts the designer’s guess inside the question. Even if she agrees, that may not have been the original cause.

## Turn a small event into a useful question

Observing does not require writing down every word. To understand how players decide who stays behind, watch when they propose plans, respond, and act. To understand whether the planks are easy to distinguish, watch where pieces are picked up and placed, and where questions arise. A focus helps us choose what to record. It should still leave room for surprises. If neither player knows whose turn it is, that will affect their cooperation too.

After this round, a useful question is: “Can players recognize that staying on the starting bank can help their shared goal?” Jumping straight to “collecting should also move the animal forward” skips ahead to a change. That change would remove the cost of staying behind and alter how the crossing works.

Write the actions, your own feelings, and your guesses separately, then read them back. The gaps become easier to find, along with who to watch and what to ask next time.
