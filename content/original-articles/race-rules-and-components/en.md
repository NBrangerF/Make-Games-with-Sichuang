# With two dice on the table, how do the rules produce one move?

Remember the 2 and 6 beside a pawn on 6? Choice version C lets you choose 2 and climb to 17. Now deliberately add the dice: eight steps from 6 land at 14 and send you back to 4. One missed instruction to choose can send the turn in the opposite direction.

## Put the distinction where the action happens

Writing “choose a result” on the opening page may be insufficient. Two dice placed together can suggest addition. A short reminder can say: “Roll two. Choose one. Move once.” A drawn box for the selected die, with the other placed aside, makes the choice visible.

The box adds no new action. It preserves a decision so another person can check the result used, or the player can recover their place while counting. Doubles still mean selecting one die and moving once, without a bonus.

Locate information near its use: choice beside the dice, destination beside the ladder, and exact arrival beside the finish. A distant block of complete rules may be difficult to consult at the relevant moment.

## Let the text and board explain the same sequence

The turn has a short procedure: inspect both dice and select one; calculate the destination from the current position; if it exceeds 24, stay at the turn's starting position and end; otherwise move and apply that space's snake or ladder transfer. Reaching exactly 24 wins immediately.

None of the four transfer destinations starts another transfer. Readers need not guess about chains. Passing through 8 does nothing; landing there moves the pawn to 17, without permitting a return climb. A local diagram can show these distinctions more clearly than a lengthy list of possible misunderstandings.

Check a second position: space 23 with results of 2 and 6. Both overshoot, so the pawn stays on 23 and the turn ends. There is no bounce, reroll, or bonus turn because neither result fits. Improvising a rescue at the table would change the rules.

## Components should not communicate through color alone

Give each transfer a starting number, destination number, and directional arrow. Snakes and ladders may use different pictures, but their meaning should not depend only on red versus green. Distinct pawn shapes can also help identify players.

Make the finish visually distinct while retaining 24. Place the starting area 0 outside numbered spaces 1–24, avoiding uncertainty about whether a pawn on 1 has already moved. Allow enough room for two pawns to share a space, so physical crowding does not imply exclusive occupation.

Place the instructions beside the board and use them. At “choose one,” is there somewhere to put the selected die? At “share a space,” can both pawns fit? Matching these details helps the materials explain the rules.

## Observe one turn before revising the explanation

Prepare the board, two dice, and a short rule card. Set out the pawn at 6 with results of 2 and 6, then ask a reader to move. Note which die they choose, where they begin counting, whether they use the ladder, and where they pause to ask for help.

Locate any error before explaining it. Adding dice may require a stronger choice cue. Selecting 2 but reaching 12 may concern counting. Landing on 8 without transferring may concern the arrow. Do not reduce all mistakes to careless reading.

Change the cue that most affects the task, then try that task again. Once this turn reads clearly, use the complete rules in chapter 27 with someone who has not heard your explanation, starting from setup. The die box, arrows, and finish reminder you refine here can all go on that rule sheet.
