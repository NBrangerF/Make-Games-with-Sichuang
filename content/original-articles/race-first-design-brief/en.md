# Give the little race a direction

“Make Snakes and Ladders more fun.” Write that down and the ideas soon pile up: another die, a few item cards, a way for the trailing player to catch up. Each sounds promising. Which one should you make first?

A design brief gives you a reason to choose. It can be short: say what you want players to do differently, then decide which rules would make that possible.

## Choose one thing to change

Try this: “Give two players a chance to choose how they move, as well as waiting for the roll. They should be able to look at the board and explain why they picked a number.” That keeps the anticipation of Snakes and Ladders while giving this draft a manageable task: add one small movement decision.

Put a pawn at 6 and set out dice showing 2 and 6. Choosing 2 lands at 8 and climbs to 17; choosing 6 stops at 12. “Choose how to move” now means something you can show. A player needs to look at the landing space as well as the number on the die.

This suggests a first draft: roll two dice each turn, choose one for a single move, and ignore the other. Doubles still give one move. We call this the choice version, C; the other rules stay as they were in base version B. The brief describes the change we want, and this rule gives us a way to try it.

## Keep the draft small enough to make

Use the existing twenty-four-space board and its four snake and ladder connections. You need two people, two pawns, and two six-sided dice. Players still alternate turns, may share spaces, and win by reaching exactly 24. With those materials ready, you can try the choice without first making cards or a shop.

If you want a game that fits a short break, put timing on your playtest list. Twenty-four spaces look short, but snakes and repeated attempts to finish can extend a game. Explaining and packing away take time too.

That limit helps you handle new ideas. Note the item cards for later. Before adding the work of making and explaining them, ask what they would contribute to this movement decision.

## Know which moment to watch

Ask someone to handle the 2 and 6 at space 6, and listen to how they choose. If they add the dice together, check the instruction to choose one. If they move correctly but keep wanting to hurry to the next roll, ask how the extra pause feels.

Those responses suggest different revisions. The first calls for a clearer instruction. The second might tell you that this person prefers the pace of rolling and moving straight away. A note saying only “went well” or “went badly” would leave you guessing what to change.

Add a sentence to the brief: “First watch whether someone can choose a die and complete the move without help, then ask whether the pause to think was worthwhile.” That is our plan for testing the draft. Whether people enjoy the choice is something we still need to find out through play.

## Let the brief help you choose

Suppose someone suggests removing the exact-finish rule. That mainly addresses waiting near the end. It is worth trying separately, though it adds no movement decision. A reroll is closer to the brief, but asks a different question: will you give up a known result to try your luck again?

You can fit the brief on one sheet: who you will invite, what you want them to do differently, what you will make, and which moment you will watch. When you change your mind, revise that part and note why.

Look at the ideas on the table again. You now have a reason to start with one of them. Make the version that best expresses this direction, and keep the others for when you need them.
