# How Can a Friend Begin from the Instructions?

Imagine a friend reading the crossing rules for the first time. The instruction says, “Stay at the start and collect two planks.” The friend takes the planks, then moves the animal forward. Perhaps collecting seemed like preparation before moving. The designer knows that a turn permits only one action, but the page has not made the relationship clear enough.

Writing rules means turning the settled design into instructions somebody else can execute. That includes sentences and the arrangement of the objects in front of them.

## First explain what people are trying to finish

A reader encountering exceptions before the stones and planks may struggle to understand their purpose. Begin with the goal: “Work together to get Rabbit and Hedgehog across within four rounds.” Then introduce Lin controlling Rabbit, Yu controlling Hedgehog, and both starting on the near bank. The route is the start, first stone, second stone, and far bank, in that order.

Details now have a place. Moving means spending one plank from the center to reach the next adjacent position ahead. Both animals can occupy the same place without blocking each other. Naming the direction resolves a question that “move one step” could leave open.

Explain the timing too: “In each round, Lin takes one turn, then Yu takes one turn. The round ends after both have acted.” One person’s turn and the round containing both turns now have distinct scopes.

## Write the connection where a misreading occurs

Start the actions with, “On your turn, choose exactly one of the following.” Then give move, collect, and wait. For collect, write: “You may choose this only while your animal is at the start. Leave it there, take two new planks from the reserve paper pieces, and add them to the center.” Waiting moves nothing and gains no planks.

Collecting now reads as the turn’s action rather than a free preliminary step. Keeping the condition beside the action also avoids making players discover much later that an animal on a stone cannot collect. Explain that an animal on the far bank can only wait on later turns near the consequences of moving, and include it in a reference reminder too.

Continue with a worked round to see whether the sentences connect. Lin spends one plank and moves Rabbit, reducing the central supply from four to three. Yu collects two new planks; Hedgehog stays at the start and the supply becomes five. Both have now acted, so the round marker advances to round two. Neither receives an extra move.

Place the ending at a definite moment: both animals arriving means an immediate win; otherwise, the end of round four means a loss. There is no need for a purposeless extra action after both have arrived.

## Paper pieces belong in three different places

Available planks in the center, used planks beside them, and reserve paper pieces off the table have different roles. The center holds the shared resource the players currently own. Spent planks enter the used area and cannot be collected again. Reserve pieces represent newly obtained planks later; they cannot pay for a move now.

For the basic paper version, prepare twenty pieces: four in the center and sixteen in reserve. Four rounds allow at most eight actions, so sixteen reserve pieces suffice even if every action is collect. This prepares the components; it does not add a limited warehouse for players to compete over. A version with more rounds needs enough paper pieces for that version too.

Separating the three places makes the position easier to check than asking people to remember which pieces in a mixed pile are usable. Names should remain consistent as well. Calling the same item a plank in an action, a bridge in a picture, and a building resource in a list can make it seem like three different things.

## Make the current judgment easy to see

A large Rabbit figure may attract attention, but if it hides a stepping stone, Yu may struggle to identify the animals’ positions. Reducing its size or changing its placement can reveal information that matters. Decorative size and color direct attention, so appearance is more than a question of liking the look.

The round must also remain visible. Unmoved animals could mean neither player has acted, or that both just waited. Positions alone cannot identify the round. A simple marker labeled one through four can preserve that otherwise invisible information. It updates after both players complete their actions.

## Understanding first, finding a detail later

A first reading needs the goal, setup, turn order, and a complete action in an understandable sequence. Later, someone may only want to find whether an animal on the far bank can collect. Short action reminders and clear headings support that search. Essential conditions should also appear in the complete instructions, rather than only on a small card that could go missing.

Following the instructions through the first round can reveal connections the designer omitted. Having somebody unfamiliar begin independently can reveal further problems; a later chapter examines that process. For now, make the explanation, names, and table point to the same rules. Next we consider how different people can see and carry out those rules while keeping their own decisions.
