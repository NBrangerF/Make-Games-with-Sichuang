# Are Four Options More Worth Thinking About Than Two?

Four cards have different colours, but each gives two points and you can take only one. If you want the most points, what is there to choose? Two more colours would do little. With four cards and seven stones, we can examine how the number of options relates to their consequences.

## Four Colors, One Result

Imagine four cards: red, blue, yellow, and green. You choose one. Every card gives two points, and then the activity ends. The only objective is to gain more points; color has no other effect.

For scoring, every choice is equivalent. Adding purple and orange simply adds two cards to inspect. If these choices are meant to invite strategic thought, the question is what a card changes about the result, beyond how many colors appear.

Now change only the red card to three points. The others still give two. There is a difference, but choosing remains straightforward: if points are all that matter, take red. Different consequences do not necessarily require a long comparison.

An easy choice is not necessarily a problem. It could help someone learn to take a card and count points. But if the activity promises repeated, careful planning, these cards do not yet provide it.

## Two Ways to Take Stones Lead to Different Routes

Consider another example. Seven stones lie on a table. Two people take turns removing one or two. Whoever takes the last stone wins. All stones are visible; there are no dice or secrets. You go first.

Take one, leaving six for your friend. Suppose they take two, leaving four. You take one, leaving three. Your friend takes one, leaving two. You take both and win.

What if your friend takes differently? From six, they take one and you take two, again leaving three. From three, they take two and you take the final one. The important step is this: after leaving six, make your friend's move and your following move total three stones. Six becomes three, then zero, with you taking the last stone.

If you take two on the opening move instead, you leave five. Your friend can take two and leave three for you. Whether you then take one or two, your friend can take the remainder and win.

There are only two permitted ways to take stones, yet understanding the first move requires connecting what remains now with what the next person can take. Visible information does not mean the answer has already been explained.

## Finding the Answer Does Not Make It Last Forever

Once you know this approach, you quickly recognize the advantage of taking one first. The seven-stone example also clearly favors the starting player. It can explain how to think ahead without being a balanced game that keeps offering reasons to return.

It differs from the four equal cards. Those cards have no scoring difference from the beginning. The two opening moves with seven stones do differ, but following the later moves makes that difference visible. Calling both situations “nothing to choose” makes it harder to identify what needs attention.

If the purpose is to help readers understand a relationship, explaining the stone sequence can serve it. If the purpose is to give players a fresh question every game, a designer needs to find situations that change. Adding names to cards does not automatically do that work.

To create another situation worth comparing, a designer could change the starting number of stones before adding ten ways to take them. Change only seven stones to six and keep yourself first. Your friend can now make each pair of turns remove three stones and win. Changing the starting state changes who can first leave a multiple of three to the other player. It also shows why going first is not advantageous at every starting number. This supplies another reasoning situation, while familiar players can still find its fixed answer.

## Some Choices Are About Something Other Than Points

Finally, return to the original four cards, each worth two points. Suppose choosing a color means choosing a flag for your character. You might like blue and prefer it without gaining an extra point. That choice expresses a preference. Having no scoring difference does not make it useless.

A character flag, however, cannot stand in for a strategic comparison that was promised. The designer needs to understand the choice's job: expressing a preference, learning an operation, or comparing consequences. Asking whether the options do that job brings us closer to the problem than asking how many more cards could be added.
