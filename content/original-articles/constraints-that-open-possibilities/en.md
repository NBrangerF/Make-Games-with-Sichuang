# Making Less and Allowing Fewer Moves Are Different Choices

For a theatre game, you imagine ticket sales, performer training, a programme, and stage decorations. Making only the programme at first saves work. That differs from a rule allowing a player to schedule only two acts: one limits what you build, the other what a player can do.

The theatre draft starts with arranging acts, leaving ticket prices, performer development, and decorations for later. That gives us time to examine how a few programme cards affect one another, then decide which limits to put on the players.

## A smaller scope still needs a relationship to examine

In the first draft, two people arrange a show together. Each has two face-up cards: one “Singing” and one “Storytelling.” They alternate adding one of their cards to the end of a shared program. Placing all four ends the activity. There is no score or winner; for now, we are checking whether arranging acts can run.

Either type may come first, but nothing yet makes different orders produce different consequences. Players have cards with different names while possibly having no reason to compare them. Reducing the scope of production does not guarantee that what remains offers worthwhile choices.

We can recognize that gap before choosing which relationship to add. Ticket money may not help yet. It needs rules for when people receive it, how much they receive, what it buys, or how it affects the ending. If none of those things affects the program, we have only added another set of numbers to maintain.

## A player restriction can connect actions and remove choices

Now try a separate rule version: singing and storytelling must alternate, so matching types cannot be adjacent. On your turn, if you have a card that can legally follow the last one, choose and place one. If you have no legal card, including having no cards left, pass. End when all four cards are placed, or earlier if both players pass consecutively. There is still no score or winner.

This restriction makes one act affect the next. Suppose the first player opens with Singing. The second must place Storytelling. The first player’s remaining Storytelling card is now illegal. What the second player did has changed what the first is allowed to do.

Continue the sequence. The first player must pass. The second still has Singing and can place it. The first then places Storytelling, completing all four cards. The rule does not inevitably trap the activity, but it often reduces a turn to one legal action. We have connected the acts without establishing that we have created rich choices.

If we want players to discuss the order, different legal arrangements need different consequences. Audience preferences could be explored in another version, but we would still have to explain how they work. “Add an audience” does not complete that design work.

## Can the limits make each other impossible?

Consider another separate change: the stage has only three spaces, each holds at most one card, and placed cards cannot be stacked, removed, or moved. Yet completion still requires placing the original four cards. These conditions cannot all be satisfied. Three spaces cannot hold four cards, however hard the players try.

We could change the goal to selecting three acts. That requires deciding which act stays out, who makes that decision, and how the activity ends. It might become a worthwhile new game, but replacing “four” with “three” does not settle all those steps.

When narrowing production scope, ask whether it makes the relationship we want to examine easier to see. When restricting players, examine which consequences change, how many choices remain, and whether the goal is still achievable. Once the small draft can answer its immediate question, we can decide whether tickets or performers should join it. The next addition should introduce a relationship worth handling, rather than merely making the table look fuller.
