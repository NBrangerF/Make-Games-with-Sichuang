# Why Does Adding a Score Change the Game?

In our river game, staying on the starting bank to collect planks can help the other animal move. That action earns no points of its own, but it can help both players win together. Now consider a separate little example. If an activity begins without winners and later gets a scoring rule, why might people start doing different things?

Imagine Lin and Yu each having six paper tiles: three showing woodland and three showing ponds. Each player arranges their own tiles in two rows of three, with no overlaps, to make a garden they like. Filling the rectangle finishes the activity. There is no ranking.

Lin alternates the tiles. His top row is wood, pond, wood; his bottom row is pond, wood, pond. He likes the idea of seeing different scenery wherever he walks. Yu puts the woodland in one complete row and the ponds in the other. She likes having two distinct areas. Both arrangements follow the rules.

## One new rule gives them a new goal

Next, they agree to compare scores. Each pair of matching tiles that shares an edge earns one point. Touching only at a corner does not count, and each pair is counted once. They compare scores after placing all six tiles. The higher score wins; equal scores share the win.

Yu's woodland row has two matching pairs, and her pond row has another two: four points altogether. Every tile beside one of Lin's tiles is the other kind, so he scores zero. Zero tells us that no matching tiles share an edge. It does not tell us his garden looks bad.

Before, Lin might move a tile because he preferred how it looked in the new place. Now, if he wants to win, he has a reason to put matching tiles together. The pieces are the same, and so is the movement of his hand. But “Why put it there?” has another answer: it earns points.

A scoring rule does this before anyone receives the final score. It tells players which results will help them in this particular contest.

## A goal can give someone a reason to try

Points need not spoil the activity. Imagine another friend who looks at the six tiles and has no idea where to begin. Joining matching tiles gives her a clear challenge. She can start with two points and then see whether she can reach four. Finding a better arrangement may be enjoyable in itself.

Lin may still prefer his alternating garden. He might also enjoy trying for a high score and competing with Yu. Or he might keep his original arrangement and accept that it will not win. The same scoring rule does not make everyone enjoy the same thing.

So, if we see Lin move another tile, we should not decide for him that the activity has become more fun. He may be exploring an interesting new arrangement, or he may simply want to win. Hearing his own explanation would help us understand which account fits.

## Improving and beating a friend are different results

Suppose Lin next makes a top row of wood, wood, pond and a bottom row of pond, pond, wood. Each row contains one matching pair, and no column does: two points altogether. That exceeds his previous zero while remaining below Yu’s four. The same two points can be personal progress and a trailing score in this contest.

If the activity aims at discovering new arrangements, preserving an earlier layout provides a personal reference. Comparing who leads requires both scores to follow the same conditions. Changing the reference changes what someone is pursuing. It neither changes how the two points are counted nor guarantees that everyone likes a particular comparison.

Trying again also takes time. One person may rearrange the whole garden for another matching pair; another may prefer to keep the current picture to show a friend. Counting only whether they make another attempt would miss these reasons. A valued arrangement need not rank higher.

## A score can leave out something that matters

These points do not measure whether a garden has paths, or whether anyone would like to walk through it. If the designer calls this a contest to find the most beautiful garden, four points and zero points cannot answer that question. They compare only the number of matching pairs along shared edges.

Adding points for paths, changing scenery, and many other things may not help. Every extra condition gives players more to remember. It is useful to make the promise clear first: “This round, we are competing to join matching tiles.” People can understand the contest before deciding whether they want to take part.

Return to the river game for a moment. Suppose we add one point for every collect action and finish by comparing individual scores. Collecting planks would then serve another purpose besides helping both animals cross. Someone might stay on the bank to earn personal points. Whether players would do that depends on their wishes and the rest of the revised rules. But the designer can already see a possible conflict between the new goal and getting both animals across in time.

Before adding points, it helps to finish this sentence: “The thing I want players to do more often is…” Then look at whether doing that thing helps the game reach its original goal.
