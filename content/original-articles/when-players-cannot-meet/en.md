# Can a Turn Wait Until Your Friend Comes Back Tomorrow?

You are free at lunchtime; your friend arrives after school. What must a game leave on the table so the next person can continue? A two-person postcard display lets us examine the reminders needed for past actions, current choices, and stopping.

## A row of spaces preserves two people’s choices

Imagine Lin and Yu making a postcard display in an activity room. Both want to participate, but often visit at different times. This independent example explains a handover; it has no score or winner.

The noticeboard has six spaces in a row, labeled Lin, Yu, Lin, Yu, Lin, Yu. Eight different postcards sit beside them, visible to both players. The name on the leftmost empty space determines whose turn it is.

Lin first chooses one of the eight cards and places it in the first space. Yu’s name is on the next space, so the display waits for Yu to return. Each turn places one card. Placed cards cannot be replaced or rearranged, and candidates cannot be reserved for a future turn. Filling the sixth space completes the display; neither player acts again.

A returning player need not find their friend for an explanation. Placed cards show past choices, the remaining cards show current options, and the name on the next empty space identifies the current player.

## Ten visits do not create ten turns

Suppose Lin passes daily while Yu visits every few days. Lin cannot fill later spaces just because he is nearby. When the leftmost empty space belongs to Yu, Lin may look at the display but cannot choose on Yu’s behalf.

If both continue and fill all six spaces, each selects exactly three cards. More visits do not create more opportunities. Yet Lin can still take a card both people like on the first turn. Equal turn counts do not give them identical choices.

Changing the rule to “Anyone passing may place a card” could let a frequent visitor complete most of the display. That might suit an open shared decorating activity, but it differs from preserving three choices per person. Knowing whose opportunities we intend to protect helps explain the waiting.

## Waiting moves into people’s schedules

When people can act at different times, play is asynchronous. This arrangement works because the information needed to choose, the action order, and previous results stay on the board. Yu still has her own decision on returning; Lin need not choose for her in advance.

Our display preserves turns without advancing itself. It has no countdown or automatic skip. If Yu has not returned, Yu’s space remains empty. The wait can last much longer than a wait at the table, while both people get on with other things.

Either person can stop before all the spaces are filled by marking the display “Finished, incomplete.” Both then stop acting. Leaving is not a loss and requires no approval from the other player. The ending needs to be visible alongside the preserved opportunity, so voluntary participation does not become an indefinitely owed turn.

## What does the returning person want to add?

Suppose Yu returns to find that Lin chose a seaside photograph. She might add a mountain picture to suggest a journey, or choose something simply for its color. What survives is an opportunity to choose for her own reasons, rather than a puzzle she owes somebody an answer to. People can respond to what the other has left without being present together.

A later decision to remove the display on Friday would introduce a new condition. They could announce a date when the activity ends even if the wall is incomplete, accepting a partial display. Letting the other person fill missing spaces near that deadline would instead change who has authority to choose and could not silently count as the original rule. Adjusting a schedule needs attention to both completion and personal opportunities.

## The position must survive the wait

This arrangement needs somewhere to keep the position. If the noticeboard is needed during a room cleanup, the players should pause and move the display, including its remaining candidates, somewhere it can stay. They continue after restoring that same position. A photograph can help record the arrangement, but does not place the next physical card for them.

Both people also need access to the board. Acting at different times addresses conflicting schedules without also solving the problem of living far apart. Playing with a distant friend would need another way for both people to see and change the same position.

What matters in this example is a clear handover. A returning friend can understand the previous move, find the choice that belongs to them now, and see how to stop. A turn can survive a lunch break or several days, provided those details do not disappear with the person who leaves.
