# What becomes possible when you see the dice first?

In C, players see two results before selecting one. This order allows the decision to respond to the position. If the results were hidden and players had to nominate the left or right die first, an operation would remain, but it could not use unseen numbers to avoid a snake.

Try changing only the order: choose a die, then reveal its number. The same board and dice now ask the player to make a different kind of decision.

## Once you see it, can you still change your mind?

Imagine a pawn at 6. C reveals 2 and 6 before selection. The player can choose 2, reaching 8 and then 17, or choose 6 and stop at 12. Results are known and the decision is still open, so the information can guide it.

Try a separate experiment: nominate the left die, then reveal both, with no switching allowed. If the left shows 6 and the right shows 2, the player can see the number that would reach the ladder but must move to 12. Compared with choice version C, the missing opportunity is choosing after seeing the results.

Information also needs to be understood. Seeing a 2 without recognising the ladder at 8 supplies a number without the complete consequence. Making the board legible and changing random-event timing address different stages.

## Chance can sit in different parts of a sequence

B’s single die directly determines this turn’s movement. C places two random outcomes before selection, allowing one to be adopted. R is a separate experiment: see one die, then decide whether to spend a limited token for an unknown replacement that must be accepted.

Draw the turn sequence for each approach. Circle “see the result” and “make the decision.” Their positions help explain how a game uses chance more clearly than saying it mostly depends on luck.

Seeing results first puts attention on comparison. Committing first gives you a result to wait for. Which moment do you want players to enjoy? Write that intention beside the sequence and return to it during playtesting.

## At 23, calculate just your next turn

Assume fair, independent six-sided dice. In B, a pawn at 23 reaches 24 only on a 1. Results 2–6 overshoot and leave it in place. The probability of finishing on its next turn is therefore 1/6.

In C at the same position, at least one 1 allows the player to select a winning result. The probability that neither die shows 1 is 5/6 × 5/6, or 25/36. Subtracting this from 1 gives 11/36 for at least one 1.

Simply adding 1/6 and 1/6 double-counts the case in which both dice show 1. The 11/36 result also is not C’s whole-game winning probability. We fixed a position and examined one player’s next turn, without considering whether the opponent finishes first.

## Put the number back on the board

The 11/36 result tells us that the choice version is more likely to offer a usable number at 23. Now consider how often pawns reach 23, how many setbacks they have already had, and whether the opponent could finish next turn. Those circumstances affect how much this change matters across a game.

A different random device may also require different mathematics. Drawing two numbered cards without replacement is not the same experiment as rolling two independent dice. The 11/36 result cannot simply be transferred.

Label 11/36 in your notes: “at 23, on this player’s next turn.” When you change the dice, try cards, or discuss the length of a game, that label will remind you which assumptions need checking again.
