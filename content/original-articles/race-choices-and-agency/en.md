# Do two numbers always create a worthwhile decision?

Both dice show 4. Which would you choose? In choice version C, either die sends the pawn to the same place. Now put the pawn at 23 and change the dice to 2 and 6. Both overshoot, so either choice leaves it still. Two options sometimes give you little to compare.

At other times, a small decision changes what happens next. This is a useful starting point for agency: what can I change, and do I understand how my choice makes that happen?

## Fix the position and compare final outcomes

Imagine a pawn at 6 with results of 2 and 6. Choosing 2 reaches 8 and climbs to 17. Choosing 6 stops at 12. Comparing number size suggests one intuition; following resolution exposes another. A player who can explain the ladder is connecting a decision to its consequence.

We can see that one result ends at 17 and the other at 12. To judge which position is more likely to win, we would need to consider later rolls, connections, and the opponent. Progress on this move and the chance of winning the game are questions at different scales.

At 20, results of 2 and 4 provide a clearer contrast. The 2 lands at 22 and slides to 12; the 4 reaches 24 and wins. If the current objective is to win this game, the rules directly support choosing 4. Such clear tasks help explain the mechanism, although many immediately obvious answers may require little deliberation.

## No difference, or a difference the player missed?

Check three things in order. What operations are legal? Do they leave different states? Can the player recognise that difference and act according to a goal?

At 23, the 2 and 6 leave the same position. At 6, their results differ, but the player might miss the ladder. The first case asks us to examine the choice itself. The second asks whether the board explains the difference clearly enough.

Someone pointing out the best die can also improve a pawn’s movement while taking ownership of the decision. Helping read numbers, explaining a rule, and selecting on another person’s behalf deserve separate observation. The final move alone cannot distinguish them.

## An easy choice can still have a purpose

Someone learning to read the board might enjoy spotting a ladder and choosing to use it. With practice, that decision gets quicker. For someone who wants to chat while rolling, base version B may offer a more comfortable pace.

The choice version does not need to puzzle people every turn. Watch the first time someone spots the ladder, then see whether they still want to compare results once the rules are familiar. A decision that helps someone learn may not keep the same person interested indefinitely.

Evaluation therefore needs more than a count of added choices. Ask what those choices change, how much attention they demand, and whether they disturb a rhythm the player previously enjoyed.

## Listen to explanations as well as recording selections

Offer two constructed positions: at 6 with 2 and 6, and at 23 with the same results. Let the participant finish each task before asking, “What did you compare?” Note whether they mention numbers, the ladder, the finish, or a belief that the dice should be added.

These positions do not estimate the proportion of worthwhile decisions in a game. They were deliberately selected to distinguish mechanisms, not randomly sampled. Understanding how often a difference appears during ordinary play requires a separate record of naturally occurring turns.

After the task, sort your notes into choices with matching results and choices with different results. Then look at the player’s account. Was there little to compare, a difference they missed, or a comparison they understood but did not want to spend time on? Each answer suggests a different revision.
