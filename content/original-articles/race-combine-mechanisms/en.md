# Do two useful ideas become better together?

“Choosing a die works. Rerolling works. Let’s use both.” Put the sheets for choice version C and reroll version R side by side and the suggestion is tempting. Then the two dice land, and you meet a new question: which one can you reroll?

## Construct one turn before combining rules

Place the pawn on 6 with results of 2 and 6. In C, choosing 2 reaches 8 and climbs to 17; choosing 6 reaches 12. If rerolls are added, must the player commit to one die before spending a token, or may they reroll first and choose afterward?

These procedures have different consequences. Committing first can sacrifice an already useful result. Keeping one die while rerolling another may preserve a fallback while seeking something better. The same token price does not create the same exposure to loss.

Pause the draft at that step. Does one payment reroll one die or both? Can the untouched die still be chosen? Until those answers are written, keep the combination as a proposal to finish.

## One rule changes the function of another

R has one die: spending a token means accepting its replacement. C offers two known alternatives. Combining them could make a token's value depend on the other die, while adding the need to remember which choice has already been committed.

The cost is therefore more than another sentence and a few counters. Ask which existing decisions become easier, which exceptions appear, and which intermediate states players must remember. Trace information, payment, commitment, and movement in order, including whether any step can be reversed.

A combination may work smoothly while quietly removing a previously important risk. That need not be undesirable, but it should be an intentional experience rather than an unnoticed side effect.

## See how another game connects decisions

In Eagle-Gryphon's 2021 rules for Can't Stop, pairing four dice connects to choosing whether to stop and preserve the turn's progress. Page 1 allows a pairing that advances only one column; page 2 distinguishes banking progress from losing the current turn's gains.

Consider the two connected decisions: how to allocate the dice, and when to secure this turn’s progress. The gains are at risk until the player chooses to stop. That gives the next roll something to put in jeopardy.

In our small race, the current aim is to help someone understand two destinations. Pairing four dice and tracking temporary progress would bring another set of interesting questions, along with more explanation. Keep them in your notes while this draft concentrates on its smaller decision.

## Removing a rule can finish the design

The handoff will use choice version C: roll two dice, choose one, move once, resolve the landing, and win on exactly 24. Put the reroll tokens, supply space, and unfinished combination with the other drafts. The person receiving this version has one selection to follow.

When saving the combination, name the step that caused trouble instead of writing only “too complicated.” For example: “Keeping a useful die means rerolling gives up little about the current destination.” If you later want a more forgiving retry system, that change might suit it.

Trace a complete turn in your own combined design, then temporarily remove one rule. Check whether the central decision remains and whether its explanation becomes clearer. If the intended experience disappears, you have a concrete reason to restore the rule and can describe the work it does.
