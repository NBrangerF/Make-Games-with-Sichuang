# What Should the Next Draft Change First?

The previous chapter separated three hypothetical records: both animals moved in round 1 and later lacked planks; Lin collected in two consecutive rounds and left Rabbit too little time; and one first reading treated collecting as taking back spent planks. These are teaching examples for an imagined v0.2, not completed tests with real players.

We now have three suggestions: add planks, add a round, or clarify where collected planks come from. Which should the next draft address first? The loudest suggestion, easiest change, or most exciting idea cannot decide on its own. Start with the question this round is meant to resolve.

## Connect the change to the current goal

Suppose the goal is “help first-time players run the base game correctly, then observe how they plan together.” An unclear source for collected planks interferes with that later observation. If players return spent planks, they are using a different resource system. Their later success or failure cannot tell us how understandable the base game’s planning is.

We can therefore make a materials version, v0.3. Clearly separate “Spare pieces,” “Available planks,” and “Spent planks” on the table. Write collecting as “Take two new planks from the spare area and add them to the center.” State with movement that spent planks cannot be reused. These changes belong together because players consult both the map and the action reminder. They clarify one relationship while leaving collection quantity, movement cost, and the four-round deadline unchanged.

That is a reasoned priority, not a finding that the revised materials already work. We still need to observe their use by first-time players. Previous participants can compare materials, but after hearing an explanation they cannot provide another entirely unfamiliar first reading.

## One complaint can lead to different changes

“There aren’t enough planks” can be accurate. After both animals move in round 1, two planks remain while four moves are still needed. Raising the starting supply from four to six would let both animals move every round and arrive without collecting. That version might be worth trying, but it changes what players need to discuss. It does not replace clarifying where planks go.

“Give us another round” can also rescue some plans. An animal that collects twice has only two turns left for movement, but needs three moves to cross. A fifth round gives it another opportunity to move. Yet “completion becomes possible” and “the extra round is more enjoyable” are different conclusions. We can calculate the first; the second involves how people experience waiting and planning.

Connecting a proposed fix to the situation that prompted it lets us take the problem seriously while examining the solution. Deferring an extra round does not deny that the player encountered a shortage of time.

## Separate definite errors from directions to explore

Some questions do not need a vote. If we accidentally set a three-round deadline, both players together have only six turns. Reaching the far bank already requires six moves, while the starting four planks require at least one additional collect action. Those actions cannot fit into six turns, making success impossible. We can correct that contradiction through the rules themselves.

Other proposals change direction. Weather adds uncertainty to respond to. A shortcut adds route comparisons. A personal reward for arriving first combines a shared crossing with individual competition. Each might have value, but each introduces questions beyond whether players understand collecting. Keeping them in separate proposed versions preserves the purpose of the current revision.

We do not always need the smallest possible change. If several rules together produce an unwanted pattern, an entire part may need redesigning. State which relationships change together and why. Small changes can be easier to interpret, but that should not become a reason to leave a problem unresolved.

For this revision, the reason is clear: align wording and placement so that later choices actually occur under the base rules. Compare extra planks, rounds, and mechanisms separately. Next, give a consistent set of complete materials to someone new and see whether it supports play from setup to ending without the designer supplying answers in advance.
