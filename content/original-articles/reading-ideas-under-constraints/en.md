# How Can One Direction Lead to Several Ideas?

Our provisional direction is a paper game in which two people cooperate to cross a river. Each controls their own actions while considering their partner. But “make players cooperate” does not tell us what someone can actually do on a turn. We need to bring that direction closer to the table.

Start with a particular moment. Rabbit is on the first stepping stone; Hedgehog is still on the bank. What could Hedgehog do that would affect Rabbit’s situation? Hedgehog might obtain planks both animals can use, pass something along, or operate a bridge from the bank. These are only candidate ideas. We have not decided who can receive an item or how the bridge works, so none of them should silently enter the base game.

Imagining several actions for the same moment helps us avoid treating our first answer as the only answer. Then we can examine what each proposal would require.

## Expand from something you already have

An idea does not have to begin with a complete story. With paper pieces in hand, we can ask how players might move, turn over, or share them. With a river setting, we can ask what needs preparing before a crossing. If we want two people to affect each other, we can ask what one person could do to change the other’s choices. These starting points can lead toward the same design problem without requiring a whole fictional world first.

Changing names alone may leave the play unchanged. If “collecting planks” becomes “gathering magical leaves,” but the conditions, quantities, and uses stay the same, players still face the same decisions. A new name may help communicate the theme. To find a different way of cooperating, we need to change an action or its consequences too.

We can also keep an idea before every detail is settled. “One animal prepares on the bank while the other moves ahead” already suggests a useful relationship. It does not immediately need weather, items, special powers, and three kinds of scoring. Adding all those things at once would make the original relationship harder to examine.

## When should you start choosing?

During idea generation, demanding every detail too early can eliminate everything for being unfinished. Yet collecting more ideas indefinitely will not produce a playable game either. When we are ready to make the first paper draft, we can compare candidates against our provisional direction.

Consider shared plank collection and operating a bridge. Collection needs available planks, spent planks, and conditions for obtaining new ones. A bridge needs rules for when it opens, who can cross, whether it closes again, and whether the operator can leave. Those questions might have interesting answers. If our immediate question is simply “who stays behind to prepare?”, collecting already makes that relationship visible with fewer things to explain. That makes it a useful first candidate.

Fewer rules are not always better. If our goal were coordinating a bridge’s opening and closing, those details would be the very things we needed to explore. The useful scope depends on the question, not merely the number of rules.

## Give the chosen idea details people can carry out

So we start with collecting: an animal on the bank obtains planks that either animal can use. This directly connects an individual action to shared progress. Now we must ask: can animals collect only on the bank? How many planks do they get? Can they move afterward? Do spent planks return? Different answers create different games.

The base game chooses these answers: collect only on the starting bank, obtain two new planks, use the whole turn to collect, and never reuse spent planks. These conditions make preparation helpful while giving it a cost in time. Unlimited free collection would create a different planning problem.

The other ideas can remain candidates for later comparison. They do not have to become extra rules in the next chapter. For now, we carry forward one relationship: how can we write collecting and moving so precisely that another person can perform them and the paper animals can begin their crossing?
