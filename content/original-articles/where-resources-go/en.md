# How Can Spending Water Leave Me With More?

At the river, adding planks used an action, and the rounds remaining determined whether they could be used in time. This optional reading moves to an independent example. What if spending something you have buys a tool that keeps producing more later?

Imagine managing a water collector on paper. Water and collectors are game objects here, not a calculation of real weather. We will follow four rounds, then identify the rule that lets production grow.

## Spend Three Now to Gain Two More Next Round

You start with one collector and no water. There are four rounds. First, collect two drops per collector. Then you may buy a new collector for three drops, with at most one purchase per round. A new collector starts producing next round. After round four, only remaining water scores; collectors have no score. There are no other costs or other players affecting you.

Buy nothing and you gain two drops per round, finishing with eight.

Now consider buying exactly once, in round two. In round one you collect two drops, not enough to buy. Round two adds another two, making four. Spend three on a new collector. Only one drop remains, but you now own two collectors.

In round three, each collector produces two, adding four to the one you kept. You have five. Round four adds four more, leaving nine—one more than buying nothing. This sequence makes no further purchases, so we can follow the effect of that one payment.

Looking only at the end of round two, one drop seems much worse than four. But the three spent have bought the ability to collect two additional drops each later round. Understanding your position requires looking at both your water and your collectors.

## More Water Can Buy More Collectors

We chose to buy just once, but the rules allow later purchases. If new water buys another collector, which produces more water, the change comes back around and strengthens the original growth. This relationship is called **positive feedback**.

“Positive” describes reinforcement, not a judgment that the result must be good. A single collector producing two drops per round also increases your water, but having more water does not make that collector faster. The ability to turn water into additional production creates the possibility of reinforcement here.

Preparation also needs time to pay back. Change only the ending to round three. The one-purchase sequence finishes with five drops, while buying nothing produces six. The extra round that would have brought the advantage is gone. This connects to the river: something can offer a future benefit without having time to deliver it.

## What If More Water Also Means More Evaporation?

Consider a separate variation. Keep the initial collector and choose not to buy any others. After collecting each round, lose one drop for every complete group of three in the current supply. This represents evaporation. Calculate the loss once only; do not repeatedly apply it to what remains.

Round one produces two, so there is no complete group of three and no loss. In round two, the total rises to four. One complete group means losing one, leaving three. Round three raises the total to five; lose one again, leaving four. Round four raises it to six. Two complete groups mean losing two, bringing it back to four.

A larger supply now causes a larger loss, which acts against the increase. Under these conditions, the amount settles at four. This opposing relationship is called **negative feedback**. “Negative” does not mean bad; it describes how the rule responds to change.

Suppose the rule instead removed exactly one drop each round. You would have less water, but the loss would not change with the amount stored. Removing something is not enough to make a rule negative feedback. Look at how the loss responds to the quantity, beyond whether a minus sign appears.

These changes also point to different places a designer can revise. If the game ends before a new collector can help, consider when its production starts or how many rounds remain. If keeping water is too easy, examine how stored water affects loss. Increasing every number at once changes prices, returns, and evaporation together, making it harder to see which relationship addresses the problem.

## Find the Particular Relationship Again

Positive and negative feedback name relationships; they do not choose which game people will enjoy. Continued growth might suit the feeling of preparation finally paying off. Evaporation might introduce a cost to leaving water unused when the intention is to make people consider when to spend. We still need to understand how players read and use the rules.

Next time you see a pile of resources, follow it for a while. What did the things spent buy? When does that purchase start helping? Does having more support another increase, or cause a larger loss? Following that return path explains more than simply saying the resources keep growing.
