# What makes “choose a die” a complete action?

In C, Lin’s pawn is at 6 and the two dice show 2 and 6. “I choose 2,” Lin says. If the description stops there, another player might still wonder which die is set aside, how many movements happen, or when the turn passes.

After “I choose 2,” the pawn still has to move and the turn has to pass. Follow that sentence to its end and you can see how much an action description needs to explain.

## Write from the roll to the next player

C is the separate choice version. On your turn, roll two ordinary six-sided dice together, select one result, move once, and ignore the other die. There are no reroll tokens, and the results are not added. Two 2s still mean one movement using a 2.

Lin can push the unused 6 aside to show the choice. The pawn advances from 6 to 8, climbs to 17, and the turn passes to Yu. Moving the unused die is a way to communicate a decision. It is not an extra game action and does not consume the die.

Both dice are rolled again next turn. The unused 6 cannot be saved. Saving it would introduce a new state requiring storage limits and timing rules. Such an experiment needs its own version.

## Separate decisions from required execution

Rolling produces results. Selection determines which result applies. Movement and connections then follow the rules. All contribute to a turn, but they are not all strategic decisions. B removes selection and still has a complete sequence of play.

C lets a player choose an available number. It does not let them refuse a snake, stop partway, or use both dice. When someone says, “I want to avoid that snake,” translate the wish into a legal action: which result, which landing, and why that landing avoids the connection? Naming an intention does not make it achievable.

An action description also needs the final consequence. Space 8 is an intermediate state; 17 is the position the move leaves behind. Analysing only the number 2 misses what the board does with it.

## See how another race organises dice

Classic Monza, HABA game 4416, provides another form of organisation after a roll. Players roll six coloured dice and determine an order and legal forward route that match the track. Each used die supplies a corresponding step. The rules require moving as far as possible; players cannot simply discard usable colours to stop early.

The comparison is specific. C selects one outcome from two dice. Monza arranges several colour results into a movement sequence. Calling both “roll-and-move” does not explain what the player actually organises.

Think of the dice as materials with different jobs. One numbered die can give a distance; several colour dice can invite someone to arrange a sequence. Borrowing the second approach also means considering which routes the board allows.

## Hand one instruction to someone else

Give another person a fixed task: “The pawn is at 6 and the dice show 2 and 6. Complete a turn in C.” Watch for adding the dice, following a ladder merely passed, using both results, or uncertainty about changing players. This checks the clarity of the process, not whole-game strategic skill.

Circle the step that needed a reminder. If the player can choose a die but does not know when to pass the turn after climbing the ladder, add “end your turn” there. A specific revision gives you a specific place to watch at the next reading.
