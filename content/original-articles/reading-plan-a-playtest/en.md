# What Should You Arrange Before Inviting Friends to Playtest?

Putting the crossing draft in front of friends and asking “Is it fun?” may produce an equally broad answer: “It’s okay.” Even if they play carefully, you may not know whether they mean the animals are appealing, the actions are clear, or discussing plans was enjoyable.

A playtest does not have to answer everything. Suppose our question this time is: can first-time players distinguish spare, available, and spent planks and collect correctly? Who joins, which materials they receive, and what we say at the start should all help us examine that question.

## Who plays affects what we can learn

A friend who knows the previous draft can compare how changes feel, but already knows that collecting produces new planks. Their lack of questions cannot by itself show that the new explanation is clear. Someone encountering this game for the first time is better placed to reveal difficulties with a first reading.

First-time players are not all alike either. A frequent tabletop player may look for an action reminder immediately; someone else may need longer to read a paragraph. If our intended setting is two beginners playing during a short break, we should try to approach those conditions of group size, experience, and use. Inviting only people who are especially skilled at explaining rules can hide difficulties other readers face.

Explain that this is a paper game still being revised, and that you want to find places where its materials need improvement. Participants do not need to prove they are good players. Tell them roughly how long the session is expected to take and that they may ask for help, pause, or stop. Joining should be their choice, not an obligation to help a friend.

## Which version will they receive, and how will it begin?

Gather one consistent set: complete base rules, the four-space map, two animals, twenty planks, round and turn reminders, and separate areas for spare, available, and spent pieces. Start with four available planks, sixteen spare pieces, and an empty spent area. Leave undecided weather and shortcut ideas out of this base version.

We can call this draft v0.2 to distinguish its materials. The number does not mean it has passed a test. Rules, map, and action reminder must agree. If one says “take new planks” while another says “take planks back,” participants receive contradictory instructions.

If we want to know whether the materials explain themselves, let participants prepare and read from them before giving the answer about planks. An opening could be: “I want to see whether these instructions are clear enough. You can ask me if you get stuck, and you can stop whenever you want.” If the question is instead how informed players divide collecting, teaching the rules first makes more sense. The way we host should fit the question.

## How can the host help and keep a useful record?

Watch where pieces are taken from and placed, and which questions arise. A short note should let someone later identify the version, situation, specific action, and words spoken. “With three available planks, Yu pointed at the spent area and asked whether she could take one back” is easier to investigate than “She played incorrectly.”

If she asks for help, explain. Record when the explanation happened and what it supplied, so later events can be understood in light of that help. Trying a temporary rule change to explore the rest of the game can also be useful, provided participants know the plan and the point of change is marked. The two versions should not be treated as one unchanged test.

Participants may want to stop. Materials may be missing, or a rule may leave no way to continue. Any of these can end the attempt. A session that never reaches round 4 can still reveal a preparation problem. Forcing it to finish can make the designer’s improvised additions look like abilities the original draft already had.

A well-arranged playtest may leave us with just one clear question. That is useful progress. Next we will slow down one imagined question about planks and separate what happened at the table from our explanations of why it happened.
