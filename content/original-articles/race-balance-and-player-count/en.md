# Do identical rules provide identical opportunities?

Both pawns are on 23, and both upcoming rolls contain a 1. Who wins in choice version C? Whoever acts next. The other person never gets to choose that 1. This arranged position is simple, but makes a useful point: identical boards and dice can offer different opportunities because of turn order.

## Name what you want to balance

Balance might mean equal action opportunities, similar winning chances, continued involvement while behind, or several worthwhile approaches. These are related but different goals. Identical dice do not establish identical winning chances, and compensation does not make every choice meaningful.

To compare starting positions, record rolls, choices, and pawn positions alongside the winner. Looking back through the turns helps distinguish a missing final opportunity from earlier setbacks on snakes.

Randomizing the starting player distributes access to that seat before play. It does not remove any advantage the seat may have within a game. Alternating starters across games distributes the role, but does not promise that two outcomes cancel each other out.

## Catch-up support needs precise conditions

Consider an unimplemented idea: give the trailing player another option. How is trailing measured: current number, distance to the finish, or chance of winning next turn? Space 8 immediately transfers to 17, while 22 transfers to 12. Comparing unresolved positions can misidentify the state.

Timing also matters. Checking eligibility at the start of a turn differs from checking after movement. An extra die, a reroll, and automatic advancement would change different relationships. None is added to C here; these are questions the proposal must resolve.

Listen to what the trailing player wants. A better chance of winning, or something to do while others act? The first question concerns winning chances; the second concerns attention during a wait. Helping a pawn catch up may not address both.

## More players means more than another pawn

A separate three-player C experiment can keep one pawn each, alternating two-dice choices, shared spaces, and immediate victory on exactly 24. The procedure still operates. However, two opponents now act between your turns instead of one, and more people can approach the finish and end the game.

During a three-player test, follow one person between two of their turns. How long do they wait? Can they follow the other pawns? Do they remember what they intended when their turn returns? This shows which part of play the extra person changes.

Resource totals need similar care. In the separate R experiment, two players with two reroll tokens each create four tokens in total. A three-player R trial with the same personal allocation creates six. The table's total grows, but nobody gains more personal rerolls. This remains R, without sharing or transfers, rather than an addition to C.

## Try seating a third person

Keep the final candidate at two-player C. If testing with three people, record that count and examine waiting, endings, and explanation demands separately. Changing player count, adding rerolls, and moving snakes together would obscure the cause of any different response.

For a proposed adjustment, name its beneficiary, timing, and intended improvement. Asking whether both players can explain their remaining opportunities after swapping seats produces a more tractable observation than simply asking whether the game is fair.

Make the two-player rules clear, then label the three-player draft separately. When friends sit down, you will know what to watch: how the extra person changes the time between each player’s decisions.
