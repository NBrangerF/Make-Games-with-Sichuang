# How Accurate Does This Prototype Need to Be?

We could already play the crossing game with paper pieces when we first wrote its actions. After examining more rules and ways of presenting them, we have more questions. Can both animals arrive in time? Might available and spent planks get confused? Can someone across the table read the action reminder? Drafts can help investigate all these questions, but their parts do not need equal attention.

A draft that lets people operate and examine a design is a prototype. Its fidelity is how closely it represents the intended play in the parts we care about. Fidelity is not a single ranking from rough to beautiful. An undecorated sheet can represent action order accurately; an attractive picture might be unable to run a game at all.

## What must this version preserve?

To check whether the base crossing can be completed in four rounds, we can start with written numbers and two moving animal pieces. Positions, a cost of one plank per move, collecting two only on the starting bank, one turn per player per round, and the four-round deadline must be accurate. Fur color does not affect this calculation.

To see how new players distinguish the planks, we need different details. Of twenty pieces, four are available and sixteen are spare, with another area for spent planks. The areas’ names, spacing, and handling are now part of what we are examining. If we arrange every piece for the players, we cannot also claim the materials explained the destinations on their own.

Now suppose the question is whether someone across the table can read “Collect only on the starting bank.” Enlarging the sentence to fill a page and holding it close might make it readable, but would not check the intended table reminder. We need the intended text size, position, and viewing distance. Unrelated decoration can still remain simple.

## How much can a partial draft tell us?

Sometimes we need only a short section. Put both animals on the starting bank with four available planks, then demonstrate one move and one collect action. This can reveal whether the instructions omit a plank’s destination. It does not show later choices or reach the deadline, so it cannot establish the whole game’s difficulty or appeal across repeated plays.

To examine how people plan who stays behind, we need the journey from setup to ending, allowing preparation and the deadline to matter. Starting from a nearly successful position would skip the early decision we most need to observe. How complete a prototype must be depends on how much of the process the question covers.

A partial check can still expose a definite omission. If collecting never specifies that the planks are new, the wording needs clarification. We need not organize ten complete games to establish that a sentence is missing. Whether the revised wording helps another person understand still requires observing its use.

## When do material differences change the result?

The base game has no secret information. Different marks on the backs of planks usually do not change our knowledge of the shared total. The earlier weather example is different. If a drawn card should remain secret but thin paper reveals “Rain” through its back, an unknown result has been disclosed early. An opaque back is then necessary for that situation to work, rather than an ornamental detail. Weather remains a separate comparison and is not added to the base game.

Likewise, to examine whether pieces are easy to pick up, use pieces close to the intended size and thickness. Replacing movement with spoken numbers would bypass the physical action. A future digital version that updates resources instantly might also have a different pace from moving each paper piece by hand. Checking rules on paper is useful without proving that digital interaction will feel the same.

Leave a sentence beside the prototype: “I want to see whether this person can do this task with these materials.” Make the parts that affect the answer, then stop building for now. Let an actual attempt show what needs attention next.
