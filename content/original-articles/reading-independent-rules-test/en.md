# Can the Materials Explain a Game Without You Speaking?

We have chosen to clarify where planks come from and where they go. Next, we can give the materials to someone new and examine whether they explain setup, actions, and the ending. A designer knows every detail and can easily assume that “everyone will know” an omitted step. Independent reading exposes what the designer has been supplying from memory.

First, here are the complete materials for this attempt. This is the base game’s v0.3 materials draft. The play is unchanged; the plank areas and action instructions have been clarified. It has not yet been playtested with people.

## Enough information to go from setup to ending

**Materials and setup.** Prepare one Rabbit piece, one Hedgehog piece, twenty paper planks, a round marker, and a turn reminder. Draw four connected spaces in order: “Starting bank—First stepping stone—Second stepping stone—Far bank.” Write rounds 1 to 4. Start the round marker on round 1 and the turn reminder pointing to Lin.

Lin controls Rabbit; Yu controls Hedgehog. Both animals begin on the starting bank. Draw three areas. Put four planks in the central “Available planks” area, sixteen in “Spare pieces,” and none in “Spent planks.” Spare pieces represent new planks not yet collected and cannot directly pay for movement. Spent planks cannot be used again.

**Goal and order.** Players cooperate and win only when both animals reach the far bank. There are no personal scores. Play for up to four rounds, with one turn for Lin followed by one turn for Yu in each round. Players may discuss plans, but each decides their own animal’s action.

**On a turn.** Choose one permitted action: move, collect, or wait. To move, at least one available plank must remain. Spend one by putting it in the spent area, then move your animal to the next adjacent space toward the far bank. You cannot move backward or skip a space. Only an animal on the starting bank may collect: take two new planks from the spare area and add them to the center. The animal stays still and cannot also move this turn. Waiting leaves animals and planks unchanged and ends the turn. Animals may share spaces and do not block each other. An animal on the far bank can only wait on later turns.

**Ending and rounds.** After each action, if both animals have arrived, players win together immediately. Otherwise, after Lin’s turn, pass to Yu. After Yu’s turn, if this was not round 4, advance the round marker and return to Lin. If both turns of round 4 finish without both animals arriving, players lose together. There is no weather, shortcut, borrowing, or special ability.

Twenty pieces are sufficient. Four rounds contain eight turns, so even collecting every turn requires only sixteen spare pieces. These materials can represent every base-game situation without finding extra pieces during play.

## Provide complete materials before examining the gaps

Before this kind of test, participants should know that the draft is being revised and that they can ask for help, pause, or stop at any time. Explain that before play; do not bury it among the game’s victory rules. Participants are not required to remain silent for the test.

Then let them set up and read. The designer can watch where they look, how they interpret actions, and when they cannot continue. If the host sorts all three piles and explains “Take only from the spare area” first, the session has not examined whether the materials communicate those steps independently.

Imagine a reader placing all twenty planks in the center. They may have missed the four-plank setup or understood “spare” as something immediately available to spend. This reveals a setup question, not evidence that four starting planks are already too many. Ask which wording led to that arrangement, then provide help.

If play continues after help, record where it happened. The rest of the session can still inform our understanding of the game; the whole game simply cannot be described as completed entirely from the original materials. If a missing rule prevents continuation, the attempt can stop and be arranged again after revision. Failing to reach the ending may itself reveal something useful.

## What remains after the game runs independently?

If players set up, act, and determine the ending independently, the materials supported those tasks on that occasion. This does not establish that every beginner will understand them or that the game invites repeated play. Someone may understand quickly but find the planning repetitive; someone else may enjoy planning while needing larger text. Those questions need separate attention.

When players begin discussing who should stay behind instead of only asking where planks come from, we have an opportunity to examine how they use the rules. Yet the next collaborator still needs to know which version they receive, what changed, and what has actually been checked. The final chapter organizes this draft so another person can take it forward accurately.
