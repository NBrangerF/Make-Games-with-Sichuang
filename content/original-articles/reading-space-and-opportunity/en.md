# How Does Another Route Change the Choice?

In the previous chapter, Rabbit and Hedgehog needed both enough planks and enough time to cross together. The original stepping-stone route takes each animal three moves and three planks. Here is a new idea: could spending an extra plank let an animal take one fewer step?

## Two routes, two kinds of cost

For this chapter, add just one route to the base game: starting bank to island to far bank. Reaching the island and leaving it each take one move, costing two shared planks each time. The stepping-stone route stays unchanged. The routes meet only at the banks, so animals cannot switch halfway across. Movement remains forward only, and both animals may share any location. Neither blocks the other.

The choice at the starting bank now looks like this:

| Route | Moves needed | Total planks spent |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Starting bank → first stone → second stone → far bank | 3 | 3 |
| Starting bank → island → far bank | 2 | 4 |

The island route saves one action but spends one extra plank. Both numbers matter. Calling it faster can hide its extra demand on the shared supply. Calling the original route cheaper can hide the fact that each animal has only four chances to act.

## Where does the saved action go?

Compare two opening plans. Rabbit could collect once, then take the stepping stones. That uses all four of Rabbit’s chances: one collecting action and three moves. Collecting once and taking the island uses only three: one collecting action and two moves. Looking just at Rabbit, the island allows an earlier arrival.

But Rabbit does not have a private supply. Taking both animals along the original route costs six planks. Sending one by the island and the other by the stones costs seven. The four starting planks plus the two from one collecting action make only six. Saving Rabbit an action has not supplied the team’s missing seventh plank.

The route therefore brings back the previous chapter’s question: who will collect again, and can that animal still arrive in time? For example, Rabbit could collect in each of the first two rounds and take the island in the last two. Hedgehog could wait in the first round, then make its three moves along the stones. Under these rules, both reach the far bank in round four. The team has obtained eight planks and spent seven. If Rabbit collected twice but took the original route, it would have only two chances left for a journey requiring three moves.

The extra route offers a specific possibility: an animal that collects twice can still meet the deadline by spending more planks. That does not make the island worthwhile in every situation or show that the routes are equally strong. The useful comparison is whether the extra plank buys a step the team needs.

## Close in the picture is not always close in play

Suppose we draw the island beside the starting bank but keep the two-plank labels on its route. Its cost does not fall. Drawing the second stone closer to the far bank does not let Rabbit skip it. Permitted journeys depend on which places connect and what it costs to travel between them.

Those links are the map’s connections. A real-world map might use drawn distance to represent travel distance. This example uses one connection to represent one move. Nobody measures how far a piece slides across the paper.

The drawing still matters. A decorative ripple might look like a path, encouraging a newcomer to follow a connection that does not exist. Separating the routes clearly and placing each plank cost beside its link can help someone see the choice: two moves cost more planks; three moves cost more actions.

## Sharing a place does not mean competing for it

When Hedgehog reaches the first stone, it does not claim the stone. Rabbit may join it later. Neither the base game nor this chapter’s extra-route version limits a location to one animal. A small circle in the picture does not introduce a blocking rule.

The players still affect one another because they spend from the same pile. Rabbit’s two-plank move to the island leaves two fewer planks for Hedgehog. Hedgehog collecting at the bank adds planks Rabbit can use. The map shows their separate journeys; the shared supply connects the decisions they make along them.

If a later version allows only one animal on a stone, it needs further answers. Is the other animal forbidden to enter, or may it pay more planks to enter? Who must leave to make room? These answers change possible routes; a “1” drawn on the map would not explain them. The current rules explicitly allow sharing, so the calculations above contain no extra cost for occupying a place.

Save the island route separately and return to the original two stepping stones. Mark movement costs and action counts on both maps. A later route change can then be compared in terms of what it saves and what it costs.
