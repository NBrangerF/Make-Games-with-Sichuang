# Why Discuss a Plan When We Win Together?

Return to the basic crossing game. Lin chooses Rabbit’s actions, and Yu chooses Hedgehog’s. They share four planks in the center and need both animals to arrive. If both move forward in the first round, only two planks remain, while each animal still needs two moves. Both have left the start, so neither can collect. A loss is now unavoidable.

People pursuing the same result can still choose actions that fail to work together. A shared goal tells us where they hope to end up. How their actions connect requires another look.

## Helping need not mean moving forward

Here is the successful route we can calculate. In round one, Rabbit moves and Hedgehog stays at the start to collect two new planks, taking reserve paper pieces and adding them to the center. The supply falls from four to three, then rises to five. Both move in rounds two and three. In round four, Rabbit waits on the far bank while Hedgehog makes the final move.

Hedgehog arrives later, but the first action supplied what both animals needed for their three moves. Looking only at who leads would miss that contribution. The rules give no extra points for arriving first, so Yu has not sacrificed an individual victory.

Nor does this route make collecting Yu’s permanent job. Lin could collect in round one while Yu moves first. Both can then keep moving and finish in time. The two arrangements give different people the first supporting action. Choosing between them is something to discuss.

## Who decides how a shared resource is used?

“The planks belong to everyone” does not automatically say who can act for whom. The basic game permits discussion while leaving each animal’s action to its player. Lin may point out a shortage and suggest leaving someone at the start. On Yu’s turn, she still chooses whether to collect, move, or wait, subject to the conditions for each action.

Suppose Lin says, “Could you collect this round? Then we have enough.” Yu might welcome the explanation, or she might want to know why both cannot move first. Counting the two planks they would later lack gives her a reason she can examine. If Lin takes the pieces and ends her turn without waiting for an answer, the position may follow a winning route while her decision has disappeared.

A cooperative design therefore needs to connect at least three things: which result belongs to everyone, who decides each action, and how people can exchange ideas. Writing only “work together” can leave the latter two to whoever speaks first.

## What does compulsory coordination solve and remove?

Consider one independent variation: Yu must collect in the first round; all later rules stay the same. This rules out the dead end where both animals move immediately and makes the opening easier to continue. But the designer has already chosen who stays on the bank. The players no longer make that part of the plan together.

If the purpose is to demonstrate a collect action to someone seeing the game for the first time, a fixed opening may help. If the purpose is to discuss how to share the work, it may remove the very choice we wanted. On returning to the basic game after the demonstration, explain that this compulsory opening no longer applies.

Another approach preserves the choices while making public information easier to understand, such as keeping the available planks easy to count. That can help people recognize a shared problem without guaranteeing that both want to speak. Familiarity with the rules, speed of speaking, and habits of listening also affect discussion. Giving somebody a secret or a special ability changes information and actions. A new label alone cannot establish that everybody now feels involved.

## What if a shared win also awards first place?

Consider another independent variation. Both animals must still arrive within four rounds; otherwise both players lose and there is no individual award. After a shared win, compare the rounds in which the animals arrived. The player whose animal arrived in an earlier round earns individual first place; arrivals in the same round share first place. All other rules remain. Along the route where Rabbit moves first and Hedgehog collects, Rabbit arrives in round three and Hedgehog in round four. Both succeed, and Lin also earns first place. Swapping the collector lets Yu arrive in round three and gain that additional result.

Collecting still supports the shared win while potentially leaving individual first place to the other person. Suppose Yu says, “I would like a chance to arrive first too.” She is responding to the added goal, rather than necessarily rejecting cooperation. They might agree to try the arrangements in different games, care mainly about finishing together, or discover that individual first place matters differently to each of them. The variation reveals how two goals can pull on the same action. Its rules cannot predict who will undermine a team. We now return to the basic game without individual places.

## Disagreement can belong to cooperation

Suppose both people want to collect. Lin wants to help first; Yu wants to keep an action at the bank for herself. They disagree about the arrangement, rather than one wanting to win and the other wanting to lose. They can explain their plans and check which routes still reach the far bank. The rules permit both arrangements; what matters is whether the later actions work together.

They may also want different things from this meeting. One wants to discover the solution; the other wants to finish quickly. Even perfectly correct calculations may be unwelcome if they give away every answer. That calls for returning to the intended setting: is this a demonstration, an attempt where hints are welcome, or a game where each thinks before discussing?

Ask each person to describe a decision they made, then compare the account with the resources and actions. These small passages show who proposed, decided, and carried out a move, and how work was divided behind the shared win.
