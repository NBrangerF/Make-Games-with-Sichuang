# What does one successful move tell us?

Imagine two attempts on a recording sheet. First, a player at space 6 chooses the die showing 6, moves to 12, and asks, “Should I have climbed when I passed 8?” You explain that the pawn must land at the ladder’s base. You set up the same position again. This time, the player chooses 2, moves to 8, and climbs to 17.

Both moves reached the correct position, but your explanation happened between them. A note saying only “the player understands ladders” would lose how that understanding came about.

## Find the remark worth following up

Choosing 6 is legal in choice version C. The useful clue is the question about passing 8: the player is still unsure when the ladder takes effect.

Several things might explain it. Perhaps the rule says “climb when you meet a ladder” without naming the landing space. Perhaps the ladder’s base is hard to see. The player might remember a different way of playing. Put these possibilities beside the record and look for clues in the materials. Keeping the original words and movements gives you something to check when your explanation changes.

## Your hint belongs in the record

You can describe the second attempt as “completed this position after an explanation.” That is useful: the explanation gave this person a way to continue. Try putting the sentence you spoke onto the rule card.

To check whether the card works on its own, give it to someone who has not heard the explanation. Ten more attempts by the original player may show increasing fluency, but cannot make the materials new to them again. Repetition can show learning; a new reader can test a first encounter.

An omission noticed by one person is still worth fixing. If the sheet never says “choose one,” add the instruction. Then ask someone to try it, so you can see whether the new wording is clear enough in use.

## When two games differ in length, revisit the turns

With identical rules, one game can climb several ladders while another keeps hitting snakes. Before interpreting a timing difference, check the versions, boards, starting player, and help given. Then follow the rolls and choices to find where the games diverged.

You can replay a dice sequence to check whether the same actions produce the same result. That is useful for reproducing a problem. To estimate an ordinary game’s length, you also need complete games with naturally rolled results.

Define the timing interval. Does it start with the explanation or the first roll? Are pauses included? If you did not measure, write “not timed.” Keep “it felt long” as the player’s own words, separate from a clock measurement.

## Leave the next draft a usable conclusion

Return to the opening record. A next step could be: “Check the ladder base, clarify that only landing activates it, and ask someone who has not heard the explanation to try.” This gives you more to act on than labelling the entire game easy or difficult.

When the next record arrives, look for the same question. If the difficulty has moved to counting spaces, you can follow that step instead. Each test picks up a question from the previous one, gradually helping you improve the materials.
