# Who Will Play This Game, and When?

Imagine Lin bringing the animal crossing game to an activity room. Yu would like to see it, but she has a class in ten minutes. They have a small table with schoolbags already on it. This is a different starting point from a weekend afternoon with a whole table and time to talk.

The rules are the same. The conditions for hearing an explanation, setting out pieces, and considering a move have changed. “For beginners” is not yet specific enough for a design. We need to know how those beginners will encounter it.

## Questions an age cannot answer

Two twelve-year-olds may bring different experience. One already understands taking turns; the other first encounters the idea that both players must act to complete a round. The experienced player may dislike competition, while the newcomer may want a substantial challenge. Age can prompt us to check our wording. It cannot answer these more specific questions.

In our imagined meeting, Lin could say, “This is a game about finding a way across together. Would you like to see one round, then decide whether to continue?” Yu can understand both the activity and the extent of her agreement. If she prefers to watch, Lin can demonstrate a round for both animals without treating her presence as a commitment to play.

An introduction can also resolve an unnecessary uncertainty. Suppose Yu asks, “If Rabbit arrives first, have I lost?” Both animals must arrive for a shared win; there are no individual places. Her question reveals an expectation she brought with her. Explaining the goal is more useful than deciding that she is bad at games.

## Time must hold more than the turns

The basic game lasts at most four rounds, but four rounds do not mean four minutes. Reading the actions, counting planks, and discussing who will stay at the start to collect all take time. Packing up does too. Without timing an actual session, we cannot promise that ten minutes will be enough.

Different arrangements serve different purposes. Today they could demonstrate just the first round to understand an action. They could arrange more time to finish a game. Or they could attempt a whole game and stop when class begins. The first two change the plan for this meeting. The third needs an understood way to stop.

If the bell rings before both animals arrive, they can record the session as “stopped partway” and pack up. Under the basic rules, a loss occurs when the fourth round ends without both animals across. A class interrupting the session is not another animal action, and agreeing to try a game need not become an obligation to finish it.

Space also needs a concrete look. The animals, route, available planks in the center, used planks beside them, and reserve paper pieces off the table need identifiable places. Moving the bags or changing tables may help. Later we will examine seeing and reaching the pieces more closely. For now, we are noticing conditions the game depends on.

## What people discuss matters more than how much they speak

Suppose the purpose of this meeting is to think together. A question worth preserving might be, “Who stays at the start this time so the other animal can move?” If they want to chat while playing, a long new passage to read on every turn would compete with that conversation. More story or more choices may not serve this particular meeting.

Another pair may want quiet calculation. A clear route and a limited supply of planks give them something to consider. Quiet players do not automatically need a speed contest added, and one person’s excitement does not establish the other person’s enjoyment. Their accounts and specific events can help revise our assumptions.

## A starting judgment can change

We can now describe a more useful starting point than “suitable for twelve-year-olds”: “Two friends meeting this game for the first time, somewhere they can sit and lay out pieces, with an unhurried stretch of free time to discuss a small shared problem.” Each part does work. Two people sets a player count. First contact calls for a clear explanation. Shared discussion gives a direction to the relationships between actions.

These conditions can change as we learn. If the friends can never arrive together, a different design might preserve the position and turns between visits. If people mainly want to watch a demonstration, the meeting could become an introduction they can leave when they wish. A changed situation can justify a changed direction without blaming people for failing to appear as expected.

This provisional setting gives the next chapter a starting point: what do we want people to do, how much should we build first, and what still needs checking?
