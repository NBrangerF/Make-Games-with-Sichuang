# One Friend Says “Too Slow,” Another Says “Too Rushed”

“Too slow. Add a timer.” “I haven’t worked it out yet—why rush me?” Imagine hearing both after a playtest. Picking a side gives you little help with the next draft. Find the moment each friend is describing and see whether they are objecting to the same thing.

Imagine players taking turns to repair cars. A player chooses a repair card, returns its required parts to the supply, then turns the car to its repaired side. We are considering only that procedure. One friend says, “The waiting takes forever. Add a timer.” Another objects, “I haven’t worked it out yet, and now you want to rush me?” This dialogue is invented, not a record of a real test.

## A Suggestion Points toward a Moment Someone Wants Changed

The first friend proposes a timer because some period of waiting feels too long. That wish deserves attention, but a timer is only one response. We do not yet know whether the waiting occurs while choosing a repair, finding parts, or checking the cost.

An immediate vote on time limits would make the friends argue between two solutions. Asking which part of the wait bothered them most could identify something more specific. The first friend may accept thinking time but wonder why so much waiting continues after the repair has been chosen.

Suppose clear sections for different parts could shorten the search after a repair is chosen. That might address the first friend’s concern without adding a timer. A suggestion can contain both a difficulty worth addressing and a solution that may not fit. Separating them gives the designer more possible responses to compare.

## The Same Pause May Need Opposite Responses

Suppose the second friend had already chosen a repair but was searching through three piles of similar parts. Making the parts easier to distinguish might reduce both the searching and the other person’s wait. A timer could instead turn an unresolved handling problem into pressure.

Another possibility is uncertainty about whether payment comes before turning over the car. The player keeps checking the rules because the procedure needs clarification. Or the rules and materials are clear, and the player enjoys comparing two reasonable repairs. That could be a difference in preferred pace rather than a writing error waiting to be corrected.

These are separate hypotheses, not facts already established by the imagined conversation. “What were you looking for?” makes more room for an unexpected answer than “Was the card hard to read?” The pause tells us where to return with a question. The player’s account can help distinguish searching for pieces, checking a rule, and making a decision. If they cannot explain it in words, we could invite them to point to the two cards they were comparing, without requiring a polished explanation.

## Look at the Time after the Choice

If rearranging the parts seems worth trying first, the next observation could follow what happens after a player identifies a repair card: how do they find and pay the required pieces? If that step becomes smoother, we still need to hear whether it improved the part of waiting that concerned the other person.

Speed alone is not the answer. A friend might pay quickly but choose the wrong parts. Another might take longer while discussing two repair options with a companion. Recording only the total seconds would compress these different activities into one number.

Nor should every improvement in a second game be credited to a new arrangement. The returning players may simply know the materials better. Keeping that possibility does not make the change worthless. It tells us whom else it might help to observe and which part of the action still deserves attention.

## Different Preferences Still Allow a Specific Conversation

If the friends really do prefer different rhythms, the designer can explain how much thinking space the game aims to provide and discuss whether they want to try another arrangement. There is no need to promise that one rule will satisfy every preference.

But “you dislike slow games” cannot dismiss a report of repeated payment or hard-to-distinguish parts. Taste and practical difficulty can coexist. Equally, keeping space for careful thought does not require preserving every search through a pile.

Opposing suggestions do not force the designer to choose the most persuasive speaker. Returning to the moment each person describes may reveal a shared improvement, two different needs for support, or a real decision about the pace the game should offer.
