# Why Can We Have Enough Planks but Still Run Out of Time?

Set aside the weather variation and return to the original river. Rabbit and Hedgehog start on the bank with four shared planks. Each step costs one plank. Staying on the starting bank to collect adds two. Lin acts before Yu, and both animals must reach the far bank within four rounds.

We have seen that leaving the starting bank together too early can leave them short of planks. Would collecting more always make the crossing safer? Before adding to the supply, let's follow where the planks come from and where they go—and watch the animal staying behind to obtain them.

## More Planks Mean Someone Took an Action

Collecting takes two new planks from the spare pieces; it never retrieves planks already spent. Enough spare pieces represent the new planks the rules allow. They are not resources the players already own.

The supply starts at four. Each bridge uses one, reducing that supply. Collecting brings two new planks from extra materials into the middle. A player must spend an action to add them; a new round does not make them appear automatically.

In the first chapter, Rabbit moved first, reducing four to three. Hedgehog then collected two, bringing the supply to five. Comparing only the beginning and end of the round would tell us “one more plank,” while hiding the one spent and the two gained.

We therefore need to ask both “How many are here now?” and “What changed?” Five planks do not tell us who stayed on the bank or how many turns remain. In games, things that can be obtained, spent, and saved for later use are often called **resources**. Planks are one resource here. After learning the name, we still need to ask what they are for.

The reason to get more is to move both animals across. Each needs three forward moves, requiring six planks altogether. They start with four, so at least one collecting action is necessary. The two planks it adds exactly cover the shortage.

## What If One Animal Collects Twice?

Imagine another sequence. In round one, Rabbit moves and Hedgehog collects, leaving five planks. In round two, Rabbit moves again while Hedgehog stays on the bank to collect again. Six planks are now available. That looks more comfortable than the original successful route, but Hedgehog has not set out.

Two rounds have finished. Hedgehog has only its third- and fourth-round turns left. Crossing from the start requires three steps; two turns can take it only as far as the second stone. Rabbit might reach the far bank in the third round, but it cannot give Hedgehog an extra move.

Follow the remaining turns. In round three, Rabbit arrives and Hedgehog reaches the first stone. In round four, Rabbit waits and Hedgehog reaches the second. Three planks remain, but the four-round deadline ends the game in a loss.

What is missing is an opportunity for Hedgehog to use the material in time. The extra two planks really were obtained. Obtaining them, however, replaced a step forward. More material did not restore that step.

Notice that this is about the same animal collecting twice. If both players collect once each, each animal still has three turns for moving and can finish within four rounds. We cannot call every second collecting action a cause of failure.

Adding up the team’s actions can also hide this limit. Six moves and one collect action require seven turns in total; four rounds offer eight. Yet Rabbit cannot give its unused turn to Hedgehog. In the failed sequence, Rabbit waits in round four while Hedgehog still needs another move. We need to count both how much the team must do and which animal must do each part.

## The Ending Changes What an Action Can Do

Now change one rule: allow five rounds, with everything else unchanged and enough spare paper pieces for the longer game. Hedgehog, left on the second stone, can spend a plank to arrive in the fifth round. The same collecting actions and route can now finish because another round is available.

What if we allow only three? Each animal needs three forward moves, using all three of its turns. Neither can spare a turn to collect, yet four starting planks cannot pay for six moves. With the other basic rules unchanged, the three-round version has no successful sequence. That loss cannot be explained by players failing to discover a clever enough plan.

We might choose how many planks the animals receive, then add “play four rounds” at the end. But the ending rule affects choices from the start. To finish in four rounds, each animal can spend at most one turn on something other than moving. Five rounds allow more room. Three remove the preparation the crossing requires.

This does not automatically tell us how many rounds to choose. The designer still needs to decide what kind of planning this example should invite. We can now explain a definite consequence: changing the deadline changes which plans can be completed. It does more than add or remove a few minutes.

## What Are the Remaining Planks Worth?

Return to the four-round basic version. Suppose Hedgehog begins its fourth-round turn on the second stone, with one plank available. Moving will take it across. Three additional planks would not make this animal more completely across, and unused planks earn no points.

An object's value depends on what needs to happen. When material is missing, collecting can keep a route possible. When turns are missing, the same action can delay arrival. To judge it, we need to consider animal positions, available planks, and remaining turns together.

List the planks still needed beside the remaining forward moves. To trade material for time, sketch a separate candidate route: two steps across, two planks per step. You now have a concrete proposal to compare against both shortages.
