# Am I still involved when it is someone else's turn?

Your pawn is on 20, your opponent’s on 18. In choice version C, you roll 2 and 4, choose 4, and reach the finish. Your opponent picks up the dice: “But I haven’t had my turn this round.” The rule ends the game immediately. This imagined exchange brings out an easily missed feature of taking turns: the last round may never be completed.

## Turn order allocates opportunities

Choose a starting player, then alternate complete turns: roll, choose, move, and resolve the landing space. Reaching 24 wins immediately. This procedure is straightforward and prevents simultaneous finishes, but the players may take different numbers of turns.

The rules tell us that the second player has no guaranteed final turn. To find out how much that affects winning chances, we would need to account for earlier rolls, connections, and choices, or observe enough complete games.

A separate version could finish the entire round before judging the result. It would need a policy for two arrivals in that round: a shared win, another contest, or some other criterion. The ending procedure changes when players may celebrate as well as how opportunities are distributed.

## What happens during the wait?

Imagine your opponent studying a 2 and a 6. You might count along, hope they miss the ladder, or turn away to chat. When recording a wait, look at the person without the dice as well as the clock.

Record the duration of the active player's operation separately from what the waiting player looks at or says. Notice whether they need a reminder when their next turn begins. A pause could involve calculation, difficulty reading the board, or conversation; seconds alone do not measure boredom.

The choice version has no captures or blocking. Still, an opponent nearing the finish tells you that your remaining chances may be few. To see whether that connection holds someone’s attention, watch where they look, what they say, and whether they need a reminder for their next turn.

## Simultaneous rolls create another procedure

Consider a separate experiment: both players roll two dice at the start of a round, choose one each, then move together. If both reach exactly 24 that round, they share the win. Keep C's board and landing effects, but clearly label the simultaneous procedure as a different version.

Players no longer wait for their opponent to roll first. They still wait for the slower decision. Whether they can see one another's dice and choices also matters. Announcing one choice before the other gives the players different information.

In a paper test, cover each selected die, then reveal together when both players are ready. Watch whether they know when to reveal and can follow both pawns. After removing the wait for a roll, how much work have these new steps added?

## Time saved can become work elsewhere

Sequential turns focus attention on one movement and make mistakes easier to trace. Simultaneous movement may require checking two routes together. People who need help counting may find that assistance harder to obtain during simultaneous play.

Set up the same position for both procedures and compare them. Record where waiting occurs, whether explanations need repeating, and whether both players recognize the ending. This checks a local procedure, not average whole-game duration.

Save the simultaneous draft separately; C still uses alternating turns. Give the draft a specific purpose, such as “reduce the wait for the other player to roll.” When you return to compare it, you can check whether it removes that wait and where the time goes instead.
