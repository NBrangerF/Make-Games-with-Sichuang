# Plan a playtest around one question

“Try my game and tell me whether it is fun.” A friend sits down, but the invitation still leaves your observation unclear. They might judge the artwork, concentrate on winning, or avoid mentioning confusion to be polite. A small test needs a task that both people understand.

Use the previous chapter’s board to arrange a small test. Watch whether someone new to it can follow the sheet, choose one die, and move the pawn to its destination.

## Match the materials to the question

Prepare one consistent version of the 24-space board, two pawns, two ordinary six-sided dice, and the C instructions. To examine this particular relationship, put a pawn on 6 with dice showing 2 and 6. Explain that this is a preset situation. Ask the participant to read, choose a result, and move, without first demonstrating the answer.

A full game from 0 might never produce that combination. Full play can investigate pacing and sustained participation, but cannot guarantee an encounter with the rule relationship under examination. When the scope changes, the task and record should change with it.

## Explain how help will work

Try inviting them like this: “Could you help me check whether this sheet is clear? Try one turn using what is written here. Ask if anything is unclear, and stop whenever you like. I’ll note what needs explaining.” Say how you intend to record the session, and ask separately before publishing their words or photographs.

Offer help when it is needed. Note where the person paused, what you said, and what they did afterwards. When you review the record, you can distinguish what the materials explained from what still needed you beside the board.

## Leave space for what actually happens

Record version C, starting position 6, results 2 and 6, whether any explanation was given beforehand, and whether the participant has seen this board. Leave blank fields for the actual choice, movement, consulted text, questions, and facilitator prompts. To ask about reasoning after the action, try “How did you decide?” rather than “Did you miss the ladder?”

“How did you choose?” leaves the explanation to the player. “Did you miss the ladder?” has already supplied one. Keep their words beside the movement record. Mark anything you could not capture clearly rather than filling gaps to make the notes look tidy.

## Remember that people learn during comparisons

Suppose you compare two ladder drawings. Keep rules and position fixed and change the drawing. If one person sees A and then B, the second attempt may be easier because they already know the answer. With several willing participants, you can alternate which drawing comes first. With very few, at least retain the order in the record instead of claiming the learning effect has disappeared.

Check what is on the table: one version of the materials, one task, and a recording sheet with space to write. During the test, you can watch how the person completes this turn and where it differs from your expectations, without trying to remember ten research goals.
