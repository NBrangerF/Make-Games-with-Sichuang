# What Does This River Show, and What Does It Leave Out?

The animal crossing tracks positions, planks, and rounds. It does not ask us to judge wind, water depth, or whether either animal can swim. It reduces a complicated crossing to a few relationships we can understand and change. Making those choices is a way of building a model.

A model lets us think about a problem with relatively few things. Understanding it requires noticing which relationships are in the rules, which belong to pictures or stories, and which do not appear at all.

## What question remains after the omissions?

In the basic game, each move costs one plank, only animals at the start can collect, and both must arrive by the end of round four. Together these relationships pose a question: who stays on the bank first so both animals have enough material and opportunities to finish? We can calculate that without knowing the water temperature.

Now suppose we draw fish in the river and introduce the game as protecting the river. If every action still changes only animal positions and the plank supply, players cannot affect the fish through their choices. The picture includes fish; the rules have yet to express how protection happens.

To make protection something players consider, we would need to choose a relationship, such as how an action affects fish, then specify its visible consequence and connection to reaching the far bank. Each addition can create more to understand and record. We could instead keep the fish as scenery and describe this version as planning a shared crossing. A model can be small. Its promises should fit what it actually expresses.

## Connections also take a position

Leave the animals briefly for a completely fictional model of riverside messages. This is independent of the crossing game.

There are three households, A, B, and C, in a line. A neighbors B, and B neighbors C; A and C are not neighbors. Initially none knows a message. A player has two announcement tokens. Spending one selects a household for a reading; that household and its immediate neighbors learn the message at once. The message spreads no further, and those who know remember it. The task finishes immediately when all three know. Otherwise, it ends incomplete when both tokens are spent.

Selecting B once informs B and both neighbors, A and C, completing the task with one token. Selecting A first informs only A and B; selecting C afterward informs the remaining household, using two tokens altogether. We can calculate these results from the stated rules.

But why does a neighbor definitely hear? Why does nobody tell a friend afterward? This model makes distance a condition for transmission and makes an announcement certain to work. It leaves out trust, language, and other connections. Those choices make the middle household particularly effective. The rules express a way of considering a problem, rather than a picture of reality without any choices behind it.

Using this to discuss real communication would require finding out whether those relationships hold in reality. The fictional result establishes what happens under its stated conditions. It cannot establish that a real middle household must have the most influence.

## A story can leave room that an action cannot

Return to the crossing. Why does Hedgehog stay at the bank? We can leave that without a single required answer. Someone might imagine caution; another might imagine care for a friend. If both follow the same action rules, the story has room for different interpretations.

Where collected planks go cannot remain open in the same way. In the center, both animals can use them. In a private pocket, the other animal cannot spend them directly. That changes the next available move and needs a definite rule, rather than a guess about an animal’s personality.

Rabbit and Hedgehog could have different speeds in another story, but this version gives them the same step length. That lets us attend to order and shared material. The names supply character images; equal movement is a choice made by the model.

## A recollection chooses its emphasis too

Suppose somebody describes a finished game as, “Rabbit led the way and reached the bank first.” That fits our successful route but omits Hedgehog’s first collect action. “Hedgehog prepared the material so both could arrive” emphasizes support without retelling every move either.

“Rabbit discovered the route for everyone” would add a discovery absent from the rules. In the basic game, the entire route is visible from the start. Moving Rabbit reveals no new information. Stories can emphasize different things while keeping claims about an action’s consequences connected to what happened.

Designers therefore need to attend to both selection and connection: what should players consider, which actions express it, which omissions help make it clear, and whether pictures or narration promise something the rules cannot deliver.
