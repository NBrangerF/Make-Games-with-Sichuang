# Do spare reroll tokens guarantee a quicker finish?

Return to a separate experiment. R starts from B, with two reroll tokens per player at setup. Each turn still begins with one die. Before moving, the player may spend one token to reroll once and must accept the replacement, even if worse. Declining keeps the original result. At most one reroll is allowed per turn. Spent tokens leave the game and never refresh.

Put away the choice version’s second die. With one die and two paper tokens per player, you can try a different comparison: keep the number in front of you, or spend a chance to replace it?

## Spending also means giving up the known result

Imagine a pawn at 6 with an initial 6. Accepting stops at 12. Spending a token could produce a 2, reaching 8 and climbing to 17, or a 1, stopping at 7. The purchase is another random opportunity, not guaranteed additional progress.

The cost has two parts: one token disappears from the limited supply, and the known 6 is surrendered. Once the replacement appears, the player cannot restore the token or spend a second token during the same turn.

When designing this resource, set out both a fortunate and an unfortunate reroll. The player faces a range of possible results. Including the disappointing ones helps explain why they might hold on to a token.

## After both tokens are gone

R has a clear supply: two tokens each at setup, with no later arrivals. A token can be spent only on its owner’s turn, after the initial die appears and before the pawn moves. It cannot be used during the opponent’s turn or retrospectively after sliding down a snake.

A player without tokens still rolls and moves, but can no longer replace the result. Spending both early brings the later game back to the base version’s rhythm. Note when the reroll option disappears, as well as how many tokens remain at the end.

Saved tokens also score nothing and are not a victory condition. A player may reach 24 with both intact or win after spending both. Inventory describes available options, not progress towards the goal by itself.

## The finish still contains uncertainty

In B, fix a pawn at 23 and temporarily ignore the opponent, including the possibility that the opponent ends the race. Each of this pawn’s turns finishes on a 1 with probability 1/6. Other results preserve the position. Under independent repetition, the mean wait for the first 1 is six of that pawn’s own turns.

Six is an average for these repeated attempts. Some finish on the first try; others take longer. It counts only this pawn’s turns from 23, ignoring an opponent ending the race and all the turns needed to get to 23.

In R, spending at 23 can still produce another overshoot. Two tokens allow at most two additional rolls during the game, not two guaranteed forward movements. They change opportunities at particular moments without guaranteeing completion before a deadline.

## Find the wait the player wants to shorten

If someone says, “Nothing happened near the end,” several explanations remain open: repeated overshoots, lengthy opponent turns, or misunderstanding when rerolls are allowed. Supplying more tokens directly changes only a resource condition. It may leave the other problems untouched.

Compare separately. R can investigate whether limited retries have understandable value. Another experiment starting from B can change only exact finishing to reaching or passing. Changing both together would make it harder to attribute any difference in duration or preference.

Draw two spaces for the paper tokens and move each spent token aside. The visible supply makes “try again now” and “save it for the finish” concrete alternatives. During a playtest, note where a player spends the last token and ask what they had in mind.
