# Does winning together already mean cooperating?

Try changing “I win if I arrive first” to “we win if we both arrive.” Call this cooperation experiment S and start from single-die base version B. Each person controls one pawn and takes alternating turns; both must reach 24 by the end of round 10. A round gives each player one turn, with finished pawns skipping later turns. There is no die selection, reroll, or new helping action.

## I have finished, but I still need you to arrive

Set out a position after round 9: you are at 24 and your partner is at 23. On the last round, a 1 brings shared success. Any other number leaves the pawn still, and the deadline brings shared failure.

In competitive B, your arrival would already have won. In S, your partner's progress remains relevant. The victory condition changes the reason to care, but you have no legal action that changes their die or position.

You can hope for that 1 together, but cannot give your partner another chance. If you wanted the players to negotiate and divide work, this turn shows what is missing: an action that helps someone else.

## Advice requires something to decide

S inherits B's mandatory movement. When the partner rolls 2, discussion cannot legally turn it into 1. If the intended experience is negotiating actions, these rules supply little to negotiate. “Discuss your moves” does not create options that the game lacks.

Adding options would not automatically distribute participation. A separate experiment with shared reroll tokens would need to specify who decides to spend them: the active player, both together, or a rotating controller. Those arrangements create different relationships. None has been added to S here.

Observe who proposes, who decides, and who physically carries out an action. Moving a pawn on someone else's instructions may involve meaningful participation, or very little personal judgment. Touching a component is not enough to distinguish those experiences.

## A cost makes helping concrete

The preserved river-crossing alternative offers another direction. Its characters can remain at the starting bank to collect shared planks, using an action they could otherwise spend on their own progress. Both animals must arrive for a shared win. Helping and personal progress are connected through resources and limited opportunities.

The river path asks who stays to prepare planks, who moves first, and how the pair divides the work. Switch to that complete alternative when you want to examine how helping uses one person’s action.

Put the boards side by side and ask, “What can I do for you now?” S makes both people care about one result. The river game also lets someone spend an action to affect it. The answer brings out the difference.

## Give the shared goal actions to match

If the intention is sharing suspense about a result, S is a proposal worth testing. If it is planning and helping together, decisions that affect the shared situation are still needed. Then check whether one person takes over all judgment. Choose the intention before adding rules.

The ten-round deadline is also an untested parameter. Snakes, ladders, exact arrival, and the requirement that both finish all affect success. No calculation or session yet establishes that ten rounds is appropriate.

Keep S as a separate draft; the handoff still uses competitive, two-player C. To develop S further, return to the position after round 9. What would you like the finished player to be able to do besides wait? Write one complete helping action and see how it changes the final round.
