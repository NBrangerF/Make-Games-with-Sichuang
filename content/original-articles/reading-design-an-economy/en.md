# Where Do Planks Come From, and Where Do They Go?

In the previous chapter, Yu collected first, so Lin saw the additional planks before moving. Order helped us follow when material arrived. But how do planks enter the shared supply? Can spent planks be taken back? The rules must determine these things too.

Return first to the basic game: four shared planks at the start; collecting takes two new planks from spare pieces; moving spends one and puts it aside as used. Both animals must make three moves within four rounds and reach the far bank together. We will first keep this goal and deadline while following the things that support their actions.

## Things Can Enter and Leave Without Going Around

The central pile contains planks the players can spend now. Spare pieces represent material not yet obtained; the used pile represents costs already paid. The pieces may look alike, but the three places mean different things.

In round one, Rabbit moves and Hedgehog collects. The central pile goes from four to three, then from three to five. The spent plank stays aside. The two added planks come from the spares. Planks follow a path from acquisition to use; no rule returns them to the middle.

Changing collect to “take two planks back from the used pile” would create a different source. The used pile starts empty, so nothing can return before someone spends a plank. Basic collecting works on the very first turn; the new version would have to wait for spending. Both may look like adding two planks, but they differ in when the action can begin and when it is available.

Deciding where resources come from and go is part of designing a game economy. An economy does not require money or shopping here. If planks can be obtained, kept, and used, their connection to actions is worth following.

## Why Might More Raw Material Produce No More Finished Planks?

Consider a separate preparation scene on the bank before the animals set out. It is not a new stage attached to the basic game and gives no extra planks to a later crossing. We compare only preparation: make four finished planks within two rounds. Reaching four completes the task immediately; otherwise it is incomplete at the end of round two.

Lin and Yu still take one turn each per round, in that order, for four turns altogether. They start with no logs and no finished planks, and share all materials. On a turn, a player may collect two new logs, consume two logs to saw them into two finished planks, or wait. Sawing requires two logs. Raw material is plentiful, there are no other costs, and the animals do not move during this scene.

In round one, Lin collects two logs. Yu saws them, leaving no logs and two finished planks. Repeating those actions in round two reaches four planks and completes the task. Raw and finished material must be counted separately: two unprocessed logs are not two completed planks.

Would increasing collect to three logs make it faster? In round one, collecting supplies three logs; sawing leaves one log and two planks. In round two, collecting brings the logs to four; sawing leaves two logs and four planks. This still takes four turns and still produces four finished planks.

Increasing collection added material waiting to be processed. Two sawing actions producing two planks each limit this plan’s output. A step that holds up later work is a **bottleneck**. Once enough raw material is available, adding more does not add opportunities to process it.

## What Change Would Address This Particular Problem?

Changing only sawing to “spend three logs to make three planks” does not immediately solve it either. With collecting back at two logs, the first collection cannot even pay the new cost. Another turn must provide more material. What a step produces needs to meet what the next step requires.

If collecting supplies four logs and sawing consumes four to make four planks, the two turns in round one complete the original task. That shows a way to make it faster, but it also removes the original second round of preparation. If the intention is to have the players discuss how to divide the work, a designer must consider whether the increased speed removes the process they wanted.

These rules split “get planks” into collecting and processing. The split makes the order of preparation worth considering, while adding another material to track and another conversion to explain. If the point is simply who stays on the bank to help, basic collecting already does that. The extra pile of logs earns its place when the processing sequence itself offers something worth comparing.

## When Does Growth Make Further Growth Faster?

An increase need not require a player to collect actively. Consider a separate supply rule that adds two planks at each supply step. Three planks become five; six become eight. Having more stored does not increase what this supply step gives.

Now compare a different supply rule separately: for every complete group of three planks present at the start of the supply step, gain one new plank, calculating once only. Starting with three gives one and reaches four. Starting with six gives two and reaches eight. A larger supply produces a larger addition, which can help reach more groups of three later. This connection from growth back to the condition for growth is **positive feedback**. We are comparing only the supply step, without adding it to either the preparation scene or the crossing rules.

Both arrangements increase the supply, but fixed additions and additions based on stored quantity work differently. The latter may give keeping planks an extra benefit. Yet if planks must eventually help the animals cross, keeping them unused may delay departure. To judge the value of this relationship, connect its benefit to the goal and deadline instead of simply approving faster growth.

Put away the logs and supply variations and return to the basic game. Planks support movement, collecting takes a turn, and four rounds determine whether there is still time. Following acquisition, processing, and use helps locate the step worth changing.
