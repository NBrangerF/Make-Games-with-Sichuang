# Several Comments, One Next Question

“There aren’t enough planks.” “One more round would fix it.” “Does collecting mean taking back used planks?” If these three comments follow a playtest, should the next draft add planks, extend the game, and rewrite the instructions all at once? First, look closer. All three sound like trouble crossing the river, but they may concern different events.

The previous chapter followed one observation. Now we will bring several records together. All three sessions in this chapter are hypothetical examples, not tests that actually happened. They use the same basic rules and instructions, which we will call v0.2: each animal needs three moves to cross, moving only to the next position ahead without going backward or skipping a position; the players share four planks; moving one step costs one plank; collecting two new planks is possible only on the starting bank. Each round gives Lin one turn, then Yu one turn. There are at most four rounds, and both animals arriving ends the game immediately with a shared win. Waiting also uses a turn. There is no weather, shortcut, or personal score here.

## Put Each Comment Back Where It Happened

The first record says that Lin moved in the first round, then Yu moved too. Both animals stood on the first stone, with two shared planks left. Someone then said, “There aren’t enough planks.”

That position really cannot lead to success. Each animal needs two more moves, costing four planks in total. Only two remain, and neither animal is still on the starting bank to collect. But this does not show that starting with four planks always makes success impossible: in the successful sequence from chapter one, one animal stayed behind to collect. What we know is that this departure plan used up the opportunity to replenish the supply. The record does not tell us whether the players understood that consequence beforehand or found the discovery interesting.

The second table took a different route. In each of the first two rounds, Lin collected while Yu moved. Rabbit is still on the starting bank; Hedgehog is on the second stone. Six planks remain: the initial four, plus four from two collections, minus two spent moving. Someone says, “One more round would fix it.”

Here the shared supply is sufficient, but Rabbit has only two turns left to make three moves. Adding planks cannot fix that difficulty. An extra round would indeed allow another move. Yet making this particular plan succeed is a different decision from extending the whole game. A new deadline would affect other plans too. We still need to consider the thinking space we want the game to offer.

At the third table, before the first collect action in round one, someone asks, “Does collecting mean taking back used planks?” The facilitator explains that collecting takes two new planks from the spare paper pieces outside the playing area. Play then continues. This record first points to how the instructions were understood. Play going smoothly after a prompt does not show that the original instructions worked independently.

## Similar Complaints May Describe Different Problems

If we group all three records under “Crossing is too difficult,” lowering the difficulty may seem like the only response. Following the events reveals three things to investigate: whether the players noticed their opportunity to collect before departing, whether preparation included checking remaining turns, and whether collecting itself was clear.

Do not jump to the opposite conclusion that three causes have now been established. We can calculate why the first two plans fail, but we do not yet know why those players chose them. They may have overlooked the deadline, wanted to try an extreme approach, or failed to consider both animals’ plans together. The third table asked a question; that does not establish that every first-time reader would interpret the rule in the same way.

A useful question can be quite specific: “Before sending both animals off the bank, did the first table discuss where later planks would come from?” If that part is missing from the record, write that it was not recorded. Next time, we could observe that discussion or ask afterward how the players had planned to continue. A recollection is still the player’s account, not a word-for-word record of the conversation at the time.

Organized this way, a page of comments becomes more than a list of changes to make. Each comment has a place in the game, a record that currently supports it, and something still unknown.

## Check Whether the Records Concern the Same Thing

Suppose a fourth record comes from a five-round version. Combining its wins and losses with those from the four-round version would discard a crucial difference. Someone playing for the fourth time may also understand preparation differently from someone seeing the board for the first time. Keep the version, prior experience, starting position, and any help from the facilitator with each record.

These records can still inform one another. Five rounds might reveal a plan that has no time to develop in four. A returning friend might discover a way to cooperate that everyone previously missed. They should not, however, be compressed into a single total saying, “People lose whatever we change.”

If the facilitator introduces a new rule during play, mark where the change happened and record the rules before and after it. That session can help explore an idea, but it no longer checks one unchanged version from beginning to end. Keeping the distinction lets a future collaborator—or your future self—see which part answers which question.

## What Comes First Depends on This Round of Investigation

Suppose the purpose is to find out whether people seeing the materials for the first time understand collecting. The third table raises a direct question about the instructions and the areas for paper pieces. Even a question asked by one person can prevent a basic action from continuing. Two other comments about crossing do not create a two-to-one vote for extending the game instead.

If we have already established that the participants understand collecting, and want to observe how they discuss departure, the first two tables deserve closer attention. We could examine when they noticed the need for more planks and the deadline, while keeping the four-round rule for now. Discovering a legal plan that cannot succeed is part of this game. The designer must then consider whether that discovery offers the intended players an understandable reason to try again.

Some comments need clarification before we can prioritize them. “Too complicated” might describe one sentence or the need to calculate many moves every turn. Asking for a particular moment helps the next draft more than labeling the entire game complicated.

## Carry the Diagnosis into the Next Decision

This review could leave a provisional conclusion like this: “In v0.2, two plans ran into different limits: the opportunity to replenish planks and the number of turns left. These records do not show that every opening must fail. Another player asked where collected planks came from and continued after an explanation. First check whether the instructions distinguish spare, available, and spent planks for a new reader, then observe how players plan their departure.”

Keep the revision note beside the original record. Someone else can follow why this change comes first, and the next feedback will have a decision to be compared against.
