# What Have You Already Guessed before Reading the Rules?

Before opening a box, you may already expect a race or a cooperative adventure. A character on the cover and a verb in the title can set expectations before the first rule. A fictional post-office game lets us follow that expectation into setup.

Imagine a fictional story in which the courier Lan crosses a mountain alone in a storm to deliver a letter to a lighthouse. A later board game still shows Lan running alone on its box and advertises “the fastest courier.” Yet its players jointly run a post office, with no individual delivery ranking. Someone looking only at the box may expect a race.

## An Image Offers a Direction First

That guess need not come from carelessness. A lone runner combined with “fastest” readily suggests speed and individual victory. Hearing “you complete the task together” requires the newcomer to adjust an understanding already taking shape.

Expectations can also help someone begin. Knowing you are a courier can make an envelope on paper feel worth delivering. Trouble arises when images and words keep pointing toward one kind of play while the rules ask for another. The newcomer must correct an impression before learning can continue.

Turning a story into a game involves selecting a part people can act within. Preparing Lan’s departure, arranging relays between couriers, and handling the return after the letter arrives all connect with the solitary delivery while posing different problems. Choosing to run a post office calls for making assignment and handovers understandable, rather than only reproducing a picture of somebody running toward the lighthouse.

## Partners in the Story, Letters on the Table

To see the difference, add only a handover rule. Everyone wins together, but a letter sits on its current holder’s courier card. Two couriers must be in the same location. The holder may spend one action on their own turn to pass one letter to the other courier.

Lan and Mu now stand together at a bridge, with the letter on Lan’s card. On Mu’s turn, Mu may suggest a relay but cannot simply take the letter. The handover must wait for Lan’s turn and cost Lan an action. This is a fragment of imagined play, not a complete game.

“We run one post office” describes a relationship. Shared victory describes the result everyone wants. The handover sentence specifies how one letter changes hands now. Cooperation does not automatically make letters public objects anyone can take, and fictional trust does not supply missing action timing.

## Answer the Earliest Guess

The lone figure need not disappear from the box. An introduction could say, “Lan made that delivery alone. In this game, you organize the whole post office.” A reader can now recognize both a connection with the story and a change.

Placing the letter on a courier card and showing one legal handover at the bridge then connects that relationship to a visible action. The introduction gives direction; the example explains possession, the acting player, and timing. They support one another without performing the same job.

“Everyone works together” alone is too vague. Starting with every handover exception might instead bury the reason for delivering a letter. The introduction needs to connect them: offer a purpose worth understanding, then explain the immediate step clearly.

## Knowing the Story Does Not Set Rules for Everyone Else

Suppose a story enthusiast says, “Lan would never give someone else an important letter.” That is one interpretation of the character. Another person might interpret a relay as Lan learning to trust a partner. Both readings can be discussed while the rules still allow the handover.

If everyone wants to try “Lan cannot pass letters,” it should be named as a new agreement and accepted together before being used. It removes an action and may affect how the post office cooperates. Hiding it inside “any fan would know that” quietly takes a choice away from someone unfamiliar with the source.

A new player also has room to ask why letters have individual holders when everyone wins together. The question might reveal useful meeting arrangements created by handovers, or an explanation that remains unclear. Not reading the original story does not prevent someone from taking the game seriously.

Knowing the story can also give somebody more opportunities to speak. Suppose a player names every supporting character and repeatedly calls a newcomer’s plan ignorant of the post office. That knowledge might provide interesting details, but it does not grant authority over another player’s action. A complete introduction to the relevant relationships lets the newcomer propose a plan using the known rules, without first passing a test of story memory.

An invitation to add a little character background can let an enthusiast share before returning the current decision to its player. Sharing background and running somebody else’s turn can remain separate.

Try showing your cover or invitation without explaining the game. Listen to what someone expects to do and who they think will win, then open the rules and follow the first actions. A mismatch gives you a place to revise the title, picture, or explanation.
