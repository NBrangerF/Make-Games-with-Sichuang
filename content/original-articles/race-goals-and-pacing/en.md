# Why can the final space feel longer than the rest?

Put a pawn at 23 in base version B and set out the results 6, 5, 2, 4, 3, 1 in order. It stays still for the first five attempts and finishes on the last. Walk through this arranged sequence yourself. One space remains, yet you pass the die back and forth several times before arriving.

## You wanted large numbers; now you only want 1

Only one space remains, but large numbers are no longer useful. B uses one ordinary six-sided die. Assuming fairness and independent rolls, each of this player's turns has a one-in-six chance of producing 1. Previous failures do not make success due next time.

Ignoring the opponent and repeatedly attempting from this fixed position, the expected number of attempts is six of the player's own turns. That is neither whole-game duration nor a guarantee of success within six attempts. The player might finish immediately or wait much longer.

C offers two dice and a choice, increasing the chance of a usable result in this particular position. Both dice can nevertheless overshoot. Adding a choice has not removed the wait created by exact arrival.

## Change only the ending rule

Compare a separate experiment in which reaching or passing 24 wins, while all other B rules remain unchanged. From 23, every possible result now ends the game. The final step changes from seeking a particular result to a guaranteed finish on the next turn.

You can feel the change by handling it. Before, you checked each roll for a 1. Now, picking up the die means you will finish. Someone might miss the near misses; someone else might be relieved. Trying both endings gives players something concrete to discuss.

The change also affects how secure a lead is. Under exact arrival, being on 23 does not promise victory next turn. Under reaching-or-passing, it does, unless the opponent finishes first. The rule changes both the ending's duration and the opportunity to catch up.

## Where does the time go?

In future playtests, record total duration alongside arrival near the finish, turns without movement there, and whether the overall position continues to change. Two equally long games can contain very different experiences: one progressing throughout, another waiting for a particular result.

Separate operation time from waiting generated by correctly applied rules. Searching for a snake's tail or repeatedly checking instructions concerns presentation and understanding. Remaining on 23 after legal overshoots is permitted by the random process itself. Both can happen together, but they call for different interventions.

A few sessions may miss unusually long endings. One prolonged session also cannot establish the common case. Constructed end positions help examine a neglected phase; complete games reveal how players reach it.

## Describe the ending you want

Alongside “finish within ten minutes,” try writing: “Near the finish, players know what they are waiting for and want to watch the next result.” One intention calls for timing; the other calls for observation and conversation. Keep both on the test plan so a duration does not stand in for the whole ending.

If adding a turn limit later, specify how the result is decided when it expires. Leading position, a shared result, and no successful finisher express different goals. Ending the session should not require an improvised declaration that someone has done enough.
