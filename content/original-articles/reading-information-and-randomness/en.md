# What Can I Change If I Know the Weather Earlier?

In the basic game, the plank supply, animal positions, and cost of each step were visible. The players still needed to think: a quick move now could leave them without enough planks later. What changes when something is not yet known?

Consider what Yu will do on her next turn. She might have decided without telling Lin. Or she might still be choosing between moving and collecting. These are different kinds of not knowing. In the first case, asking her could reveal an answer. In the second, there is no chosen answer yet. Guessing her decision is also different from calculating the chance of a die roll: she is considering their plans.

## What Does a Face-Down Card Hide?

We will add weather to the crossing example for just one animal's action. To make the comparison clear, set up a separate moment: Hedgehog is on the starting bank, and only one plank is available. We are comparing this action, without deciding the outcome of a complete game.

Prepare six weather cards: four sunny and two rainy. Shuffle them thoroughly, draw one, and place it face down. For this action only, moving one step costs one plank in sunshine or two in rain. Without enough planks, the animal cannot move.

The drawn card already has a definite picture, although neither player has seen it. Turning it over reveals that picture; the act of revealing it does not turn sunshine into rain. If we waited until after the decision to shuffle and draw, the process generating the result would change too. Here we keep the draw the same and change only when the player sees it.

Four of the six cards make the single plank sufficient. Two do not. After a thorough shuffle, the chance of drawing sunshine is four out of six. That is the **probability** here. It describes possible results, without guaranteeing what this draw will produce.

## Say “I'll Move,” Then Check the Weather

In the first arrangement, Yu chooses her action before turning the card over. Suppose she chooses to move.

With a sunny card, she spends the plank and moves Hedgehog onto the first stone. With rain, she needs two planks but has only one. The move fails: Hedgehog stays on the bank and the plank remains available. However, the turn is used up. Yu cannot change to collecting.

That last rule matters. Otherwise, one player might assume a failed move does not count, while the other thinks choosing it uses the turn. They would be playing different versions.

Yu could also choose to collect from the outset, keeping Hedgehog on the bank and bringing the supply to three. We have followed what happens if she chooses the risky move. Not knowing the weather does not force her to take that risk.

## Check the Weather, Then Choose

The second arrangement moves the reveal earlier. After seeing sunshine, Yu can move Hedgehog to the first stone for one plank. After seeing rain, she can collect instead, staying on the bank and increasing the supply to three.

The weather cards and movement costs are unchanged. Rain still prevents her from moving with a single plank. What changes is that she has not yet committed this turn to an action, so she can choose in response to the weather.

Compare the two arrangements under the same rainy result. Committing to move leaves Hedgehog on the bank with one plank. Seeing rain and then choosing to collect leaves Hedgehog on the same bank with three. Ending in the same place does not mean the turn has produced the same result.

Knowing earlier helps here because of a specific opportunity: Hedgehog is still on the bank and can collect. Suppose it were already on a stone with one plank. In rain, it could neither move nor collect. Seeing rain and waiting might leave the same situation as choosing to move and failing. Additional information needs an available action before it gives someone a different result they can bring about.

## Misunderstanding a Rule Is Another Problem

Suppose the card is face up and everyone can see rain, but Yu thinks moving still costs one plank. She is not missing the weather information; she has misunderstood the cost. Revealing another card would not fix that. The two-plank cost needs to be explained first.

When someone says, “I have no idea what to do,” follow the action closely. Has something already happened that they cannot see? Has somebody not decided yet? Or is the explanation unclear? The answer helps determine whether to reveal information sooner, leave an opportunity to respond, or rewrite the instruction.

To repeat this comparison from the same conditions, return the weather card, shuffle thoroughly, and draw again. Without replacement, the numbers of sunny and rainy cards change, so the chances may change too. We cannot change the drawing procedure while treating the deck as the same six cards.

For example, after drawing a sunny card without returning it, the remaining five cards contain three sunny cards and two rainy ones. The next chance of sunshine is three out of five. Keeping the drawn card face up lets everyone check; turning it face down may require everyone to remember what they saw. The drawing procedure stays the same, but the effort of remembering or checking information changes. If the intended challenge is comparing actions, leaving known cards visible can keep memory demands from getting in the way.

Now put the weather cards away and return to the basic version: each step costs one plank, and nothing on the table is secret. Even with everything known, collecting more might not get the animals across in time. The next chapter asks why having enough planks can still leave them too late.
