# Same Average, So Why Prefer One Route?

The weather chapter showed how reveal timing changes Hedgehog's available response. This optional reading keeps the timing fixed: choose first, learn the weather afterward. An independent lantern-delivery example asks another question. If two options have the same average result, are they equivalent?

## One Reliable Route and One Risky Route

You make a single delivery. There are no route fees or later turns. Your current objective is to deliver as many lanterns as possible. The reliable route delivers three in either weather. The risky route delivers five in sunshine but only one in rain.

After choosing, roll a fair six-sided die. One, two, or three means sunshine; four, five, or six means rain. Three faces mean sunshine and three mean rain, so the chances are equal. You cannot change routes after rolling.

Taking the risky route might deliver two more lanterns than the reliable route, or two fewer. Average its two equally likely results: five plus one, divided by two, gives three. The reliable route also averages three, since it always delivers three.

This average, calculated using the chances of each result, is called the **expected value**. It does not mean a particular risky delivery will produce three lanterns. Nor does it promise that two deliveries will include exactly one sunny day and one rainy day. It gives us a consistent way to compare possible quantities.

## What Does the Average Include—and Leave Out?

The averages match, but the particular outcomes differ. The reliable route never delivers five and never leaves you with only one. The risky route keeps both possibilities open.

While waiting for the die, imagine Yu thinking, “I want to try for five,” and Lin thinking, “I'd rather avoid ending with only one.” They are reading the same rules while attending to different results. Choosing the reliable route does not, by itself, show a failure to understand the average.

A designer has therefore not finished the comparison by writing “equal expected values.” They still need to explain the range of results, what a poor result costs, and what the delivered quantity is supposed to help accomplish.

## Change the Task and the Better Route Changes

Start a fresh comparison with all other rules unchanged. The task now says, “Deliver at least three lanterns to finish.” We are comparing whether the task is completed. Delivering more earns no additional reward.

The reliable route always delivers three, so it always meets the requirement. The risky route succeeds with five in sunshine but fails with one in rain. It has a one-half chance of completion. For someone concerned only with completing this task, the reliable route is more dependable.

Now consider a separate task: “Deliver at least five lanterns to finish.” Three is never enough, so the reliable route cannot succeed. The risky route can deliver five in sunshine and still has a one-half chance. The same wish to complete the task now gives a reason to take the risky route.

These are separate comparisons, not two rewards added to one delivery. The route quantities and weather chances remain unchanged. What changes is which result meets the requirement. An average of three cannot tell us what this particular task needs.

Suppose a designer wants both routes to offer a reason connected to completing the three-lantern task. “Equal averages” will not achieve that: the risky route’s two extra lanterns have no further use, and sunshine still counts as just one completion. Extra lanterns could have another use, or route fees could change, but either would introduce a new rule requiring a new comparison. Finding which outcomes receive a reward helps determine whether to change the yield, the cost, or the task’s threshold.

## One Bad Outcome Does Not Explain the Whole Decision

Suppose the task requires five lanterns. You choose the risky route, roll rain, and deliver only one. You did not complete the task. Yet that does not show you should have chosen the reliable route: it had no chance of meeting this requirement.

Likewise, one successful risky delivery does not establish that you should always take risks. If the next task requires only three, or introduces a large cost for losing a lantern, the reasons change. Before judging, recover the goal and the conditions known at the time. Do not let a single later roll answer in their place.

We do not need to turn every game into arithmetic. An average helps answer a question about average quantity. A completion probability helps answer how likely a particular threshold is to be reached. Understanding surprise, disappointment, or willingness to try again also requires hearing how players describe that moment. Knowing what each question compares keeps one precise-looking number from answering all of them.
