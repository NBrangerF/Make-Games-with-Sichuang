# “The ending drags.” Which rule should change?

Suppose a playtester says, “I was almost there, but I just kept waiting.” This is worth understanding. It does not yet tell us whether to remove snakes, add a die, or replace the exact-finish rule. A comment describes an experience; a revision needs a more specific account of its cause.

Put the comment beside the finish of base version B: one die, exactly 24 to win, no movement on an overshoot. Use this hypothetical situation to work from a complaint towards one rule you could change.

## Recover the situation behind the complaint

At 23, only a 1 reaches the finish. Under a fair-die assumption, success on each own turn has probability 1/6; every other result leaves the pawn still. Temporarily ignoring an opponent ending the game first, the expected wait for that 1 is six own turns. This is neither average game length nor a guarantee of success on the sixth attempt.

“Too much waiting” might instead occur while an opponent considers dice, or during repeated consultation of an unclear rule. One concerns turn structure; the other concerns communication. Identify the position, events, and version before trying to repair a reading problem with a new finish condition.

## Match proposals to different causes

To reduce stationary overshoots, try a separate change to B: reaching or passing 24 wins. From 23, any result then finishes the player’s race. Rules reasoning establishes that consequence. It also removes the final demand for an exact result; whether that suits the intended experience remains open.

Another proposal changes B to C, allowing a choice between two dice. At 23, at least one 1 offers a winning selection. With fair independent dice, that probability is 11/36, still not certainty. C adds comparison tasks away from the finish too. A possible improvement to the ending should not hide the extra operations elsewhere.

## Start with one change you can follow

Changing the finish, adding die selection, and removing snakes together makes a smoother next game difficult to interpret. Keep the board and compare exact finishing with reaching or passing in B; alternatively, keep the exact finish and compare B with C. These are different questions and deserve separate records.

With limited time, make the proposal closest to the problem first. Write the other one beside it, naming the wait it addresses. You can keep the idea without making every draft now or having to reconstruct it later.

## Is this still the game you wanted to make?

If the invitation was “a small understandable decision for each player,” changing only the finish may address waiting without serving that intention. In a relaxed setting centered on watching chance unfold together, die selection may not be worth adding. Return to the specific invitation from chapter 4 instead of treating more rules as progress.

Leave a reproducible note beside the draft: “Start from B and change only the finish to reaching or passing. Next time, watch how long the ending takes and whether people still anticipate the final roll.” Leave the results blank. When friends sit down again, you will know which games to compare.
