# Why Can the Same Crossing Feel Different?

We have counted planks and actions and compared the players’ opportunities. Now imagine Yu in the first round. Her Hedgehog stays at the start while Rabbit moves one step ahead. Looking at positions, Hedgehog is behind. Looking at the shared planks, its collect action has made a successful crossing possible. The same event can direct attention toward different things.

Theme and feelings begin to connect through these concrete actions. They also depend on how somebody understands the game and what they are attending to now.

## Make the action fit the name

Calling a white cube Rabbit and a brown cube Hedgehog, then drawing a river, can help someone imagine movement as crossing water. Spending a plank to reach the next adjacent position can represent a temporary bridge. Moving that plank to the used area, where it does not return, fits the idea of using up material.

The names cannot silently add abilities. Both animals still move one step at a time. Rabbit cannot jump over a stone because real rabbits jump, and Hedgehog does not move more slowly because of its name. Pictures can help explain existing actions; what a player can actually do still needs to be clear.

If the introduction hopes to evoke helping each other, the rules need an identifiable way for help to occur. In the basic game, one person can stay at the start and collect two new planks that the other can later spend to move. Sharing the supply already provides an understandable helping action. A card need not announce that the players have earned friendship.

## What is the player comparing?

After Rabbit moves and Hedgehog collects in round one, five planks are available. If Yu attends only to the distance between the animals, she might feel behind. If she attends to getting both across, she might see her action as useful. These are possible interpretations, not feelings she must have.

A designer can make the second relationship easier to notice. State the goal as getting both animals across, and continue an example through the remaining three rounds so the reader sees how collecting supports the final move. If the picture instead gives the first-arriving Rabbit a huge crown, it may suggest an individual ranking absent from the rules.

A reference point can also come from a previous game. Suppose both players previously rushed away from the bank and ran short of planks. This time they discuss collecting. Comparing their current plan with their own earlier attempt can reveal an improvement. Whether they finished faster than another table is a different question. With no timed ranking, improving their plan and outperforming other people are different results.

## Story needs some attention too

Suppose every collect action is followed by a passage about the animals’ wishes. Players busy counting the remaining rounds may hurry past it to find their next action. More story on the page does not establish that the story receives more attention during play.

Important meaning can instead appear where players already need to look. Show both animals’ goal on the far bank, identify the central planks as shared, and continue the example to reveal what staying to collect makes possible. Understanding the helping relationship then connects directly to the current decision.

A separate story passage could appear before play or after the ending, when people have time to read it. It can affect expectations or recollection. That is a different moment from what a player notices while choosing an action. Placing it at the end still cannot guarantee that everyone wants to read it.

## A rule cannot write somebody’s gratitude

Imagine Lin saying after a win, “Good thing you collected first.” Yu might feel that her contribution was recognized. In another imagined session, both simply enjoy confirming that their calculation worked, without speaking as the animals. They may understand the same cooperative goal and express it differently.

Making “praise your partner” a required action first creates a speaking requirement. It might encourage somebody to speak, or make somebody eager to finish reciting the line. Rules can arrange encounters, mutual needs, and opportunities for expression. They cannot choose people’s feelings for them.

Look again at Hedgehog staying on the bank. The supply grew; the position stayed still. How do the picture, name, and shared goal lead someone to describe that step? Hear the player’s account before revising what you call the action.
