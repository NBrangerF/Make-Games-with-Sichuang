# Who is racing, who is winning, and who tells the story?

Try this sequence in choice version C. From 0, roll two 3s, choose one, and climb to 11. On the next turn, two more 3s land you at 14 and send you down to 4. Two 4s then let you reach the ladder at 8 and climb to 17. Doubles always give one move. Tell a friend about this arranged sequence and you might say, “I climbed, fell, then climbed again.”

## A sequence of consequences can become a story

No rule scripted these three scenes or required a comeback story card. Changing positions, an objective at the finish, and a person's memory of events already provide material for a narrative.

The board lets you check the destinations in that account: 11, 4, 17. To say you caught another player, you would also need their position. To describe relief, you would need to hear how the player felt. A real game is where those details can be found.

Some turns form a dramatic sequence; others are quiet. A designer can arrange connections that produce reversals. Which passage people remember is something to hear from them after play.

## The leading racer need not be the winning player

Camel Up, Second Edition supplies a useful contrast. Printed page 3 of Lookout's 2024 reprint rules explains that its five racing camels belong to no player. Players bet on the race and win through money. A moving camel carries the camels stacked above it.

Construct a local position without spectator tiles or crazy camels: yellow sits above blue, and blue's die is revealed. Blue's movement also carries yellow. The person triggering the action, the piece moving, and the person who may benefit from an earlier judgment need not coincide.

C closely links those relationships: my pawn reaching exactly 24 first wins for me. Camel Up separates them. Racing design can locate decisions in observing, committing to a prediction, or influencing a shared event, rather than only moving a personal pawn.

## Who acts, and who benefits?

When studying a case, distinguish what happens, who has authority to decide, and who receives a benefit. Carrying another camel creates a linked movement consequence. Which player becomes closer to victory also depends on existing bets and money, not just whose hand touched the pyramid.

Take a sketch of a track and imagine that no one owns its pawns. Why would each player care about them? Write a goal beside each player’s name and draw a line to movements that could affect it. This is a sketch for a new idea; the choice version’s rules stay as they are.

Our paper race keeps one pawn per person so a beginning designer can follow actions and results. Camel Up shows another place to start when you want to explore participation: change how people relate to the pieces.

## Rules consequences are not evidence about the world

Our Snakes and Ladders variant can show how exact landing affects progress. It cannot establish that real-world success mostly depends on luck. Camel Up's stacking produces linked movement without explaining actual animal behavior. Each artificial world implements the relationships its rules select.

A model intended to address reality needs further investigation: what was omitted, which parameters merely make play manageable, and which relationships have outside evidence? Interpretations should remain within what the model supports.

Return to the three moves at the opening and write a short account of the race. Check each sentence: can you point to it on the board, would you need to ask a player, or does it make a claim about the world outside the game? The revised story can keep its interest while showing where its details come from.
