# How Can Someone Else Receive the Same Game You Intended?

A friend receives the new map but an old action reminder. The rules say “collect new planks,” while a label beside the pieces says “recycle.” Every file is present, yet the set does not tell them which procedure to follow. A handoff begins with materials that agree with one another.

We will call this crossing draft “Base game v0.3.” It remains a prototype that can change. Weather, shortcuts, and other ideas discussed in the course belong to separate comparisons. They have not automatically entered this version.

## Provide enough to begin and finish

This version needs two animal pieces, twenty paper planks, a round marker, a turn reminder, and the map and rules below. These can be written on paper without special software or purchased components.

**Base game v0.3.** The map has four adjacent spaces in order: starting bank, first stepping stone, second stepping stone, far bank. Lin controls Rabbit and Yu controls Hedgehog. Both animals begin on the starting bank. Put four planks in the central “Available planks” area, sixteen in “Spare pieces,” and none in “Spent planks.” Start the round marker on round 1 and the turn reminder pointing to Lin.

Play for up to four rounds. Each round gives Lin one turn followed by one turn for Yu. Players may discuss plans but make their own decisions. On a turn, choose one permitted action. If at least one available plank remains, spend one by putting it in the spent area and move your animal to the next adjacent space toward the far bank. If your animal is still on the starting bank, you may instead collect two new planks from the spare area and add them to the center without moving. Or wait, leaving animals and planks unchanged. Each action uses the turn; collecting cannot be followed by movement on that turn.

Animals cannot move backward or skip spaces. They may share a space without blocking each other. An animal on the far bank can only wait on later turns. Spent planks cannot be reused. Spare pieces become available only through collecting. There is no weather, shortcut, personal score, borrowing, or special ability.

After every action, if both animals have arrived, players win together immediately. Otherwise, pass from Lin to Yu. After Yu’s turn, if this was before round 4, advance the round marker and return to Lin. If both turns of round 4 finish without both animals arriving, players lose together. Four rounds allow at most eight collect actions, so sixteen spare pieces are sufficient.

These instructions and the materials list provide the complete game. Anyone joining a playtest should also know that they can ask for help, pause, or stop. Those choices need no in-game vote and do not affect whether their contribution is taken seriously.

## Explain changes so people know what to revisit

Suppose a collaborator has already seen v0.2. “I made it clearer” does not identify which materials to replace. For this course’s version example, we can be specific: v0.3 aligns the names of all three plank areas, tells players to collect two new planks from the spare area, and states that movement sends planks to a spent area from which they never return. Player count, the four starting planks, the one-plank move cost, and the four-round deadline remain unchanged.

The collaborator now knows to replace the map, rules, and action reminder together, rather than just a title page. Old materials can be retained for comparison but should be marked as old so they do not enter the next setup by accident. A later change to five rounds would change play and require enough spare pieces too; changing the number on the round track alone would be insufficient.

A short version note gives later discussion a clear object. “This part is still confusing” should refer to an identifiable set of instructions, rather than different versions in different people’s minds. Recording important changes and reasons also helps someone taking over understand which decisions might need reconsidering.

## Say what has been checked and what remains unknown

The base rules have been examined through step-by-step rule analysis. There are successful plans, and some plans can be shown to fail. For example, if both animals move in round 1, the two remaining planks cannot pay for the four remaining moves. This kind of check supports claims about quantities and possibilities under the rules.

The player questions and playtest records in this course are invented examples. We have no results from real players using this materials version. It cannot yet be described as understood by beginners, enjoyed by both players, or ready for publication. A game that works in a paper calculation still needs different evidence about whether unfamiliar people can use it and want to play again.

A collaborator can help examine a first reading or suggest a new direction. Taking over a design may involve more than polishing sentences. If the shared crossing lacks worthwhile plans to compare, the rules can change too. Each time, we should know what is changing and whether earlier conclusions still apply.

Put the board, rules, and revision note together for the next player. You have a draft you can keep working on. A pause or question during their setup may give the next revision its direction.
