# What Am I Waiting For on Someone Else’s Turn?

Put aside the island route from the previous chapter. We are back to the base game: two stepping stones, one plank per move, Lin acting before Yu each round. The map tells us where movement is allowed. Turn order tells us who first changes the positions and the shared supply.

## What does the second player get to see?

In round one, Lin moves Rabbit forward, reducing the supply from four planks to three. When Yu’s turn begins, Rabbit is already on the first stone. Yu can decide what Hedgehog should do in response to that result. Moving would leave both animals away from the only place where collecting is allowed. Collecting would bring the supply up to five.

The players can discuss their plans before Lin acts. Even so, “I intend to move” differs from “Rabbit has moved.” The intention can still change. The completed move has already used an action and a plank. The second player continues from something that has happened.

Lin also needs to think beyond Rabbit’s own step. A plan to move may depend on Yu being willing to collect afterward. With a shared goal and shared planks, one person’s plan can depend on the other’s opportunity to act. Yu may therefore be following Rabbit closely before moving a piece.

## What if they choose who goes first?

Change just one rule: at the start of each round, the players agree who acts first. Each still takes one action. Everything else stays as in the base game.

They could have Yu collect first in round one, increasing the supply from four to six. Lin then moves, leaving five planks. The round ends in exactly the same position as when Lin moves before Yu collects: Rabbit on the first stone, Hedgehog at the bank, and five shared planks.

The result matches, but the order of the decisions differs. This time Lin sees the extra planks before moving. In the original arrangement, Lin spends a plank before Yu follows through. Choosing the first player makes “Who carries out our plan first?” another matter for discussion.

Choosing the order cannot repair every position. If both animals move in rounds one and two, both end round two on the second stone with no planks. Both have left the starting bank where collecting is possible. Even if they choose who goes first in round three, neither can add planks or move. Order helps through an action that changes the conditions for a later action. Reordering two moves that remain impossible does not repair the shortage.

That also adds a conversation. Reconsidering the order every round could interrupt an otherwise straightforward rhythm. We already know a successful plan using the fixed order. To understand the value of a flexible order, we need to identify a difficulty it addresses, rather than treating an extra choice as an automatic improvement.

## Choosing together is not finishing together

Set that variation aside and consider a separate one. The players may discuss, then secretly select one action each and reveal together. After the reveal, Lin’s action happens first and Yu’s second. A move that cannot be paid for becomes a wait, uses that player’s opportunity for the round, and cannot be replaced with another choice. Selecting an action and carrying it out are now separate steps.

Look at one round with Rabbit on the second stone, Hedgehog on the first, and only one shared plank. Both players choose to move. Under the procedure just described, Rabbit spends the plank and reaches the far bank. Hedgehog then has no plank and stays where it is. This does not predict that cooperating players will choose this combination. It explains what the rules must do if they do.

Allowing another discussion after a clash would add a decision. Giving Yu priority instead would move Hedgehog and leave Rabbit in place. The selected actions are the same, but the order in which they happen changes the result. We are comparing this round only, not claiming that these procedures rescue the whole position.

Writing “Everyone chooses together” therefore leaves something unfinished. Players need to know when they choose, when choices become visible, and how actions happen—especially when there is not enough of a shared item for every plan.

## Waiting can belong to the plan

Return to the successful route in the base game. In round three, Rabbit reaches the far bank first; Hedgehog then reaches the second stone. In round four, Lin can only wait. Yu spends the final plank, moves Hedgehog to the far bank, and both players win together.

Lin is not waiting because the rules are confusing or Hedgehog blocks the way. Rabbit has completed its journey, and the team needs one more step from its partner. Lin might look forward to that arrival. Or the ending may feel settled already, leaving Lin ready for another game. Seeing no piece move is not enough to tell us what the interval means to that player.

When designing turns, consider when players can act and what remains worth understanding or following when they cannot. Order helps us see who spends planks first and who adds them afterward.
