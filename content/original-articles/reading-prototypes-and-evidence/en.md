# What Do We Know When a Friend Stops to Ask?

The previous chapter set a specific question: can someone new to the crossing game distinguish spare, available, and spent planks? We will use an invented playtest scene to examine how a record can support a judgment. Here, v0.2 names a draft awaiting examination. The actions and dialogue are examples, not results of a test that has taken place.

This draft uses the base rules: four available planks and sixteen spare pieces at setup; one plank spent per move, never reused; and an animal on the starting bank may use a turn to collect two new planks. The draft includes complete rules, but we still need to examine how clearly its three areas communicate their different roles.

## Preserve a small sequence of events

Imagine that Lin moves Rabbit in round 1 and puts one plank in the spent area. Three planks remain available. It is Yu’s turn, and Hedgehog is still on the starting bank. She picks up the plank Lin just spent, points toward the center, and asks, “Does collecting mean taking back planks that have already been used?”

This account preserves positions, a quantity, an action, and a question. We know she is checking where collected planks come from. She has not yet completed a collect action. Picking up a piece alone does not establish which complete procedure she intends to follow.

“Yu did not read carefully” is a different kind of statement. It has already assigned a cause. She may have missed “new planks,” failed to connect the spare pieces with that phrase, or remembered another game in which resources return. Even if the complete rules contain the correct answer, the table layout could still direct attention elsewhere.

## What can the player’s explanation add?

The host could ask, “Where did you think the collected planks should come from?” This invites Yu to describe her understanding. “Are those piles too close together?” supplies a possible explanation before she answers.

Continue the imagined scene. Yu says, “I thought that pile beside us was the one we were supposed to take from again.” This supports a narrower judgment: at that moment, she treated the spent area as the source for collecting. It does not yet establish whether placement, wording, or previous experience led her there.

Actions and explanations can inform each other without revealing every thought. Her expectation that pieces return does not mean every new player will expect the same. One question cannot establish that the whole game is too difficult. Keeping the claim within these limits shows us what still needs investigation.

## Help changes what the next event can show

Suppose the host explains: “Collecting gives you two new planks from the spare area. Spent planks do not return.” Yu then takes two spare pieces and adds them to the center. The available supply rises from three to five, Hedgehog stays on the starting bank, and her turn ends.

We can now record that she completed this collect action after an explanation. We cannot count that correct action as proof that the original materials explained themselves. Helping has not ruined the entire session; it has changed which questions this part can answer.

Likewise, if the host says “Don’t take from that pile” before Yu reaches for it, we cannot see the question that might otherwise have emerged. For a first-understanding check, an early reminder can hide the very thing we want to examine. For a session about cooperation after learning the rules, explaining first may be appropriate. Alongside the version, keep track of words added during the session.

## Keep proposed fixes after the question

Prominent labels for the three areas, or an instruction saying “Take two from the spare area,” are possible changes. This record has not compared them or established that either works.

For now, a precise conclusion is: “In this imagined scene, Yu asked whether spent planks could be collected. After the source was explained, she collected correctly. Next we need to investigate which part of the materials leaves that source unclear.” This gives us a better next step than “The player doesn’t understand,” while keeping guesses separate from events.

A real collection of playtest notes will often contain more than one event. Someone may report too few planks, someone may want an extra round, and someone may have misunderstood one instruction. The next chapter brings several hypothetical records together to examine whether similar complaints have the same cause.
