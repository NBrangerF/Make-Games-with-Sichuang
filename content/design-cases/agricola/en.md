# Agricola: Why Is Another Person More Than Another Action?

A farm can contain rooms, fields, and animals. The design problem is how these things produce decisions. Why might taking more wood now postpone construction? Why can gaining a worker make the next harvest more demanding?

This article uses the two-player base rules of Agricola’s 2016 revised edition, separately from the 2007 edition, solo rules, and the variant without hand cards. [Lookout’s revised-edition page](https://www.lookout-spiele.de/en/games/agricolare.html) identifies the edition and designer Uwe Rosenberg. Our three situations are independently constructed local examples, not playtest reports or the book’s examples. Hand cards remain unplayed, with no special effects changing these resolutions. You can compare the situations without knowing the whole game first.

## Angle one: a growing pile still offers only one opportunity to take it

Placing a person on a shared action space both provides an action and makes that space unavailable for the rest of the round. Untaken goods on accumulation spaces remain when the next round adds more. The amount available therefore depends on when someone takes it. [Base rules, page 6](https://www.lookout-spiele.de/upload/en_agricolare.html_Rules_Agricola-RE_EN.pdf)

Suppose the work phase of round six is nearly over. Lin and Jo each have two family members, one already placed and one remaining. Lin places next, followed by Jo’s final person. Both farms have six wood, two reed, two wooden rooms, and an empty space orthogonally adjacent to a room. Forest and Farm Expansion are unoccupied; Forest contains six wood. Earlier actions have no further effects.

Compare these two specified sequences. Whoever expands builds only one wooden room, with no stable; whoever chooses Forest takes all six wood. The new room costs five wood and two reed. [Appendix, pages 6–7](https://lookout-spiele.de/upload/en_agricolare.html_Appendix_Agricola-RE_EN.pdf)

| Sequence | Lin at the end of the round | Jo at the end of the round |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lin takes Forest; Jo expands | 12 wood, 2 reed, 2 rooms | 1 wood, 0 reed, 3 rooms |
| Lin expands; Jo takes Forest | 1 wood, 0 reed, 3 rooms | 12 wood, 2 reed, 2 rooms |

Neither branch adds a person. Lin cannot take six wood and then build with the same person, or treat Jo’s occupied space as a queue still available to join. Both players subsequently return their people home. Round six has no harvest. During the next preparation, the emptied Forest receives three wood. In a different situation where nobody took the six wood all round, the next addition would instead bring it to nine.

There is no changing posted price for wood here. Rather, how much one placement brings back competes with what one placement can construct. More wood does not mean more developed farm space, and a new room does not arrive with a new person. Jo’s actions are specified choices for the comparison, not predictions of a real opponent.

When designing a shared action board, record the benefit, the duration of occupation, and the benefit’s later use separately. Calling a space “take resources” can conceal how its first visitor changes someone else’s opportunities. Renaming wood for a different setting will not by itself change that relationship.

## Angle two: additional labor starts paying off after its first feeding cost

Set up a separate two-player situation late in round seven. Lin has two people, three wooden rooms built legally earlier, and five food. Lin has one person left to place; Jo has finished all placements. Basic Wish for Children and Day Laborer are both unoccupied. Lin has no crops, animals, or other food-providing effects. Compare through this harvest, then count the people available next round.

If Lin chooses family growth, a third person is placed beside the person taking the action; Lin declines the optional minor improvement. The newborn takes no further action this round. After returning home, the two existing people require two food each and the newborn requires one: all five food are used. Next round Lin can place three people in turn.

If Lin chooses Day Laborer, two food are gained, taking the supply from five to seven. The family still has two people, requiring four food, so three remain. Next round there are still only two people to place. [Growth and feeding: rulebook pages 7 and 9](https://www.lookout-spiele.de/upload/en_agricolare.html_Rules_Agricola-RE_EN.pdf); [Day Laborer: appendix page 6](https://lookout-spiele.de/upload/en_agricolare.html_Appendix_Agricola-RE_EN.pdf)

| Choice | Does this placement add another action this round? | Food after harvest | People available next round |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Family growth | No | 0 | 3 |
| Day Laborer | No | 3 | 2 |

The benefit is an additional action next round, not consecutive turns or guaranteed access to a preferred space. At the following harvest, this person, now participating in work, ordinarily requires two food. If only the initial food changes from five to four, the growth branch is one food short at this harvest, taking one begging marker worth minus three points at the end. Having enough room and having enough food are different conditions.

This does not establish that growth should be avoided. It shows why an investment needs at least three points on a timeline: the action committed now, the first opportunity to use its output, and the first maintenance payment. Recording only “two people become three” misses both the two food Lin declined to gain and the additional feeding requirement. Looking only at the food left over misses the future choices.

## Angle three: the order of harvest decides whether two sheep can leave a third

Now compare the round-nine harvest of another two-player game. Lin has two existing family members and two sheep in one fully fenced pasture covering two farm spaces. It has no stable and a capacity of four. A Fireplace was legally built and paid for earlier. There are no crops, other animals, or relevant card effects. Only the starting food differs: state A has two, state B has four. Use the same policy in both: cook only the sheep needed to cover this feeding, then retain the animals.

There are no crops to collect in the field phase. Feeding follows, requiring four food for two people. In A, the Fireplace converts one sheep into two food; the resulting four food are all paid. In B, the existing four food are paid without cooking a sheep. The one-sheep-to-two-food conversion is visible on the [Fireplace illustration on rulebook page 10](https://www.lookout-spiele.de/upload/en_agricolare.html_Rules_Agricola-RE_EN.pdf).

Only then do animals breed. A has just one sheep left, so cannot breed: it finishes with one sheep and no food. B retains two and has room for their offspring, gaining one sheep: it finishes with three sheep and no food. Neither receives a begging marker.

Two additional food did not directly buy two sheep. They preserved one existing animal and maintained the pair needed for breeding. Conversely, A cannot borrow an anticipated newborn to feed the family first. Breeding happens after feeding, and cooking parents or offspring during the breeding phase is expressly prohibited. [Harvest sequence, rulebook page 9](https://www.lookout-spiele.de/upload/en_agricolare.html_Rules_Agricola-RE_EN.pdf)

Rooms, food, and sheep make it easy to bring farm expectations to the table. Resolution still depends on the objects, capacities, and timing this game specifies. The people are being fed here; do not invent a daily fodder cost for sheep. More sheep also need not immediately mean more endgame points: without additional scoring cards, one sheep and three sheep both score one point in the sheep category. We compared growth conditions and retained resources, not whole-game winners.

## A development account: why not one birth for every pair?

The appendix’s development credits say Hagen Dorgathen identified runaway growth from breeding per pair or per pasture during early development. They also describe using card-play records to revise overly strong and weak cards. This is the rulebook’s development retrospective, not our balance experiment; the credits attribute the current rulebook’s writing to Rosenberg. [Appendix credits, pages 10–11](https://lookout-spiele.de/upload/en_agricolare.html_Appendix_Agricola-RE_EN.pdf)

The current rules cap each animal type at one offspring per harvest. Maintaining the condition for reproduction is therefore different from multiplying the production rate without limit. In your own growth system, you could vary the number of producers, output per event, and frequency separately, then record successive results. The credits supply neither a complete earlier ruleset nor comparative testing data, so that proposed experiment should not be presented as the designer’s documented method.

## Check your understanding

1. After taking the six wood from Forest, can Lin immediately build using the same person? **No. That placement resolves Forest. Another action requires a person and an available space.**
2. Does the third person gained in round seven act again that round? **No. The action benefit starts next round, while this round’s harvest already requires one food for the newborn.**
3. Why can two more food before harvest leave two more sheep afterward? **They preserve one parent and thus keep the two-sheep breeding condition satisfied; the subsequent breeding supplies the additional sheep.**
