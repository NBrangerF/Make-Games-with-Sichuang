# Dominion: why can one more free card still have a cost?

The shop offers a card that costs nothing and can give you money later. Should you take it? Looking only at its price, there seems to be no downside. In Dominion, however, a new card joins your own deck and will later be drawn alongside the old ones. You have added something useful, while changing the chances of seeing other things.

This case examines deck-building in the second edition of the base game: **how does buying one card change future turns?** The key is not simply having more cards. Players keep changing what they will draw later. We examine draw chances, the capacity to play what arrives, and the value of cards when the game ends.

## Angle one: where does a purchased card go first?

Each player starts with seven Coppers and three Estates. A normal hand has five cards, and a turn provides one Action and one Buy; card effects can increase those allowances. A Copper produces one coin when played, and a Silver produces two, but Copper costs zero and Silver costs three. The price of acquiring a card is different from what it produces when you draw it later.

Purchased cards normally go to your discard pile, not straight into your hand. During Clean-up, you discard played cards and the rest of your hand, then draw five. When you need to draw and the deck is too small, you shuffle the discards and put them beneath what remains of the deck. Spending money does not remove the Coppers: they still belong to you and can return later. Unspent money, however, does not carry into your next turn. [English second-edition rules, pages 3–5](https://www.riograndegames.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/Dominion2E.pdf)

New cards therefore have a delay before use. Their first appearance depends on how much of the old deck remains, when the next shuffle happens, and whether any effects draw extra cards.

## A constructed opening: eleven cards or twelve?

Suppose your first hand is four Coppers and one Estate, with Silver and Copper available in the Supply. No other player's cards change your hand or deck between these two turns. On turn one, you play four Coppers for four coins and spend three to buy a Silver. At Clean-up, exactly five cards remain in your original deck, so your second hand must be the remaining three Coppers and two Estates. The Silver you bought is still in the discard pile; it does not join those cards early.

On turn two, you play your three Coppers. Compare two legal choices: use this turn's Buy to take a free Copper, or skip buying. Three coins could also buy another Silver. Set that option aside for this comparison so we can isolate what adding a Copper changes.

If you buy nothing, Clean-up brings all eleven cards together for a shuffle: seven Coppers, three Estates, and one Silver. Buying Copper instead makes twelve cards: eight Coppers, three Estates, and one Silver. In either branch, you draw five from the entire newly shuffled deck for your next hand.

With a properly random shuffle, the single Silver's chance of appearing in that five-card hand is **5/11** in the first branch and **5/12** in the second. The new Copper cost no coins, but it added a card competing with Silver for a place in your hand.

The Buy is another resource. You have only one this turn. Buying Copper uses it, so you cannot also spend the remaining three coins on a Silver. “Free” describes the coin price. It does not waive the buying opportunity or the consequences of adding a card to your deck.

## Angle two: can you play what you draw?

That calculation only concerns drawing the one Silver. It does not prove that the next hand will have less money. The extra Copper itself produces a coin, and some hands may now reach the price of a desired card. Adding a strong card can justify a larger deck. An unsuitable card may instead make it harder to find an important one in time.

Drawing more Action cards does not necessarily let you use all of them either. By default, you can play one. For example, each Smithy simply draws three cards. With two Smithies in hand and no other effects allowing further plays, you can still play only one of them this turn. To understand deck growth, ask both whether you can draw a card and whether you can use it after drawing it.

Compare a complete Action phase from another constructed state. Both branches start with Village, two Smithies, and two Coppers in hand; one Action; an empty discard pile; and seven Coppers left to draw. We stipulate that deck order for analysis, without allowing an actual player to inspect it. Village draws one card and adds two Actions; Smithy draws three. Playing an Action card spends an Action before its instructions resolve. [Second-edition rules, pages 3–6](https://www.riograndegames.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/Dominion2E.pdf)

| Order | New Coppers drawn | Actions remaining | Coppers available in the Buy phase |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Smithy first, then no further Action plays | 3 | 0 | Initial 2 plus 3: 5 |
| Village → Smithy → Smithy | 1 + 3 + 3 = 7 | 0 | Initial 2 plus 7: 9 |

After Village resolves in the second branch, two Actions remain. Each Smithy spends one, so both can be played. The first branch still holds Village and another Smithy but has no Action left to play either. Playing all Coppers then produces five coins in the first branch and nine in the second. Stop before buying; the example does not choose a purchase for you.

Drawing and play capacity must meet in the same turn to work together. Extra Actions without cards to use them do not generate a reward by themselves. Comparing only the number of cards drawn misses this bottleneck; neither does the example imply that buying more Villages is always helpful.

## Angle three: the ending changes the deck’s purpose

The ending changes the comparison as well. An Estate in your hand normally does not help buy cards, but each is worth one point at the end; each Province is worth six. In the base game, the game ends at the end of a turn if the Province pile is empty or at least three Supply piles are empty. A newly purchased Province counts toward your final score even if it never reaches your hand.

Whether to add a card therefore cannot be judged only by how smoothly the next hand will work. With plenty of time left, you may care more about what it can do repeatedly. Near the end, final points may matter more than completing another cycle. This is a reason the deck's purpose changes over time, rather than a universally best shopping list.

To transfer this mechanism, ask when an acquired item first becomes usable, what future space it will occupy, and whether an item with no monetary price still consumes another opportunity. Players' ability to alter what they will draw in the future is at the heart of this kind of deck growth.

For a concrete end check, suppose one Province remains and every other Supply pile has at least two cards. Your current cards total eight points and your opponent’s thirteen. You have eight coins, one Buy, and no other scoring effects. Buying the last Province brings you to fourteen points. Its empty pile ends the game after your turn, and you win fourteen to thirteen. Whether you ever draw that Province does not affect its six points.

If you instead buy a six-coin Gold, its pile remains nonempty, you still have eight points, and neither ending condition is met here. The deck can produce more money later, but the winner is undetermined. This contrasts future capacity with ending and scoring now; it is not a rule to buy Province whenever you have eight coins.

## The designer’s account: changing the opportunities a set provides

In his 2016 second-edition retrospective, Donald X. Vaccarino listed card replacements, clearer wording, and improved rulebooks among his reasons to revise. In a same-day reply, he described watching a set’s mix of extra Buys, extra Actions, attacks, and other interaction, with priorities that evolve rather than form a fixed recipe. [Designer’s opening post and reply, 3 October 2016](https://boardgamegeek.com/thread/1648227/the-secret-history-of-the-dominion-2nd-editions)

This suggests a test question of our own. If a Supply often leaves players holding Actions they cannot play, should we inspect the set’s connecting opportunities before reducing every card’s price? The Village example demonstrates one connection; it does not establish that this situation motivated Village’s design or revision. The account identifies a level at which the designer works. Diagnosing our own game still requires our own observations.

## Check your understanding

1. Does 5/11 being greater than 5/12 prove that skipping Copper always produces more money next hand? No: it only measures drawing the single Silver.
2. Why are there two Actions after Village, not three? The initial Action is spent playing Village before two are added.
3. Does the last Province lose its six points if it remains in the discard pile? No: final scoring includes all your cards; drawing it is not required.
