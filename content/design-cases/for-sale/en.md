# For Sale: Why Can Buying the Best House Leave You with Less?

You bid for the biggest number and later collect the biggest check, yet someone who passed earlier can finish richer. Three different results need separating: which house you acquire, where it ranks when sold, and how much money the exchange leaves you.

For Sale was designed by Stefan Dorra. This article uses IELLO’s 2020 English V2 base rules. Its product page dates this edition to July 10, 2020; that is not the game’s original publication year. [IELLO’s official product page](https://iello.fr/jeux/for-sale/)

All three situations below are our constructed examples, not playtest records or the designer’s account. Money is measured in thousands: cash of 5 means $5,000; House 5 is a ranking number. The design question is: **what changes when cash first buys a hand of cards, and those cards later earn money through relative rank?**

## Angle one: passing pays a cost and changes the next player’s fallback

In buying, a round offers one house per player. Players act clockwise, raising or passing. A passer takes the lowest remaining house and recovers half their bid, **rounding the recovered amount down**. The last bidder pays everything bid for the last house. [English rules, pages 4–6](https://iellogames.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/For-Sale_Rulebook_EN_V2.pdf)

A, B, and C sit clockwise in that order, with A starting the first round. Each has 28 cash and no houses or checks. Houses 4, 17, and 29 are public; future decks are hidden. A bids 1, B bids 3, C bids 5, and A raises to 6. A adds 5 to reach a total of 6, rather than adding another 6. B then passes, followed by C.

| Player | Final bid this round | Recovered / paid | House received | Cash left |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| B | 3 | 1 / 2 | 4 | 26 |
| C | 5 | 2 / 3 | 17 | 25 |
| A | 6 | 0 / 6 | 29 | 22 |

When B passes, the smallest house is 4. Only after B removes it does C’s fallback become 17. Passing does more than end someone’s participation: it improves the next remaining player’s fallback. A starts the next buying round. This example stops at the auction’s end. House 29 is not $29,000, and no selling income has arrived yet.

A paid 3 more than C for a higher house. Whether that difference pays off depends on later checks and the cards opponents commit. C did not leave for free either: passing after bidding 5 costs 3. Rounding the payment down instead of the refund would incorrectly make that cost 2.

For a design review, examine three consequences of withdrawal: who can still act, what the departing player receives, and what the remaining players face next. Everyone here receives one card. Competition changes their ranking strength and cash balances; failing to win the highest house does not mean receiving nothing.

Public bids also differ from cash balances, which may be hidden. Everyone can verify A’s bid of 6 without directly seeing the cash left behind it; this example states each starting 28 for calculation. In a prototype with fully public balances, observe how people assess an opponent’s ability to raise. Do not assume this change must remove suspense. [Rules, pages 3 and 5](https://iellogames.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/For-Sale_Rulebook_EN_V2.pdf)

## Angle two: a house number sets a rank within this batch, not a sale price

In selling, checks are public before everyone secretly selects a house. All selections are revealed together, with descending house ranks matched to descending checks. Sold houses leave play. [English rules, pages 8–9](https://iellogames.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/For-Sale_Rulebook_EN_V2.pdf)

Consider a separate three-player game with buying finished and two selling rounds left. The table gives readers the complete remaining hands, cash, and previous income for calculation. It does not make all those holdings permanently public at the table. Acquisitions were public, but this round’s commitments are revealed together.

| Player | Remaining houses | Cash | Checks already collected |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 6, 24 | 15 | 64 |
| B | 12, 27 | 11 | 63 |
| C | 18, 30 | 8 | 63 |

The current checks are 2, 10, and 15. A has committed 24. Compare two simultaneous reveals:

| Branch | Houses A / B / C | Income A / B / C |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One | 24 / 12 / 18 | 15 / 2 / 10 |
| Two | 24 / 27 / 30 | 2 / 10 / 15 |

The same 24 ranks first in one branch and third in the other, a difference of 13. It has no fixed sale price: the comparison uses the opponents’ actual commitments. Remembering that they acquired 27 and 30 does not establish that they will spend them now.

Finish both branches with the final checks fixed at 0, 7, and 14, revealed only next round. Remaining hands are 6 / 27 / 30 in branch one and 6 / 12 / 18 in branch two. With one card each, A / B / C receive 0 / 7 / 14 in both. Adding cash and all checks gives final totals of 94 / 83 / 95 and 81 / 91 / 100. C wins both, without a tie.

This is not advice based on A knowing the final batch. Fixing the continuation isolates what happens when opponents change cards against the same 24. Earning 13 more now does not mean leading the game by 13: earlier income, remaining cash, and consumed cards still matter. In a design with simultaneous choices, examine whether current rewards are visible, whether commitments can change, and whether another player changes your legal action or the return on that same action.

## Angle three: cash becomes a supply of cards that can each be used once

Now compare two paths from the final buying round to the end. After nine buying rounds, each player has 8 cash and these houses. The final houses offered are 3, 16, and 28, with A acting first.

Hands and cash are listed for readers’ calculations. The current houses and bids are public; future checks have not been shown, and nobody can wait to see opponents’ selling cards before choosing their own.

| Player | Nine houses already held |
| --- | --- |
| A | 1, 6, 10, 11, 15, 20, 21, 25, 30 |
| B | 2, 7, 8, 12, 17, 18, 22, 26, 27 |
| C | 4, 5, 9, 13, 14, 19, 23, 24, 29 |

Again, bids are A 1, B 3, C 5, A 6. B passes, recovering 1 and paying 2 for House 3, leaving 6 cash. C must now act. Compare two legal branches:

| C’s choice | A’s result | C’s result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pass | Pay all 6; receive 28; keep 2 cash | Recover 2, pay 3; receive 16; keep 5 cash |
| Raise to 7, then A passes | Recover 3, pay 3; receive 16; keep 5 cash | Pay all 7; receive 28; keep 1 cash |

By raising rather than passing, C pays 4 more to replace 16 with 28. Once A passes, there is no further bidding opportunity: C settles as the last player. Every house has now been distributed, ending buying.

To isolate those extra 4, construct the same continuation in both branches. Everyone saves this newly acquired house until the final sale. The previous nine houses are sold through an identical legal sequence of commitments and check batches, earning A, B, and C totals of 70, 70, and 69. Finally, checks 4, 12, and 13 appear. Each player has only the newly acquired card to reveal. This continuation is an analytical condition, not knowledge players had while bidding.

| Branch | Final sale income A / B / C | Final wealth A / B / C | Winner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C passed | 13 / 4 / 12 | 85 / 80 / 86 | C |
| C raised to 7 | 12 / 4 / 13 | 87 / 80 / 83 | A |

C’s higher card really earns 1 more, but costs 4 more, reducing C’s final wealth by 3. This calculation need not assume an opponent made a mistake: it examines specified actions without proving that passing is best across every continuation. A different gap between the final two highest checks, or using these houses in earlier batches, would change the comparison.

Cash and houses serve different purposes. Unspent cash remains part of final wealth; checks cannot reopen the completed buying phase. In this three-player base game, 30 houses give everyone ten purchases and ten sales. Paying more changes the strength of a commitment within that supply, rather than adding another sale. [Rules, pages 2–4, 6, 8, and 10](https://iellogames.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/For-Sale_Rulebook_EN_V2.pdf)

Our interpretation of this connection suggests four separate design checks: quantity, permitted uses, when something leaves the system, and final value. “Money becomes cards” names a conversion, but does not yet distinguish extra actions from stronger choices within the same number of actions.

Houses 1–30 are unique, so house ranks cannot tie. Check values can repeat, yielding equal payments at those ranks. Equal final wealth is broken by unspent cash, then shared victory. Neither completed example needs that tiebreaker. [Rules, pages 2 and 10](https://iellogames.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/For-Sale_Rulebook_EN_V2.pdf)

## Check your understanding

1. In example one, does C pay 2 or 3 after passing on a bid of 5? **3: the recovered amount is rounded down to 2. B already took 4, so C receives House 17.**
2. Does playing House 24 in example two guarantee the 15 check? **No. Against 27 and 30, it ranks third and receives 2.**
3. Why does C finish 3 poorer in example three despite paying 4 more for 28 and taking the biggest final check? **That specified continuation adds only 1 income: 1 − 4 = −3. A larger check alone does not prove a better whole-game return.**
