# Hanabi: Why Can Everyone See the Fireworks but Not Their Own Cards?

A teammate can see your cards but cannot simply announce every detail. The person who must choose a card is the person who cannot see it. Hanabi places “I know something, but you must act” at the center of cooperation.

The thematic question is: **how does a shared fireworks display become a situation players actually face through card visibility and communication limits?** This analysis uses Cocktail Games’ 2019 French base rules, without the expert variant.

## Angle one: a shared goal needs different people to complete the information

The official setting casts players as fireworks makers who have mixed up their materials. Five colored sequences advance from 1 to 5. Players hold their cards facing outward, showing others what they cannot see themselves. The person choosing an action and the people who know the card are separated. [Cocktail Games’ official description](https://www.cocktailgames.com/jeu/hanabi/)

A turn consists of a clue, discard, or play. A clue spends a blue token and identifies one color or number, indicating every matching card together. Discarding restores a spent clue token and is unavailable when all are already available. A card that cannot extend its color’s sequence is discarded and adds an error; the third error ends the game. [Official 2019 rules](https://www.cocktailgames.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Hanabi_regles_0519_BD.pdf)

Helping someone therefore requires a choice. Spending a turn improving their information means not playing your own card that turn. Discarding to recover a clue may remove material needed later. A shared goal does not remove individual decisions. It connects their consequences to one display.

## A constructed situation: combining two clues

This two-player situation is constructed for analysis, not taken from a playtest. The red firework has reached 2, blue has reached 1, and the other colors have not started. Cards remain in the deck, and two clue tokens are available.

Mia sees Leo’s five cards in order: red 3, blue 3, yellow 4, green 4, and white 5. Leo cannot see their faces. An earlier red clue told him that only the first card is red. Since that clue, his hand has not changed and he has kept the same positions. He does not yet know the first card’s number.

On her turn, Mia spends a clue token to indicate that the first and second cards are both 3s, leaving one token available. She must indicate both together. She cannot point only to the card she hopes Leo will play or add information by emphasizing one of them.

On Leo’s turn, he combines the clues: the first card is red and is a 3, so it fits after the red 2. The second is also a 3 but was excluded from red by the earlier complete clue. No other color can yet accept a 3. Leo has a clear reason to play the first card. Red advances to 3, and he draws a new card without seeing its face.

That progress did not come from Mia simply saying “Play the first card.” What she chose to communicate, what Leo remembered and combined, and what the public display could accept worked together to make an uncertain hand actionable. In this situation, knowing only the color or only the number would leave part of the answer missing.

## Angle two: a clue can return; the last essential card cannot

Consider another two-player situation, on Mia’s turn. Red has reached 2, green 1, and the other colors have not started. No blue clue tokens are available, no errors have occurred, and at least two cards remain in the deck. Earlier complete clues have established that Mia’s first card is red 3 and her second is green 1. The other red 3 is visible in the discard pile; no other required value has lost all its copies. Her remaining cards are not used here. Compare one discard and its replacement draw.

| Mia discards | Immediately recovered | What remains after drawing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First card, red 3 | One clue token | Both red 3s are discarded; red can never reach 3 |
| Second card, green 1 | One clue token | Red 3 remains in hand; the played green 1 is unchanged |

Both branches draw from the same nonempty deck, restore her hand to five, and leave errors at zero. The recovered clue is identical; the remaining possibilities are not. Each color has two 3s. Discards remain visible but cannot be recovered, while a firework must grow in sequence. Red is therefore capped at 2 in the first branch. Even if all four other colors reach 5, the total cannot exceed 22. This is an upper bound, not a prediction that this game will score 22. [Rules, pages 1 and 4–6](https://www.cocktailgames.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Hanabi_regles_0519_BD.pdf)

The second branch keeps the red-3 route open without guaranteeing 25 points: information, correct timing, and enough actions still matter. When a resource cycle exchanges a discarded object for another opportunity, check both what returns and which future path disappears permanently. A clue-token ledger alone misses the latter.

## Angle three: what does the component preserve, and what must people track?

We can read the growing colored sequences as a performance built together. Everyone sees the work advancing while lacking complete access to the materials in their own hands. Errors consume the team’s tolerance for mistakes, and clues use limited opportunities. “Wait, I need another piece of information” and “Now we can add this part” point toward the same shared result.

This is an analysis of the rules’ relationships, not a claim that real fireworks makers must work without seeing their materials or proof of the designer’s intentions. Even without fireworks illustrations, reasoning from colors and numbers would still function. The fireworks theme offers a coherent way to understand colored sequences, shared progress, and the risk of mistakes. Illustrations alone could not supply those relationships.

The earlier red-3 example keeps positions unchanged so both clues refer to the same physical card. Page 7 of the 2019 rules permits rearranging a hand to help remember information. A position can serve as a reminder, but its old properties do not pass to a replacement card. If red 3 is played and a new card goes into that position, neither “red” nor “3” automatically describes the newcomer.

When comparing hand-held cards with a rack in your own prototype, hold visible faces, clue contents, and play rules constant. Observe whether players keep referring to the same card. Having to hold cards up, other people’s ability to read them, and applying old clues after replacements are concrete operations to check. A rack alone does not establish better understanding.

## Which communication limits are we discussing?

This edition says that teammates may not freely comment or influence the current player’s turn. Its final page also expressly invites groups to set communication rules that suit them. It would therefore be inaccurate to say that the official rules absolutely prohibit every agreement. It would also be inaccurate to assume that every private signaling system automatically belongs to the same base rules.

When comparing experiences, identify which reminders or conventions a group allows. A group with practiced signals and a group reasoning only from literal colors and numbers already face different learning requirements. Outward-facing hands prevent someone from directly inspecting their own whole hand; they do not guarantee that an experienced player can never dominate discussion.

For your own cooperative design, ask what should make players depend on one another: information, physical actions, or resources? After restricting communication, do players have legal ways to fill in what they need? Can the theme explain why everyone cares about the result? If more reminders are allowed, does that reduce a reading or memory burden, or replace work that previously required a teammate? Those questions carry more of this design’s value than simply copying “no free talking.”

## The designer’s account: keep outward-facing hands, change the goal

In his account of Hanabi & Ikebana, Bauza describes two early games that were too similar. A matching experiment with outward-facing hands tested poorly, although people liked holding cards that way. Following his wife’s suggestion, he brought that element into a cooperative design aimed at completing colored sequences together. [Bauza, “Petite Histoire”](https://www.antoinebauza.fr/?p=734)

This separates a promising part from a whole that is not yet working. Outward-facing cards do not themselves establish cooperation. The same restriction can serve different goals; who wants to reveal information, who bears mistakes, and who receives points still need examination. The account concerns early development. Our worked situations follow the 2019 base rules.

## Check your understanding

1. If a number-3 clue identifies two cards, does it establish that both can be played? **No. In the first example, only the known red card fits; the second was excluded from red.**
2. Does discarding the last red 3 cost the same as discarding the duplicate green 1 because both recover one clue? **No. The former permanently blocks further red progress; the latter leaves the played green 1 intact.**
3. A new card replaces the played red 3 in its position. Do the old clues prove that it is also red 3? **No. The clues described the previous card; the replacement needs new identification.**
