# Carcassonne: why does finishing a city change your next turn?

You draw a tile that could close a gap in a city wall. Put it there and you score now; place it elsewhere and you could claim another city. On the map, these look like two alternative arrangements. Count the little people still beside you, and another decision appears: will you have anyone available to send out next?

Carcassonne connects tile placement, claiming features, scoring, and the return of your people. The highest final score wins. We will follow that connection through cities in the basic game, leaving fields, the River, and the Abbot aside.

## One tile changes three things

A turn starts by drawing a tile and joining it to the map with matching terrain along adjacent edges. You may then put one meeple on the tile just placed. Finally, features completed by that placement score. A city is complete when its walls close around it with no internal gaps. Each city tile scores two points, each coat of arms adds two, and its meeples return to their owners' supplies.

A meeple is not a marker that scores as soon as you place it. While it claims an unfinished city, it is also unavailable elsewhere. Each player has only seven meeples to deploy, so a tile placement affects the map, the timing of points, and how many other places you can claim later. [English basic rules, pages 2–5](https://cdn.svc.asmodee.net/production-zman/uploads/2024/09/carcassonne_v3_rulesheet_en-1.pdf)

That helps explain why a bigger city is not automatically a better choice. It may be worth more, but it may also need particular tile shapes to close its gaps. You have committed to something that still needs help from future tiles. Placing the meeple has not yet delivered the reward.

## A constructed situation: finish one job or start another?

Imagine this local position. You have one meeple left in your supply. Another occupies your unfinished, single-tile city, while your other five are on unfinished features elsewhere. The little city has no coats of arms and one open city edge.

Your new tile also has no coats of arms. One edge is city and the other three are fields. There are two legal places for it. You could join its city edge to the old city, completing a closed, two-tile city. Or you could connect a field edge elsewhere on the map, leaving the city edge open and starting a separate city. All adjacent edges match in both locations, and neither placement completes any other feature. The following are separate branches, not two consecutive moves.

If you finish the old city, you choose not to place a new meeple. You score two tiles at two points each: four points. The city's meeple returns, increasing your supply from one to two. The meeple-placement step has already passed, so you cannot deploy the returning piece again this turn, but it is available on a later turn of yours.

If you start the new city, you place your last available meeple in it. No city is complete, so you gain no immediate points and your supply drops to zero. The old and new cities each have an open edge. Suitable later tiles could complete both, but that requires future opportunities. It is not a reward you have already collected.

The second choice claims another potential source of points, while postponing four points and committing your last available person. If no meeple returns before your next turn, then even a tile offering an easily completed road cannot receive a new meeple from you. You still have to place the tile legally.

## Everyone builds the map; the tile placer does not automatically get the points

Another player can finish your city, and the meeples in it still determine who scores. Completing someone else's feature can return their people to them. The same tile might also help a different plan of your own. Each placement therefore affects when other players finish their commitments, as well as your own position.

The claiming rule also does not mean that a city can never contain more than one meeple. When placing a new meeple, the whole connected city must be unoccupied. However, two initially separate cities can later be joined. At scoring, the player with the most meeples receives the full value; tied leaders each receive the full value, rather than dividing it. Being nearby and already being connected are therefore materially different states.

## When should you leave a meeple committed?

If you have plenty of people available, or a large city is nearly complete, keeping a meeple committed may be more valuable than recovering it quickly. The comparison above explains the cost of a shortage. It does not prove that small cities always beat large ones.

The ending matters too. After the drawable tiles run out, an unfinished city still scores one point per tile and coat of arms; it does not become worthless. That is half its completed value, however. If the two single-tile cities never change, they yield just two points together, while finishing the two-tile city secured four. As the end approaches, expecting to complete something later needs increasingly concrete support.

To carry this relationship into your own game, ask when a committed unit returns, whether the builder also receives the reward, and what an unfinished project is worth at the end. Together, those rules make a map more than a place to put pieces. It records people still committed and plans whose rewards have yet to arrive.
