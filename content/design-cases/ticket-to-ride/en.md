# Ticket to Ride: Why Can a Complete Hand Still Leave You Without a Route?

A single space can be cheap to claim yet determine whether you spend several more turns taking a detour. A seven-point ticket also involves more than adding seven points: completing its last connection before the deadline removes a seven-point penalty at the same time.

Alan R. Moon designed *Ticket to Ride*. This article uses the original USA base game and the publisher’s **2015 English reprint rules**: 30 Destination Tickets, with three dealt initially and at least two retained. The official site now provides both old and new rules; the 2025 V2 changes components and the initial ticket deal. We do not combine that revision, Europe, USA 1910, or anniversary rules with this case. [2015 rules, p. 2](https://ncdn0.daysofwonder.com/tickettoride/en/img/tt_rules_2015_en.pdf); [official rule downloads](https://www.daysofwonder.com/game/ticket-to-ride/).

The three independent two-player situations below are our constructions, not playtest reports or solutions supplied by the designer. The map is public. Hands and tickets follow the game’s information rules; showing an opponent’s cards or a hidden deck order for calculation does not give that information to players. We examine three distinct constraints: **whether space remains, when resources arrive, and how many actions remain after they arrive.**

## Angle one: The same map has different capacity at different player counts

Dallas and Houston have two parallel one-space gray routes. In a two- or three-player game, claiming one closes the other. With four or five players, different players may claim the two routes; one player can never claim both. [Rules, p. 3, Claiming Routes](https://ncdn0.daysofwonder.com/tickettoride/en/img/tt_rules_2015_en.pdf).

Suppose the game has just begun, with Lin playing first and Jo next. Both have 45 trains, no routes, and zero route points. Lin’s four starting cards are two red and two blue; Jo has two green, one yellow, and one white. We compare two ways Lin could pursue a connection between Dallas and Houston. We are not inventing a Destination Ticket bearing those cities.

Lin can immediately spend one red to claim a gray route: one train, one point, three cards left, and this connection made. If Lin draws cards instead and Jo then spends one yellow on either parallel route, Lin cannot use the other one next turn, even with a larger hand.

The direct option has closed; the cities have not disappeared. If the following routes remain available, Lin can collect the required cards and build this particular detour:

| Route | Trains and cards needed | Points from this claim |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dallas → Little Rock | 2; a set of any one color | 2 |
| Little Rock → New Orleans | 3; a green set | 4 |
| New Orleans → Houston | 2; a set of any one color | 2 |

The detour uses seven trains and at least three of Lin’s claim turns, scoring eight points. The direct route uses one train and one claim turn for one point. Two blue, three green, and two red could pay for the three listed routes, but Lin’s starting four cards are insufficient; collecting the missing cards requires additional turns. We have not established that this is the shortest or best detour in every position, or assumed Jo will always leave its routes open. [Publisher’s map, pp. 2–3; scoring table, p. 4](https://ncdn0.daysofwonder.com/tickettoride/en/img/tt_rules_2015_en.pdf).

Two accounts matter. Those six extra spaces do produce seven more route points, but they also consume six more trains and at least two more claim actions, potentially changing how later tickets connect. Extra points do not establish that the detour has no cost. Extra distance does not establish that it is worse for every plan.

For a map designer, the number of lines printed and the number of players allowed to use them are separate decisions. In your own route prototype, hold cities, lengths, and objectives fixed while changing when parallel routes open. Record whether players contest the same connection, claim it earlier, or switch to another path. This is our proposed experiment, not a claim about Moon’s historical testing method.

## Angle two: A wildcard’s price includes how and when you acquire it

Consider the six-space black route from Los Angeles to El Paso. In a separate two-player game, each player has taken one draw turn and the map is still empty. Lin has five black and one green card; Jo has six black. A legal prefix is four black cards dealt to each player, followed by Lin blindly drawing black and green, then Jo blindly drawing black and black. This stays within the twelve-card supply of each regular color.

Lin now acts. The five face-up cards are a locomotive, red, blue, yellow, and white. To compare branches, fix the hidden top two cards as locomotive, then black. Lin does not know that order, so it cannot be treated as the basis of a recommendation.

| Lin’s draw sequence | Cards actually gained | Hand afterward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Take the face-up locomotive first | Only that card; no second draw | Five black, one green, one locomotive: 7 cards |
| Take face-up red; a locomotive replaces it; draw the second card blindly | The second card is black; neither face-up locomotive may be taken | Six black, one green, one red: 8 cards |
| Blindly draw a locomotive first; then take face-up red | Black replaces red afterward | Five black, one green, one locomotive, one red: 8 cards |

All three hands can form a six-card black payment; the first and third use a locomotive for one black. The acquisition process differs: **taking a visible locomotive consumes the whole draw action; blindly drawing one counts as just one card**. The newly revealed locomotive in branch two is also unavailable as the second card. [Rules, p. 3, Train Car Cards](https://ncdn0.daysofwonder.com/tickettoride/en/img/tt_rules_2015_en.pdf).

Branch three gaining more cards does not mean blind drawing always earns an extra card. Its result depends on the fixed hidden order. A white card on top would change the outcome of the same decision. Branch one guarantees a locomotive before the choice is made. When designing resource acquisition, track certainty, flexibility, and quantity separately rather than assigning every card a fixed price.

Time is a fourth consideration. Lin chose to draw and cannot also claim a route in that turn. Fix Jo’s next action as spending six black on Los Angeles–El Paso: Jo has 39 trains left and immediately scores 15 points. In every branch, Lin’s next turn arrives too late to claim that occupied route. The collected cards remain available for other uses, but they have not reserved space on the map.

A designer can check whether acquiring resources and committing an action occur together. Separating them makes the intervening opponent turn consequential. Public hands would make the threat easier to assess, but would not automatically remove that interval. Information rules and action timing therefore need separate consideration.

## Angle three: A final turn turns a seven-point ticket into a fourteen-point swing

The third independent game is nearing its end. Lin has claimed only the two-space green Chicago–Saint Louis route and the three-space green Little Rock–New Orleans route. That is five trains used, six route points, and 40 trains remaining. Lin holds the real **Chicago–New Orleans ticket worth seven points**. The two separate pieces of network lack the still-open two-space gray Saint Louis–Little Rock route.

Jo’s low train supply also has a concrete history. Jo has connected the entire western path below; numbers show trains used between consecutive cities. Only the specified lane of any parallel pair is used.

| Jo’s continuous path | Consecutive route lengths |
| --- | --- |
| Vancouver → Calgary → Winnipeg → Helena → Seattle → Portland → Salt Lake City | 3, 6, 4, 6, 1, 6 |
| Salt Lake City → San Francisco (orange) → Los Angeles (yellow) → Phoenix → El Paso → Santa Fe → Denver | 5, 3, 3, 3, 2, 2 |

That totals 44 trains. Jo has just claimed the two-space gray Santa Fe–Denver route, reducing the stock from three to one and triggering the ending at the end of Jo’s turn. **Lin now gets a final turn, then Jo gets a final turn, then scoring begins.** The triggering player is included. The game neither ends immediately nor grants everyone else two more turns. [Map, pp. 2–3; Game End, p. 4](https://ncdn0.daysofwonder.com/tickettoride/en/img/tt_rules_2015_en.pdf).

Lin has two blue cards to pay for the gap. A complete reachable prefix also leaves seven green, twelve purple, eleven locomotives, six white, seven orange, eight red, and one yellow: 54 cards altogether. This edition has no hand limit. The large hand establishes that missing cards are not the problem; it is not advice to hoard. These routes and hands can be reached from normal dealing through 32 Jo turns and 31 Lin turns in alternation, respecting twelve cards of each regular color and fourteen locomotives.

Compare just two specified choices, fixing Jo’s last turn as taking one visible locomotive and claiming no further route:

| Lin’s final action | Lin’s route points | Chicago–New Orleans ticket | Subtotal of these two components |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spend two blue and claim Saint Louis–Little Rock | 6 + 2 = 8 | Connected: +7 | 15 |
| Draw train cards; the map stays unchanged | 6 | Unconnected: −7 | −1 |

The difference is 16: two points from the short route and fourteen from changing that ticket’s outcome from minus seven to plus seven. We are not relabeling the ticket as having a fourteen-point face value; we are comparing **failure with completion**. Lin’s other tickets are excluded from the table, so these are not complete final scores and do not establish a winner. Jo’s continuous 44 trains exceed Lin’s maximum of seven, so Jo receives the longest-path bonus in both branches.

This does not show that every short route is more important than a long one. If another path already completed the ticket, those two spaces would not change its scoring again. If two separate routes were still missing, even two cheap claims could not both fit into one remaining action.

The prototype lesson is that inventory does not equal opportunity. Lin still has 40 trains and enough cards, but “draw now, build next time” cannot extend beyond the ending. Write an ending condition as an actual sequence, then play through the triggering player’s action, everyone’s remaining actions, and scoring. Check whether that sequence provides the recovery window you intended.

## Follow the designer’s actual development process

In a retrospective co-written with Mark Kaufmann, Moon describes outlining rules and a map, testing alone that day, testing with friends several days later, and adjusting routes, rules, and tickets over subsequent weeks. Kaufmann separately describes publication work on the period setting and title. These are participants’ recollections; they do not establish the specific intention behind each route. [*The History of Ticket To Ride*, printed pp. 6–7](https://d1vzi28wh99zvq.cloudfront.net/pdf_previews/147192-sample.pdf).

Our practical proposal is to record each map change alongside the objectives it makes easier or harder. Separately record how naming, images, and historical setting affect understanding. Mechanical and presentation changes can support each other, but require different evidence. Testers wanting another play is useful feedback; it still does not establish balance for every route, ticket, or player count.

## Check your understanding

1. In a two-player game, Jo claims one Dallas–Houston gray route. Can Lin claim the other? **No. Only with four or five players can different players use both lanes.**
2. Lin takes red, revealing a locomotive in its place. Can that become the second card? **No. A blindly drawn locomotive, however, still counts as one card.**
3. Why does assembling six cards for a black payment not reserve Los Angeles–El Paso? **Drawing and claiming are separate turns; Jo may claim first.**
4. Why does the last example’s two-point route change the tracked subtotal by 16? **Two points come from the route; fourteen come from moving the ticket from −7 to +7.**
5. Does Jo ending a turn with three trains trigger the ending? **No. The threshold is zero, one, or two, and the triggering player also receives one final turn.**
