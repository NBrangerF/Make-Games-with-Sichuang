# Pandemic: clear the cubes first, or bring the cards together?

A city has three disease cubes, and you want to remove them immediately. A teammate says, “Take my card first.” In Pandemic, that may help rather than delay the rescue: it can allow a later treatment action to accomplish more.

The useful design question is: **how can one person's action change what others can do next?** Winning or losing together is only the beginning. Cards, locations, action limits, and cures provide the connections between players. This case concerns the ordinary base game.

## Removing the immediate problem is not the same as reaching the goal

The team wins by discovering cures for all four diseases. An empty board does not mean that those cures have been found. Conversely, discovering the fourth cure wins immediately, even with cubes remaining. Too many cubes can lead to outbreaks; being unable to place required cubes or draw the required Player cards can also cause a loss.

Treating the immediate situation and advancing the shared goal therefore compete for actions. A player normally has up to four actions per turn. Treatment removes one cube of a color from the current city, but once that color is cured, a treatment removes all its cubes there. Discovering a cure normally requires discarding five City cards of one color at a research station. Roles modify some of these rules. [English basic rules, pages 3–4 and 6–8](https://cdn.svc.asmodee.net/production-zman/uploads/2024/09/Pandemic_Rulebook.pdf)

One person does not have to draw all five cards. However, cards cannot simply be passed across the world. Ordinary Share Knowledge requires both players to be in the same city, and the transferred card must match that city. Both must agree. Location therefore determines not only where you can help, but whether cards distributed among the team can become one usable set.

## A constructed situation: the same result, one action apart

Imagine that it is Lan's turn. She is the Contingency Planner and still has all four actions. She and her teammate, the Dispatcher, are in Chicago. It already has a research station and three blue cubes. Blue has no cure, blue cubes remain in other cities, and red is also uncured. Lan holds exactly four different blue City cards, none of them Chicago. Her teammate holds Chicago and agrees to give it to her. This example uses no Event cards or special role actions.

These conditions are constructed to compare action order. We follow only Lan's action phase, stopping before she draws Player cards, without assuming the outcome of later infections.

First, follow the impulse to remove a cube immediately. Action one treats Chicago, reducing its three blue cubes to two. Action two shares knowledge: Lan takes Chicago and now has five blue cards. Action three discards those five at the station to discover the blue cure. Action four treats Chicago again, removing its remaining two blue cubes together. All four actions are used, and Lan is still in Chicago.

Restart from the same position and change the order. First take Chicago; second discard the five cards to discover the cure; third treat the disease. This time all three blue cubes leave in one treatment. Lan has one action left, which she can use to drive along the connection to Atlanta.

Both branches spend the same five cards, discover the blue cure, and remove Chicago's three cubes. The second branch delays the first treatment until after the cure takes effect, leaving an action for movement. The teammate's card changes treatment efficiency. The benefit of research extends beyond the action that performs it.

## Helping also gives up another use

Giving Chicago away has a cost. The teammate can no longer discard it later for a direct flight to Chicago. Lan also has to reach a location where the transfer and research are possible. The players cannot stay in separate corners and simply announce that they have five cards between them. Cooperation has specific, inspectable content here: who gives up what, who spends actions, and which later action becomes possible.

This sequence does not imply that research should always come first. If the players have not met, travel may consume the whole turn. Without a research station, that location requirement still needs solving. The Medic can remove all cubes of an uncured color with one treatment, while the Scientist needs only four matching cards for a cure. Those roles change the comparison too.

Cured blue disease can still add cubes. It is eradicated only when it has a cure and its last cube has left the board. Epidemics also shuffle the Infection discard pile back onto the top of the Infection deck, putting recently infected cities near danger again. Immediate treatment remains valuable; the team cannot simply ignore the board while collecting cards.

The rules encourage discussion, while leaving decisions for the turn with the active player. A shared interest gives people a reason to talk, but does not automatically give everyone room to contribute. When designing cooperation, ask what concrete cost helping another player involves, whether its effect is visible, and whether different players can offer plans or one person ends up arranging everyone's actions. This case shows how rules connect contributions, while leaving shared goals and shared participation as distinct things to examine.
